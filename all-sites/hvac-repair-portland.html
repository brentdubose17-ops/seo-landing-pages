Licensed HVAC Repair & Heat Pump Service in Portland, Oregon

Portland runs on heat pumps, not air conditioners. With roughly 150 rainy days a year, mild 35°F winter nights and a growing number of 100°F summer afternoons, the equipment that keeps a Portland home comfortable is different from what works in Phoenix or Minneapolis. Our licensed Multnomah County technicians repair and replace heat pumps, gas furnaces and ductless mini-splits across the metro.

Why Portland's Climate Makes Heat Pumps the Default Choice

Portland's marine West Coast climate is unusually mild by American standards: winter lows average in the mid-30s, summer highs usually top out near 80°F, and the city gets more than 35 inches of rain a year. That profile is close to ideal for an air-source heat pump, which delivers heat efficiently down to about 5°F with modern cold-climate inverter compressors. Most Portland homes built before 1980 were equipped with a gas furnace and no central air conditioning at all.

That legacy matters when equipment fails. A 1990s furnace with a cracked heat exchanger, a 15-year-old air handler with a failed blower motor, or a mini-split head clogged with moss and mildew are the three most common calls we run in Portland. The June 2021 heat dome that pushed Portland to an all-time record 116°F also drove a wave of heat pump and mini-split installs, and many of those first-generation systems are now entering their service-and-repair years.

Wildfire smoke is the other Portland-specific factor. During smoke events, a standard 1-inch fiberglass filter does very little. Upgrading to a MERV 13 filter, sealing the return plenum and adding a fresh-air intake with filtration is what keeps indoor air breathable, and undersized returns are the reason many Portland systems lose capacity during the hottest week of the year.

Oregon Licensing, Permits and Energy Trust Rebates

Oregon requires any contractor performing heating and cooling work to hold a Construction Contractors Board (CCB) license, and you can verify a license number at oregon.gov/ccb before anyone touches your system. Handling refrigerant requires EPA Section 608 certification, and replacing a furnace, heat pump or air handler in Portland requires a mechanical permit issued through Portland Permitting & Development. A licensed contractor pulls that permit; an unlicensed one usually asks you to.

The incentive stack is the reason to plan a replacement rather than react to a failure. Energy Trust of Oregon currently pays heat pump rebates in the range of $800 to $1,000 for most homeowners, with income-qualified households eligible for up to about $3,000, and separate incentives for duct sealing, insulation and heat pump water heaters. Portland General Electric and Pacific Power run their own programs on top. Rebates are reserved on a first-come basis within each program year, so the paperwork sequence matters as much as the equipment choice.

Equipment standards have also shifted. R-410A is being phased down in favor of lower-GWP refrigerants such as R-32 and R-454B, which means replacement parts for older systems will get more expensive each year, and new equipment requires different leak-detection and handling procedures. When a 12-year-old system needs a $600 repair, comparing that against a rebated replacement is usually the better question than repairing twice.

Common Portland System Failures We Repair

Run capacitors and contactors are the most frequent summer failure, and they are also the cheapest to fix. Refrigerant leaks come next, typically at the Schrader valves, the line-set flares or the evaporator coil, and a proper repair includes a vacuum test and a leak trace rather than a top-off. Blower motor and inducer assemblies on aging gas furnaces run $450 to $950 installed depending on parts availability.

Portland's pollen season deserves its own line item. The Willamette Valley grows most of the world's grass seed, and the resulting spring pollen load clogs filters and coats evaporator coils faster than in almost any other metro. A coil that has never been cleaned will lose significant capacity, and a mini-split head that has never been washed grows visible mildew on the blower wheel. Annual coil cleaning and filter changes are not upsells here; they are basic maintenance.

If your system is short-cycling, icing over at the outdoor unit, or blowing air that never gets below 70°F, the diagnostic is the cheapest call you will make. We test refrigerant pressures, superheat and subcooling, static pressure and amp draw, then give you a written range of options from repair to replacement.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do Portland HVAC contractors need a license?

Yes. Oregon requires a Construction Contractors Board (CCB) license to perform heating and cooling work, and refrigerant handling requires EPA Section 608 certification. You can check any CCB license number at oregon.gov/ccb.

How much does a heat pump cost in Portland after rebates?

A ducted cold-climate heat pump typically runs $9,500 to $19,000 installed, and a single-zone ductless mini-split runs $4,500 to $8,500. Energy Trust of Oregon rebates currently run about $800 to $1,000 for most homeowners and up to roughly $3,000 for income-qualified households.

Do I need a permit to replace a furnace or heat pump in Portland?

Yes. Furnace, heat pump and air handler replacements require a mechanical permit through Portland Permitting & Development. A licensed contractor pulls the permit and schedules the inspection.

How often should HVAC systems be serviced in Portland?

Once a year for both heating and cooling. Portland's heavy grass-seed pollen season and damp winters clog filters and coat evaporator coils faster than in drier climates, and annual coil cleaning protects system capacity.

How fast can I get emergency HVAC repair in Portland?

Most calls are handled the same day, with 24/7 availability during heat waves and hard freezes. The diagnostic runs $89 to $150 and is credited toward the repair once you approve it.
