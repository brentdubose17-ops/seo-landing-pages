Tennessee Business Taxes: What Nashville Owners Actually Pay

Tennessee's biggest selling point for business owners is what it doesn't have: no state personal income tax. Wages, distributions, and pass-through income from an LLC are not taxed at the state level — a major advantage over neighboring states and a big reason Nashville's small-business scene keeps growing.

But Tennessee businesses aren't tax-free. LLCs and corporations pay the franchise and excise (F&E) tax: a $500 minimum flat franchise tax (after the 2024 reform replaced the old minimum), plus a 6.5% excise tax on net earnings. Nashville's combined sales tax is 9.25% (7% state + 2.25% Davidson County) — the rate your retail, restaurant, and contractor business collects. And under the 2024 "Elvis Act," businesses that overpaid the old franchise tax can claim refunds — worth checking with a professional if you've been filing for years.

What Does a Virtual Bookkeeper Do Differently From an Accountant?

A virtual bookkeeper keeps your day-to-day financials accurate and current — categorizing transactions, reconciling bank accounts, tracking receivables and payables, and producing monthly P&L and balance sheet reports. An accountant (often a CPA) works from those clean books to do tax strategy, entity decisions, and filing. The two are a team: the bookkeeper builds the foundation, the accountant drives the strategy — and trying to skip the foundation is why so many Nashville businesses hit tax season with a shoebox.

With a virtual bookkeeper, "in the cloud" is the point: you get real-time access to your books through QuickBooks Online or Xero, and the work happens on a secure stack with bank-level encryption. Your bookkeeper reconciles monthly (not quarterly), flags anomalies the moment they appear, and hands your CPA a clean file instead of a year of bank statements. For Nashville's remote-first and music/healthcare businesses, that workflow fits how the city actually works.

How to Pick a Nashville Virtual Bookkeeper

Ask five questions before hiring: (1) What software do you use, and can I see the live dashboard? (2) How often do you reconcile — monthly or quarterly? (3) Who's actually doing the work — the person I'm talking to or an offshore subcontractor? (4) Do you handle sales tax filing for Tennessee's 9.25% combined rate? (5) What's your turnaround on monthly close? Any hesitation on #3 or #4 is a yellow flag.

Expect a fixed monthly price, a signed agreement with defined deliverables, and read-only or role-limited access to your accounts — never hand over your banking password. A good Nashville bookkeeper will also want to see your current tax situation and talk to your CPA on a discovery call. If they close their books by the 10th of each month, deliver a clean P&L, and answer emails within a business day, you've found your keeper — and that consistency is what keeps your Tennessee F&E filing and sales tax remittance stress-free.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a bookkeeper for my Nashville LLC?

Not legally — but practically yes. Tennessee has no income tax, but the franchise/excise tax, 9.25% sales tax, and quarterly federal estimates still punish sloppy books. A $299+/month bookkeeper is cheaper than one late filing.

What's the difference between a bookkeeper and a CPA?

A bookkeeper keeps your daily financials current — transactions, reconciliations, monthly reports. A CPA handles tax strategy, entity decisions, and filing. Most Nashville businesses need both: clean books first, strategy second.

How much does catch-up bookkeeping cost in Nashville?

$800–$3,000 one-time, depending on how many months of transactions need reconstructing. Catching up before tax season is always cheaper than filing extensions and guessing at numbers.

What sales tax rate does my Nashville business collect?

9.25% — 7% state plus 2.25% Davidson County. Restaurant, retail, and contractor businesses must register with the Tennessee Department of Revenue and remit monthly or quarterly, with penalties for late filing.

Is a virtual bookkeeper secure?

Yes, with the right setup: read-only bank feeds, role-limited access to QuickBooks Online or Xero, and a signed data-processing agreement. Never share your banking password — a professional bookkeeper won't ask for it.
