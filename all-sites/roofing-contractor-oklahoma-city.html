Oklahoma City Roofing Contractor & Storm Damage Experts

Oklahoma City sits squarely in Hail Alley — severe storms roll through several times a year, and a single hailstorm can damage thousands of roofs at once. This guide covers what a roof costs in Oklahoma City, how hail claims actually work, and how to pick a CIB-licensed contractor who will still be here when the warranty matters.

How Hail Claims Work in Oklahoma

When a storm hits OKC, the clock starts. Most policies require you to report damage promptly, and the insurer's adjuster will inspect your roof — often within a week or two. Hail damage shows up as bruising on asphalt shingles: soft, dark dents where the granules are knocked loose. It also dents gutters, vents, fascia, and outdoor AC units, which strengthens the claim.

Here is the part that trips homeowners up: you do not have to accept the adjuster's findings. If the contractor's inspection documents more damage than the adjuster found — or if the adjuster says no hail damage at all — a licensed roofer can help you push back with photos, or request a second adjuster visit. In a storm-heavy year, insurance companies are aggressive about denying marginal claims, and homeowners who don't push back leave thousands on the table.

Asphalt vs. Metal: Choosing a Roof for Hail Alley

Architectural asphalt shingles are the OKC default — they're affordable, insurance-approved, and look good for 15–25 years. The tradeoff is that hail above a certain size can bruise them, which is why so many OKC roofs get replaced through insurance claims rather than age.

Standing seam metal is the durability play: it shrugs off hail that destroys asphalt, lasts 40–60 years, and often earns an insurance discount. The catch is cost — roughly 1.5–2x an asphalt reroof — and finding a crew that installs metal correctly in Oklahoma's wind environment. Many homeowners split the difference: metal on the primary roof, and budget-conscious asphalt on sheds and detached structures.

Hiring a Roofer After a Storm: The Storm Chaser Trap

Within 48 hours of a big OKC hailstorm, out-of-state 'storm chasers' blanket neighborhoods with door-knockers, 'free roof' offers, and high-pressure same-day contracts. Many are not licensed in Oklahoma, use the cheapest materials, and disappear once the insurance check clears — leaving you with no warranty and no one to call when the roof leaks.

Oklahoma's Construction Industries Board (CIB) licenses roofing contractors, and the license is publicly verifiable. Before you sign anything: confirm the contractor holds an active CIB license, has a physical Oklahoma address and local phone number, carries workers' comp and liability insurance, and will put the scope, materials, and warranty in writing. A local contractor who does 100+ roofs a year in OKC cares about its reputation in ways a storm chaser never will.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a new roof cost in Oklahoma City?

An asphalt shingle reroof on a typical OKC home runs $9,000–$20,000 depending on size, pitch, and deck condition. Metal roofs run $14,000–$30,000 but survive hail far better.

Does homeowners insurance cover hail damage in Oklahoma?

Yes — hail damage to your roof is a covered peril under standard Oklahoma homeowners policies. The catch is proving the damage and documenting it before the adjuster's deadline.

How do I know if my roof has hail damage?

Look for bruising on asphalt shingles (soft, dark dents), missing granules, cracked or split shingles, and dents on gutters, vents, and AC units. A licensed roofer will inspect and document it free.

Are roofing contractors required to be licensed in Oklahoma?

Yes — roofing contractors must be licensed by Oklahoma's Construction Industries Board (CIB). Verify the license number on the CIB website before signing anything, especially after a storm.

Should I use an out-of-state storm chaser for my roof?

No. Storm chasers often lack Oklahoma licenses, use substandard materials, and disappear before warranty work is needed. Choose a local, CIB-licensed contractor with an Oklahoma address.
