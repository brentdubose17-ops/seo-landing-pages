Licensed Roofing & Waterproofing Contractor in San Francisco, CA

San Francisco roofs fail from salt fog, wind-driven rain and seismic movement, not from snow load. A reroof here means flat tar-and-gravel or modified bitumen on the city's rowhouses, tile underlayment replacement on the older homes in Pacific Heights and the Marina, and a cool-roof specification that satisfies California's Title 24 energy code. We pull the DBI permit and do the work to inspection standard.

What San Francisco's Climate Does to Roofs

The marine layer and salt-laden air off the Pacific attack fasteners, flashing and metal edge trim year-round. Within a mile or two of the ocean, galvanized fasteners commonly rust through in under 15 years, which is why coastal re-roofs specify stainless or hot-dip galvanized hardware. Salt fog also degrades the granule surface on composition shingles faster than inland UV alone would.

Atmospheric river storms are the real leak trigger. When 3 to 6 inches of rain falls in 48 hours with wind behind it, water gets driven sideways under lifted shingles, through failed step flashing at dormers and chimneys, and into parapet walls through cracked stucco. Because San Francisco homes are built shoulder-to-shoulder, a leak at one rowhouse often shows up as a stain on a neighbor's ceiling, which turns a repair into a two-property conversation.

October is San Francisco's warmest month, and thermal movement on dark flat roofs cracks the flood coat and opens the seams. Combine that with the city's pre-1950 building stock and the seismic racking that goes with it, and most flat-roof leaks here trace back to a failed base flashing or a split at a stress point rather than to the field membrane itself.

Permits, Title 24 and California Licensing

California requires a contractor license for any job over $500 in labor and materials, and roofing is a C-39 classification. You can check a license, bond and workers' compensation status in one lookup at cslb.ca.gov. Since 2023 the required contractor bond in California is $25,000, and unlicensed operators cannot lawfully contract for roofing work or enforce payment for it.

San Francisco requires a building permit for reroofing through the Department of Building Inspection, including overlay and tear-off work. The permit process also triggers California's Title 24 energy requirements: most reroofs must use cool-roof rated materials listed by the Cool Roof Rating Council, with reflectance and emittance thresholds that vary by roof slope, and only narrow exceptions for buildings that already meet higher insulation or efficiency requirements. A contractor who proposes a standard dark shingle without addressing the cool-roof requirement is setting you up for a failed inspection.

It is worth knowing that a San Francisco reroof permit can also trigger requirements around the number of existing roof layers and the condition of the drainage system. Scuppers, roof drains and overflow outlets are usually the first things an inspector looks at on a flat roof, because ponding water is the failure mode that insurance adjusters and inspectors both look for.

Repair or Reroof? How to Decide

Repair first when the failure is localized: a single flashing detail, a cracked vent boot, a failed pipe collar or one section of split membrane at a stress point. A targeted repair with a moisture survey typically runs $450 to $1,200 and buys real time if the field membrane is still sound and dry. Repair is the wrong answer when moisture has migrated into the decking or the insulation layer, because the wet material stays wet and the leak reappears somewhere else.

Reroof when the membrane is past its service life, when a moisture survey finds widespread saturation, when there are multiple active leak paths, or when a building sale is approaching and the roof becomes a disclosure item. Built-up tar-and-gravel roofs in San Francisco commonly last 15 to 25 years depending on traffic and ponding; modified bitumen roughly 15 to 20; composition shingles 20 to 25 inland and less in the fog belt; tile itself can last a century while the underlayment beneath it fails in 20 to 30 years.

If you are on the fence, ask for three things in writing: a moisture survey result, the number of existing roof layers, and the itemized scope for both options. That comparison is what separates a real estimate from a sales pitch.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a permit to reroof in San Francisco?

Yes. Reroofing requires a building permit from the San Francisco Department of Building Inspection, including overlay work. The permit also brings in California's Title 24 cool-roof requirements.

What is the Title 24 cool roof requirement in San Francisco?

Most reroofs must use cool-roof rated materials listed with the Cool Roof Rating Council, with reflectance and emittance thresholds that depend on roof slope. Exceptions are narrow and generally require the building to already meet higher efficiency standards.

How much does a new roof cost in San Francisco?

Expect roughly $14,000 to $38,000 for a flat reroof on a 1,200 to 1,800 square foot home and $18,000 to $45,000 for a composition shingle reroof with tear-off. Tile underlayment replacement runs higher.

Does my roofing contractor need a C-39 license in California?

Yes. California requires a contractor license for any job over $500 in labor and materials, and roofing is the C-39 classification. You can verify license, bond and insurance status at cslb.ca.gov.

How long does a flat roof last in San Francisco?

Built-up tar-and-gravel roofs typically last 15 to 25 years here, and modified bitumen about 15 to 20. Salt fog corrosion of fasteners and ponding water are the two factors that shorten those numbers most.
