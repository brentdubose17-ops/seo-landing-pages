Philadelphia Roofing Contractors Who Do It Right

Philadelphia roofs are not like the rest of the country's. Rowhome roofs are small but hard to reach, many are flat and covered in modified bitumen, and older homes still carry slate that no rookie crew should touch. The city also regulates the work tightly: roofers on one- and two-family homes must be registered Home Improvement Contractors with the Pennsylvania Attorney General's Office, bigger jobs need a contractor license from L&I, and most replacements need a building permit. This guide covers real Philadelphia pricing, the credentials to check, and how to handle storm damage without getting burned.

What Does Roofing Cost in Philadelphia?

Philadelphia prices sit close to the national average, with rowhome jobs priced by the smaller square footage plus access difficulty — crews on rowhome roofs often work off ladders with no room for dumpsters in the street. Most asphalt shingle jobs on pitched rowhomes land between $7,000 and $15,000, and larger single-family roofs run $12,000–$22,000.

Licensing and Permits: PA Registration and Philadelphia L&I

Pennsylvania requires any contractor doing home improvement work — including roofing — on existing one- and two-family dwellings to register as a Home Improvement Contractor with the Pennsylvania Attorney General's Office. That registration is public and searchable, and it is the first thing to verify. Contractors doing work on larger residential buildings, commercial properties, or new construction need a contractor license from Philadelphia's Department of Licenses and Inspections (L&I) instead — and every business operating in the city must register for the BIRT (Business Income and Receipts Tax), which is why a legitimate roofer can produce both a state registration and a city tax account.

Permits are not optional in Philadelphia. L&I requires a building permit for most roof replacements and structural repairs, and the permit triggers an inspection once the work is done. A roofer who tells you a re-roof does not need a permit is wrong — and unpermitted roof work becomes your problem at resale, when the buyer's title search surfaces the work with no inspection record.

Rowhomes, Flat Roofs, Slate, and Philly Weather

Philadelphia's housing stock dictates the roofing market. Hundreds of thousands of rowhomes have small, flat or low-slope roofs covered in modified bitumen, EPDM, or torch-down — systems that need different skills and materials than pitched shingle work. Older neighborhoods like Fairmount, Queen Village, and Germantown still have slate roofs that can last a century but require specialist repair; a general crew that hammers through slate will destroy a historic roof in one visit. Ask any contractor how many rowhome flat roofs and slate jobs they have done this year, and get references for your specific roof type.

Philly weather is hard on roofs in its own way. Nor'easters drive wind-driven rain under flashing, snow load stresses flat roofs where drainage is poor, and freeze-thaw cycles in late winter crack flashings and lift shingles. Two maintenance habits matter more here than anywhere: keep flat-roof drains and scuppers clear of leaves in the fall, and have flashing inspected after every major wind event. Most Philly roof leaks are flashing failures, not membrane failures — and flashing repairs are the cheapest work a roofer sells.

Storm Damage and Insurance Claims in Philadelphia

When a nor'easter or wind storm damages your roof, the sequence matters. Contain the damage first — a tarp from a licensed roofer, typically $500–$1,500, is usually covered under your policy's emergency mitigation. Photograph everything before any repair. Then file your claim promptly; most homeowners policies require prompt notice and proof you mitigated further damage. Your insurer's adjuster will assess the roof, and the roofer should be present at the adjuster visit to point out damage the adjuster cannot see from the ground.

Two Philadelphia-specific cautions. First, watch the deductible: if your roof is older than 15–20 years, your policy may pay actual cash value rather than replacement cost, leaving you with a significant gap — know which you carry before the storm. Second, beware storm-chaser contractors who arrive from out of state after big weather events, offer to waive deductibles, and disappear. A registered Pennsylvania Home Improvement Contractor with a local address, a real BIRT account, and verifiable references is the only safe hire after a storm.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a roof replacement cost in Philadelphia?

Asphalt shingle replacement on a typical Philadelphia rowhome runs $7,000–$15,000, and larger single-family homes with two or more roof planes run $12,000–$22,000. Repairs are usually $300–$1,800 depending on the leak.

Are Philadelphia roofers required to be licensed?

Roofers who work on existing one- and two-family homes must register as Home Improvement Contractors with the Pennsylvania Attorney General's Office. Contractors doing larger or commercial work need a contractor license from Philadelphia's Department of Licenses and Inspections (L&I), and all businesses operating in the city must register for the BIRT tax.

Do I need a permit to replace my roof in Philadelphia?

Yes. Philadelphia's L&I department requires a building permit for most roof replacements and structural repairs. Your contractor pulls the permit and schedules the inspection — never let a roofer start without one.

How long do rowhome flat roofs last in Philadelphia?

A modified bitumen or EPDM flat roof on a Philadelphia rowhome typically lasts 15–25 years with routine maintenance, while asphalt shingle roofs on pitched homes last 18–25 years depending on the shingle grade.

What should I do if my roof leaks during a storm?

Contain the water, move valuables, and call a roofing contractor for an emergency tarp — expect $500–$1,500 for tarping and stabilization. Document the damage with photos and contact your insurer quickly, since most policies require prompt notice and proof of mitigation.
