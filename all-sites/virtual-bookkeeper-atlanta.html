How Much Does a Bookkeeper Cost in Atlanta?

Virtual bookkeeping in Atlanta runs $300–$1,200/month for most small businesses, with price driven by monthly transaction volume, number of bank/credit accounts, and complexity (payroll, inventory, multiple entities). A solo business with under 100 monthly transactions typically pays $300–$500/month, while a growing business with employees, inventory, and multiple accounts pays $800–$1,200/month.

Catch-up bookkeeping — cleaning up months or years of messy or missing records — is billed separately, typically $1,000–$5,000 depending on how far behind the books are. Compare that to a local Atlanta firm's in-house bookkeeper at $25–$40/hour or a full-time hire at $45,000–$60,000 plus benefits: for most small businesses, virtual flat-rate bookkeeping is the clear value, since you're paying for the work, not a seat in your office.

Bookkeeper vs CPA in Georgia: What's the Difference?

In Georgia, "bookkeeper" and "accountant" are not licensed titles — anyone can call themselves a bookkeeper — but a CPA is a regulated credential issued by the Georgia State Board of Accountancy and requires passing the CPA exam plus continuing education. Practically, that means the two roles do different work: a bookkeeper records transactions, reconciles accounts, and produces financial statements; a CPA handles tax strategy, tax filing, and audit representation.

Georgia CPAs are the only ones authorized to represent you in a state tax audit or sign off on audited financials. The division of labor most Atlanta businesses settle into: a virtual bookkeeper monthly for clean books, and a CPA quarterly and at tax time for strategy and filing. This pairing costs less than a full-service CPA firm and keeps your books accurate year-round rather than panic-cleaned every April.

Georgia Sales Tax & Compliance for Small Business

Georgia's state sales tax is 4%, and local jurisdictions add their own — Atlanta businesses typically collect a combined 8.9% (4% state + 3% Fulton/DeKalb county SPLOST + 1% MARTA tax, with slight variation by county). If you sell taxable goods or certain services, you must register with the Georgia Department of Revenue, collect sales tax, and file monthly or quarterly returns.

The compliance trap for Atlanta startups is the use tax and the 1099/Nexus rules. If you buy equipment online without paying Georgia sales tax, you owe the equivalent use tax; and if you hire contractors, you owe 1099-NEC filings at year-end or face penalties. Georgia also requires an annual report and registration fee for LLCs and corporations. A bookkeeper who understands Georgia's combined rates and filing calendar keeps you out of these traps before the DOR sends a notice.

Catch-Up Bookkeeping: Fixing Messy Records

Atlanta's startup scene moves fast, and the books are usually the last thing founders update — which is how you end up a year behind with unreconciled accounts, mixed personal and business expenses, and no idea of true profitability. Catch-up bookkeeping rebuilds the records: importing and categorizing every transaction, reconciling all bank and credit accounts, and producing clean year-to-date financials.

The cost of ignoring it is steep. A founder who went two years without bookkeeping spent $3,800 on a catch-up cleanup and discovered $18,000 in deductible expenses they'd never claimed — the cleanup more than paid for itself in a single tax filing. If you're behind, don't wait for the April rush; Atlanta bookkeepers are swamped January–April, and starting catch-up work in the fall gets you clean books before year-end and a faster, cheaper tax season.
