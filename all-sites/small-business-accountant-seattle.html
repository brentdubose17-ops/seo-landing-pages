How Much Does a Seattle Small Business Accountant Cost?

Seattle small businesses pay $250–$800 per month for ongoing bookkeeping, $300–$1,500 for business tax preparation, and $500–$2,500 for entity formation and setup. Payroll services run $50–$150 per month plus per-employee fees. Most Seattle firms bundle bookkeeping, monthly reconciliation, and quarterly tax filings into a flat monthly rate.

The investment pays for itself in tax avoided and penalties prevented. Washington's B&O tax is a gross-receipts tax — it applies whether or not you're profitable — so a knowledgeable accountant often finds deductions and apportionment strategies that save far more than the monthly fee.

Why Washington's B&O Tax Changes Everything

Washington is one of the few states with no personal income tax and no corporate income tax — but it makes up for it with the Business & Occupation (B&O) tax, charged on gross receipts at rates from 0.471% (retailing) to 1.5% (services), depending on your classification. Seattle adds a local B&O surcharge on top.

B&O classification is where most Seattle businesses get into trouble: miscategorizing services as retail can double your tax rate, and missing quarterly filings triggers penalties and interest. A Seattle accountant handles classification, apportionment for multi-state revenue, and the state's new annual tax structure changes — all of which are easy to get wrong on your own.

How Much Are Seattle Sales Taxes for Your Business?

Seattle's combined sales tax rate is 10.25% (state base plus King County and city local options) — one of the highest in the country. If you sell products or taxable services, you must register for a Washington seller's permit, collect sales tax on every sale, and file returns monthly or quarterly. A 2024 Washington Department of Revenue compliance push has increased audits of small e-commerce sellers.

Remote sellers also face nexus rules: if your business sells into Washington or employs Washington residents, you likely owe B&O and sales tax regardless of where you're headquartered. Seattle's remote-work boom means many out-of-state companies now have Washington nexus through remote employees — a common audit trigger.

What Should Seattle Startups Look for in an Accountant?

Seattle's startup economy runs on equity, RSUs, and venture funding, which creates tax complexity beyond basic bookkeeping: 409A valuations, R&D tax credits, multi-state payroll for distributed teams, and founder tax planning. Look for a CPA who works with funded companies and understands stock compensation — it's a different specialty than restaurant or construction accounting.

For service businesses and contractors, the key services are clean monthly books, quarterly B&O filings, sales tax returns, and payroll. Ask any candidate three questions: how they handle B&O apportionment, whether they file sales tax for you, and how they communicate during tax season — the answers separate generalists from Seattle specialists.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Washington have state income tax?

No — Washington has no personal or corporate income tax. The main business taxes are B&O tax on gross receipts and sales tax. Seattle also has no local income tax.

What is the B&O tax rate in Seattle?

State B&O rates range from 0.471% (retailing) to 1.5% (services), and Seattle adds a local surcharge. Your rate depends on your business classification — getting it right is where an accountant earns their fee.

Do I need a CPA to file my Seattle business taxes?

Legally, no. Practically, B&O classification, sales tax nexus, and apportionment rules are complex enough that DIY businesses frequently overpay or face penalties. Most Seattle firms offer a free consultation to assess your exposure.

How often do I need to file Washington business taxes?

B&O tax is filed quarterly (some businesses qualify for annual filing). Sales tax is filed monthly or quarterly depending on volume. Late filings incur penalties and interest.

Can you help if I'm behind on my books?

Yes. Seattle accountants regularly take on catch-up bookkeeping and past-due filings. Bringing three months of unorganized records costs $500–$2,500 to clean up — cheaper than the penalties you'd otherwise face.
