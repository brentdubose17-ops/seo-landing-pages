A New York Virtual Bookkeeper That Keeps You Compliant

New York taxes small businesses harder than almost any state in the country — and it taxes them in layers: state corporate franchise tax up to 7.25%, New York City's own 8.85% general corporation tax on C-corps in the five boroughs, payroll taxes, and a remote-work rule that reaches across state lines. Yet the bookkeeping itself needs no license, and the services are not subject to sales tax. A good New York virtual bookkeeper sits at the intersection of those rules — this guide explains what the job covers, what NY throws at you, and what it costs.

What a New York Virtual Bookkeeper Does

A virtual bookkeeper runs your monthly financial engine from the cloud — no office visit, no shoebox of receipts. The work is concrete: bank and credit card feeds reconciled in QuickBooks or Xero, clean monthly financials, payroll runs through a service like Gusto or ADP, sales tax filings where they apply, 1099s for contractors, and a watchful eye on worker classification so your contractors are not reclassified as employees in an audit.

For a New York business the bookkeeper also owns the registration paperwork: withholding registration with the New York State Department of Taxation and Finance, sales tax registration when you sell taxable goods, and the city-level filings that apply to your structure. A bookkeeper cannot give legal or CPA-level tax advice, but a great one flags the questions that need a CPA before they become penalties.

New York Tax Rules That Trip Up Small Businesses

New York State's corporate franchise tax tops out at 7.25% for most C-corporations, and New York City stacks its own general corporation tax of 8.85% on C-corps doing business in the five boroughs — a combined corporate burden few founders price in when they incorporate. S-corporations and most LLCs sidestep the entity-level corporate tax and pass income through to owners, but then face the state's personal income tax, which runs to double digits at the top brackets.

Two more New York rules deserve special attention. First, the MCTMT — the Metropolitan Commuter Transportation Mobility Tax — applies to employers with payroll above a set annual threshold in the MCTD (which includes NYC and surrounding counties). Second, the convenience of the employer rule: a nonresident who works remotely from another state for a New York employer generally still owes New York income tax on those wages, because the state treats the work as performed where the employer needs it. That single rule creates withholding obligations for businesses whose employees have never set foot in the city.

Bookkeeper vs. CPA in New York

New York does not license bookkeepers — anyone can offer bookkeeping services. What is regulated is the CPA title: only individuals licensed by the New York State Education Department can call themselves CPAs, sign financial statements, or perform attest work. That split matters for hiring. A bookkeeper keeps the books current and the filings on time for $300–$1,000 a month. A CPA — often working with your bookkeeper — owns tax strategy, entity choice, the year-end return, and representation if New York or the IRS comes calling.

The businesses that run clean in New York use both: bookkeeper monthly, CPA quarterly and at year-end. The businesses that bleed money try to make the CPA do monthly bookkeeping at CPA rates, or skip the bookkeeper entirely and discover in March that their records are a year of chaos. In a state with this many tax layers, the monthly discipline is not overhead — it is the cheapest insurance you can buy.

How Much Does Virtual Bookkeeping Cost in New York?

New York rates run above the national median because compliance complexity is real. Monthly virtual bookkeeping for a small business typically runs $300–$1,000 depending on transaction volume; payroll adds $75–$250 per run; e-commerce and multi-entity businesses pay at the top of the range; and catch-up cleanup — taking a year of neglected records and making them audit-ready — runs $500–$3,000 as a project. Flat monthly pricing is the model to look for, with the scope written down so the price does not drift when your transaction count grows.

Ask any candidate about New York specifics before you hire: have they filed NYS and NYC taxes for businesses like yours, do they handle the MCTMT, and do they understand the convenience rule for your remote staff? A bookkeeper who only knows generic QuickBooks work will learn New York on your dime.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do bookkeepers need a license in New York?

No license is required to offer bookkeeping services in New York. The title CPA is regulated — only individuals licensed by the New York State Education Department can call themselves CPAs or sign attest work.

Are bookkeeping services subject to New York sales tax?

No. New York generally does not tax professional and bookkeeping services, so a New York bookkeeper does not collect sales tax on service fees — though payroll and sales tax registration for clients is still part of the job.

What is New York's corporate tax rate?

New York State's corporate franchise tax tops out at 7.25% for most C-corporations, and New York City adds its own general corporation tax of 8.85% for C-corps doing business in the five boroughs. S-corps and most LLCs file as pass-throughs instead.

I work remotely for a New York company from another state — do I owe New York tax?

Under New York's convenience of the employer rule, a nonresident working from home for a New York employer generally still owes New York tax on those wages. Your bookkeeper should plan withholding accordingly.

What does virtual bookkeeping cost in New York?

Monthly virtual bookkeeping for a small New York business typically runs $300–$1,000 depending on transaction volume, with payroll runs added at $75–$250 each and catch-up cleanup projects billed separately at $500–$3,000.
