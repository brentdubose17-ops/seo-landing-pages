Seattle Roofing Built for Rain, Moss, and Wind

No climate in the country tests a roof quite like Seattle's: roughly 37 inches of rain a year, months of overcast damp, moss that colonizes shaded slopes by spring, and winter windstorms that strip shingles in a single night. Washington also runs one of the strictest contractor registration systems in the country — every roofer must be registered with the state Department of Labor & Industries, bonded, and insured, and homeowners can verify all of it in seconds. This guide covers Seattle roofing costs, the L&I registration you should check, and how to keep moss and moisture from eating your roof early.

L&I Registration, Bonding, and How to Verify a Roofer

Washington state requires every construction contractor — roofing included — to register with the Department of Labor & Industries before doing any work. Registration is not a license exam; it is a consumer-protection system that requires contractors to carry a state-required bond and liability insurance and to maintain a record L&I can show the public. Roofing is treated as its own specialty contractor registration, and the registration renews every two years.

The practical move is the L&I Verify a Contractor lookup, which is free and takes about a minute. Enter the company name and you will see whether the registration is active, whether the bond is in place, and any complaints or citations on file. Never hire a roofer who cannot produce an active L&I registration — an unregistered contractor cannot legally pull the permit for your reroof, and if they damage your home, you have no bond to claim against.

Why Seattle's Weather Is the Enemy of Roofs

Seattle's damp, overcast climate does four kinds of damage that roofers in drier states rarely see. Moss and lichen grow on any shaded, north-facing slope, and moss holds water against the shingles all winter — lifting edges, wicking moisture under the course above, and rotting the decking underneath. Algae streaks darken light-colored asphalt, mostly cosmetic but a sign of chronic moisture. The long wet season — October through May — means any flashing failure or popped nail has weeks to leak before you even notice. And winter windstorms, which regularly gust past 60 mph in November and December, lift shingles whose adhesive strips have aged or never sealed properly in the cold.

Prevention is cheap relative to replacement: treat moss every couple of years, keep gutters clear so water drains fast, trim branches that drop debris and shade the roof, and inspect after every significant windstorm. Seattle homes with good ventilation and clear drainage routinely get 5–10 extra years out of the same roof as identical homes left to the moss.

Asphalt, Cedar, or Metal: What Belongs on a Seattle Roof

Architectural asphalt is the workhorse of Seattle — affordable, familiar, and perfectly adequate for the climate with one caveat: it needs the moss managed. Cedar shake and shingle roofs are the classic Pacific Northwest look and handle rain beautifully, but they demand the most maintenance of any option and carry higher fire-risk ratings unless treated. Standing seam metal is the low-maintenance champion: water and moss slide off it, it survives windstorms that lift asphalt, and a 40–60 year lifespan makes it the best long-term value for homeowners who plan to stay — the trade-off is a large upfront check.

Whatever material you choose, the details matter more in Seattle than anywhere: synthetic underlayment instead of felt, ice-and-water membrane in the valleys and the first several feet of eave, new flashing around chimneys and skylights rather than reused, and real ridge ventilation so the attic can dry out. A roofer who talks about those details is a roofer who has replaced a lot of moss-eaten Seattle roofs — which is exactly the experience you want on yours.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to replace a roof in Seattle?

On a typical 2,000-square-foot Seattle home, an architectural asphalt shingle reroof runs $9,000–$18,000. Cedar shake roofs run $16,000–$32,000, and metal roofs $20,000–$38,000 depending on panel type and complexity.

Do roofing contractors need a license in Washington?

Yes. Washington requires every construction contractor, including roofers, to register with the state Department of Labor & Industries (L&I), carry a state-required bond, and maintain liability insurance. Homeowners can verify any contractor's registration, bond, and complaint history through L&I's free Verify a Contractor lookup.

How do I get rid of moss on my roof in Seattle?

Professional moss treatment runs $300–$700 for a typical home and includes cleaning plus a preventive treatment. Skip power washing — it damages shingles — and prevent regrowth with zinc strips, trimming overhanging branches, and keeping gutters clear so water drains fast.

How long does an asphalt roof last in the Seattle climate?

18–25 years is realistic for architectural asphalt shingles in the damp Pacific Northwest, a little less than the 25–30 year rating because constant moisture and moss shorten life. Cedar lasts 20–30 years with care, and metal 40–60 years.

Does Seattle require a permit for roof replacement?

Most reroofs that replace roof decking or change the roof structure require a permit from Seattle's Department of Construction and Inspections. A registered roofing contractor will tell you whether your reroof needs one and will pull it as part of the job.
