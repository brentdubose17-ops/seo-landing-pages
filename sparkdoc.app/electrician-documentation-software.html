Electrician documentation software that survives a real job site

Most tools in this category are sold to the office and used in a crawlspace. Those are two different environments, and only one of them decides whether your crew still opens the app in month two. Here are the criteria that hold up with a phone in one hand and a flashlight in the other, the features that impress in a demo and get ignored in the field, and a two-week trial you can run on live work instead of a sample project.

Start with the job site, not the feature list

A 20-minute demo on good Wi-Fi tells you almost nothing. What tells you everything is the moment the dead front comes off: one hand on the phone, one on the flashlight, a homeowner asking how long the power stays off, and no signal in the basement. Every tap required before the camera opens is a tax on that moment — and it is a tax your crew will eventually refuse to pay by going back to texting photos to the office phone.

Score the tool against the work, not the marketing page. Four numbers beat any feature list:

  • Camera to a saved, tagged photo: 15 seconds or less.
  • Thirty-photo panel walkthrough with captions: 10 minutes or less.
  • Change order captured, priced, and approved in front of the customer: 15 minutes or less.
  • Finding last spring's photo of a specific panel by address: 30 seconds or less.

If a tool misses those on your phone, in your basement, on a Tuesday afternoon, the rest of the comparison is academic.

The criteria that actually decide it

Seven criteria separate tools that get used from tools that get cancelled. Score each one from 1 to 5 during a trial. Anything below a 4 on offline capture or export is a stop sign, regardless of everything else.

Criterion What good looks like How to test it in minutes
Offline capture Photos, captions, tags, and timestamps save to the device first, then sync when signal returns. No spinner, no lost entry, no duplicate. Airplane mode on. Add five photos and notes to a job. Force-quit the app, reopen it, and confirm nothing is missing.
Photo markup Arrows, circles, and text drawn on the image and baked into the exported file, so the markup survives email, printing, and a client's PDF viewer. Mark up three photos with a defect label and a circuit number. Export and open the file on a different device.
Mobile speed Camera opens from the job screen in one tap. Tagging uses a list of your real panel and room names instead of free typing. Time yourself from lock screen to saved photo, five times. Consistency matters more than your best run.
Report output One PDF per job or per area, with your logo, license number, service address, captions, and timestamps — not a dump of raw images. Export a finished log and email it to a client from the truck. Open it on a phone, not just a laptop.
Change-order workflow Capture the extra, write the scope, add price and schedule impact, get written or signed approval — all attached to the same job file as the photos. Run one real change order end to end, approval included, before you make a decision.
Export and ownership Bulk export of original files in a standard format, plus a written retention and deletion policy you can read. Export everything you created during the trial and open it on a computer that has never had the app installed.
Findability Search by address, panel name, tag, or date, and filters that still work at 40 photos or 4,000. Without scrolling, find one photo you took in week one using search only.

Two honorable mentions: device support and pricing model. Your crew is split between iPhones and Android phones, and a tablet-only app that needs a desktop to start a job will lose to a phone every time. On price, do the annual math — five users on a $30 per-user monthly plan is $1,800 a year, roughly one billable service call. That is an easy number to justify if the change-order day goes smoothly and a hard one to justify if it does not.

What to ignore when you are comparing

  • Auto-written defect descriptions. If the software drafts a paragraph about a code violation from a photo, a human still has to verify it, and that human is you — the one signing the report.
  • Feature counts. A checklist that weights 80 features equally rewards the wrong product. You need six things to work, not sixty to exist.
  • Integrations you will never wire up. Accounting sync, CRM sync, and project management suites matter to a 200-person contractor. If you run three to ten electricians out of two trucks, the integrations page is decoration.
  • Trials that require a card and a 30-minute onboarding call before you can take a photo. If you cannot photograph a panel in the first five minutes, the onboarding is the product.
  • Per-report fees. Charging per exported PDF punishes you for documenting more, which is exactly backwards.
  • Template libraries. Twelve templates that match your actual work — service upgrade, panel change, rough-in, trim-out, generator install, inspection punch list — beat 400 generic ones.
  • Desktop-first tools. The documentation happens in the building, not at the kitchen table at 9 p.m.

Your data: export, ownership, and the exit clause

Your photos are evidence in a dispute over pre-existing damage, a failed inspection, or unpaid extra work. That makes two questions more important than any interface: can you get your files out, and in what shape. Ask directly and get the answers in writing.

  • Can I export all photos and reports in bulk, not one job at a time?
  • Do I get full-resolution original image files, or only flattened PDFs?
  • Are timestamps and location data preserved on export?
  • If I cancel, how long do you retain my data, and how do I retrieve it before deletion?
  • Is storage capped or metered, and what happens at the cap — does the camera stop working?

A vendor that hesitates on bulk export of originals is telling you something. Data that exists only inside one app is not documentation; it is leverage. On retention, many contractors keep job files for seven years, but the right answer depends on your state's statute of limitations, your insurance carrier, and your licensing board. Ask your attorney, not a software salesperson. And decide your own policy on location tags: geotagged photos prove where you worked and can be unwelcome in a customer's home.

Run a two-week trial on a live job

Skip the demo project. Pick one real job — ideally a service upgrade or panel change with a genuine change order in it — and run the software for two weeks. Install it on at least two devices: the owner's phone and the newest person on the crew. The apprentice is your most honest usability tester.

Week one

  • Day 1: Install on both phones. Shoot the before photos — panel with the dead front off, meter, service mast.
  • Days 2–3: Build a full photo log for the job, tagging panel name, room, and circuit where it applies. Aim for 15 to 40 photos.
  • Day 4: Mark up three photos with a defect label and a circuit number, then export and check them on a laptop.
  • Day 5: Export the job report as a PDF, send it to a client and to yourself, and time the whole task.

Week two

  • Days 6–8: Run one change order end to end — capture, scope, price, approval — attached to the same job file as the photos.
  • Day 9: Test offline for real. Airplane mode in a crawlspace, ten photos with captions, then back into signal to confirm everything synced without gaps or duplicates.
  • Day 10: Find a week-one photo using search only. Over 30 seconds means your tagging scheme or the search needs work.
  • Day 11: Export everything and open it on a computer that has never had the app installed.
  • Day 12: Debrief the crew and ask who avoided using it, and why. One person quietly opting out is the most reliable predictor that a tool will not stick.

On day 12, score the seven criteria, count the minutes the trial saved on change-order day, and compare the annual cost against that. If the paperwork gets finished faster and the customer approved the extra work on the spot, you have your answer.

Frequently asked questions

Does electrician documentation software have to work offline?

Yes. Most residential and light commercial work happens in basements, crawlspaces, mechanical rooms, and new construction with no usable signal. If the app cannot save photos, captions, and notes without a connection and sync them later, your crew will stop using it within a week. Test it in airplane mode before you pay for anything.

What should a pre-work photo log actually include?

Address and job name, a date and time stamp, a label for the panel or equipment, and a one-line caption saying what the photo shows. Cover the existing condition before you touch anything: panel interiors with the dead front off, the meter and service mast, existing wiring methods, wall and ceiling finishes, and any pre-existing damage. A typical service upgrade runs 15 to 40 photos.

Can I write and send a change order from the job site?

Only if the tool handles capture, scope, price, and approval in one flow. You photograph the extra work, write the scope in plain language, add the price and any schedule impact, and get the customer's written or signed approval before you start. If the change order lives in a different app from the photos, the two records drift apart and you lose the argument later.

Who owns the photos and reports I create?

You should, and you should be able to prove it. Read the terms for bulk export in a standard format, full-resolution original images rather than only flattened PDFs, and a written retention and deletion policy. If a vendor will not let you walk away with your own originals, treat that as a deal breaker no matter how good the app looks.

How long should I keep electrical job documentation?

It varies by state and by your insurance carrier, and many contractors keep job records for seven years. Check with your attorney, your carrier, and your licensing board rather than guessing. Because your photos can become evidence in a dispute, keep the originals with their timestamps, not just the exported report.
