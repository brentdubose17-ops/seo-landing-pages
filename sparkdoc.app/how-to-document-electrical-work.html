How to Document Electrical Work: A Repeatable Process

A working process for electrical contractors — what to photograph at pre-work, rough-in, and closeout, how to name and file it, how to tie photos to change orders, and how to keep the whole thing under two minutes per area so your crews actually do it.

Why most electrical documentation fails before it starts

Most electrical shops don't lose money because the work was wrong. They lose it because nobody can prove what the work looked like when they walked away. Drywall goes up, the homeowner repaints the hallway, and three weeks later it's one person's memory against another's.

Documentation is a habit problem, not a paperwork problem. The standard to aim for: if a photo can't be found in 30 seconds by someone who wasn't on the job, it doesn't exist. That means a timestamp on every image, a folder for each stage, and a filename that says what the photo shows without opening it.

"Done" on a job means four things: a dated photo set for every stage, a photo plus a written note for every deviation from the print, before-and-after evidence plus a signature on every change order, and a closeout package assembled within 48 hours of the final inspection instead of the week the customer stops calling.

Stage 1: Pre-work photos, before you touch anything

Pre-work photos are the cheapest insurance in the trade. Ten minutes at the start of a job kills the two most common disputes: pre-existing damage and unexpected existing conditions.

  • A wide shot of every room you'll work in, taken from the doorway so the room is identifiable.
  • Panel interior with the dead front on and off, plus a straight-on shot of the existing breaker directory.
  • Existing damage anywhere near your work: cracked tile, scuffed flooring, a hole behind the TV mount.
  • Existing wiring method and condition — cloth-covered NM, aluminum branch circuits, a corroded SE cable, a double-tapped breaker, a bonding jumper missing from the water line.
  • Service equipment and meter before you pull anything, including the meter number.
  • The customer's stored items if they're staying inside your work area.

Caption while you're standing there. "Kitchen — pre-existing burn mark at outlet, SW corner" takes eight seconds and saves an hour later. If you're photographing a floor-protection transition or a stairway you'll be dragging conduit through, shoot it from an angle that shows the whole surface, not a close-up of one scratch.

Stage 2: Rough-in and in-progress evidence

Rough-in is the only stage you can't re-create. Once drywall is up, a photo of an open box is the difference between "we installed it to code" and "prove it."

  • Every box, open, with the circuit number visible. Write the number on painter's tape and stick it to the stud next to the box before you shoot.
  • Panel interior before conductors land and after, including the torque mark on each lug.
  • Grounding electrode conductor and bonding jumpers, with the ground rod or water pipe clamp in the frame.
  • Underground work in stages: trench depth with a tape measure in frame, conduit before backfill, warning tape, then backfill.
  • Cable support spacing, with a tape measure showing that staples are within 12 in. of the box and every 4.5 ft along the run per NEC 334.30.
  • Any redline markups, shot with the print lying next to the actual condition.

Three angles beat one: a wide shot for location, a medium shot for method, and a close-up for detail — a torque mark, a wire gauge stamp, a label, a test reading. If a photo doesn't show a landmark or a circuit ID, add one. A mystery photo of gray conduit helps nobody during an inspection six months later.

Stage 3: Closeout, where photos become a report

Closeout is where a pile of camera roll images turns into something an inspector, a general contractor, or a homeowner can actually read.

  • Panel schedule: type it, then photograph the printed directory and the finished panel together. Handwritten directories shot at an angle are unreadable, and inspectors will say so.
  • Every breaker labeled, with a photo of the closed panel where the labels are legible without zooming.
  • Test results with the tester in the frame — GFCI and AFCI trip tests, receptacle polarity and ground checks, and a clamp meter reading at the service if you're documenting connected load.
  • Device covers installed, plates flush, then one room shot per wall showing where the devices landed.
  • Torque marks on the main lugs and neutral connections after landing.
  • As-built notes: what changed from the print and why.

Then assemble the package: cover page with address, permit number, scope, crew, and dates; a photo index by area and stage; test results; the change order log; and warranty information. Keep the PDF under about 20 MB so it sends by email, and deliver it within 48 hours.

Naming, filing, and tying photos to change orders

A naming convention only works if it's short enough to type on a phone with gloves on. Use date first, then area, stage, detail, and a sequence number:

No spaces, hyphens between words, three-digit sequence at the end. Sort the folder by name and the photos stay in the order the work happened — which is exactly the order an inspector or a lawyer wants to read them in.

File by job number, with a folder per stage so nobody digs through 400 images to find the panel shots:

  • 1024-Maple-St/01_Pre-Work/
  • 1024-Maple-St/02_Rough-In/
  • 1024-Maple-St/03_Inspections/
  • 1024-Maple-St/04_Change-Orders/
  • 1024-Maple-St/05_Closeout/

For change orders, number the CO before the extra work starts (CO-001, CO-002), then attach a before-and-after photo pair, the material list with quantities, the labor hours for that day, and any redline markup. Get approval in writing — an email reply is fine, a verbal "go ahead" is not. Keep the photos and the change order in the same job record, not in a text thread where they'll be buried by Tuesday.

The one-day rule: a photo taken today gets attached to the job record today. Backfilling a week of images into the right folders is the step where every documentation system dies.

Making it fast enough that crews actually do it

Any process that costs more than a couple of minutes per area gets skipped the first busy week. Design for 60 to 90 seconds per area per stage, and photograph at the end of each task rather than the end of the day, when nobody remembers which box was which.

  • Use the phone that's already in the pocket. Issuing cameras means charging, syncing, and losing them.
  • Turn on auto-upload to the job folder so nobody has to remember to move files.
  • Print a stage checklist of 6 to 10 shots and keep it in the van or taped inside the panel cover.
  • Batch-caption at lunch or in the truck — about 10 minutes covers a full day.
  • Make the photo set a gate: no photos, no job marked complete, no final draw.
  • Give one person ownership of the closeout package so it doesn't become everyone's job.

Keep the quality bar achievable. A slightly blurry photo beats no photo, and a wide shot plus a close-up beats fifty random ones. The first disputed change order you win with a five-month-old photo of an open junction box pays for the whole habit.

Frequently asked questions

How many photos should I take at each stage of an electrical job?

For a residential service call, 10 to 15 pre-work photos and 20 to 30 during and after the repair is plenty. A whole-house rough-in is usually 40 to 60 photos at pre-work and 100 to 200 during rough-in, one set per room. The real test is not the count, it is whether someone who was not on the job can find an answer in under 30 seconds.

What naming convention should I use for electrical job photos?

Lead with the date, then area, stage, detail, and a sequence number: 2025-04-18_Kitchen-Remodel_Rough_JBox-12_014.jpg. No spaces, hyphens between words. Sort the folder by name and the photos stay in the order the work happened.

How do I tie photos to a change order?

Number the change order before the extra work starts, then attach two photos: the condition that triggered it and the finished work. Add the material list with quantities, the labor hours for that day, and any redline markup. Get written approval by email or signature, and keep the photos and the change order in the same job folder instead of a separate text thread.

Do I need to document electrical work that gets covered by drywall?

Yes, that is the most valuable documentation you will take. Once drywall or backfill covers the work, a photo of the open box, the panel interior, the grounding electrode conductor, or the trench is the only evidence of how it was installed. Shoot it before the cover goes on, with a circuit number or a tape measure in frame.

How do I get electricians to actually take the photos?

Keep the standard at 60 to 90 seconds per area, photograph at the end of each task instead of the end of the day, use the phone already in their pocket, and turn on auto-upload so nothing has to be synced by hand. Then make the photo set part of the job checklist: no photo set, no job marked complete, no final draw.
