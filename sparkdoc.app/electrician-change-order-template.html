Electrician change order template: the fields that hold up

A change order is the only document that turns "we found something" into money you can actually collect. If it is missing a trigger, a scope delta, a price, a schedule impact, or a dated signature, you are working on hope and a memory of a phone call.

What Actually Triggers a Change Order

A change order is not a second chance at anything you should have caught during your walkthrough. It is for work that appears after the contract is signed, or that someone else directs you to do.

  • Hidden existing conditions. Knob-and-tube, aluminum branch wiring, a missing grounding electrode conductor, a rusted 100-amp service that now has to become 200-amp, or feeders chewed by rodents behind drywall.
  • Code corrections found at rough-in. No AFCI or GFCI where the current code requires it, box fill past the limit in Article 314.16, cable buried shallower than Article 300.5 allows, missing bonding on gas piping or pool equipment.
  • Owner or designer additions. Extra receptacles, a garage subpanel, an EV charger, a dedicated 240V circuit for a tankless water heater, or recessed lighting that never made it onto the plan.
  • Other trades creating need. The HVAC contractor needs a condenser disconnect, the plumber added a mini-split, or the cabinet layout moved the island receptacle to the floor.
  • Utility or inspector direction. Service relocation, meter height, or working clearance that fails on inspection and has to be rebuilt.
  • Schedule and access changes. After-hours or weekend work, phased occupancy, or a drywall crew that closed a wall before you trimmed it out.

Clarifying what your original proposal already included is an RFI, not a change order. Coming back to fix your own work is a callback. Only new scope, new conditions, or directed extras belong here — and most GC contracts require written notice within 5 to 10 days of discovering the condition, so the clock starts the day you find it.

The Fields Every Electrician Change Order Template Needs

Short forms get paid faster, but they still have to be complete. Every missing field is an argument the other side gets to make.

FieldWhat goes in it
Number and dateSequential change order number, plus the original quote or contract number and date it references. Revisions get a letter suffix.
TriggerWhy this happened: hidden condition, code citation (for example NEC 210.8 or 210.12), owner request, or another trade.
Scope deltaWhat is added and what is deleted, with location: panel, circuit number, room, drawing sheet. "Add one 20A circuit to Panel B, circuit 14, for a garage freezer" — not "add outlet."
ExclusionsWhat is not included: drywall repair, painting, fixture cost, permit fees, trenching, disposal.
PhotosTimestamped photos of the condition before anything gets covered up.
PriceLabor hours × rate, material with markup, permits and inspection fees, equipment rental, subcontractor, tax, subtotal, and the new contract total.
Schedule impactCalendar days added and which milestone moves. If there is none, write "no schedule impact" so it is on the record.
Payment termsDeposit, whether it bills on the next draw, net terms, retainage.
Contract language"All other terms of the agreement remain unchanged," plus how long the quoted price holds.
Signature blockPrinted name, title, date, and an approval sentence that authorizes the work and makes the change order part of the contract.

Money details worth standardizing on every job: 15% to 25% markup on change order labor and material, an administrative minimum of roughly $150 to $350 so a two-hour add is still worth processing, and a written dollar threshold above which every extra becomes a formal change order instead of a handshake.

How to Get the Signature Before the Work Starts

The rule is simple, and it only costs you a job once: no signature, no work. Say it to the homeowner and to the GC superintendent the same way.

Keep the template on your phone or tablet so you can fill it in on site. Point at the panel, show the timestamped photo you just took, then give the three numbers: what changes, what it costs, how many days it adds. Ask for the approval while you are standing in the room with the person who controls the money. That conversation takes five minutes; chasing a signature for three weeks takes a lot longer and usually ends in a discount.

When you genuinely cannot price the work yet — you need to open a wall or trace a circuit — issue a time-and-material work authorization instead. Cap it in writing: "Authorized up to 8 hours at $X per hour plus material at cost plus 20%, not to exceed $1,800." That gets you moving legally, and you replace it with a priced change order within 24 hours.

Then protect the paper trail. Photograph the signed page, email a copy to the signer the same day, and file it against the job. If approval comes by email, save the full message with headers, not a cropped screenshot. If the person who signed is a field superintendent rather than the project manager, copy the PM on your confirmation email so nobody can claim they never saw it.

The Mistakes That Make a Change Order Unenforceable

  • No signature, or the wrong signature. A superintendent who cannot commit funds is not an approver. Confirm at contract stage who signs for money.
  • A verbal go-ahead with nothing in writing. If your contract has a written-modification clause, a phone call changes nothing.
  • Fuzzy scope. "Repair wiring as needed" has no measurable finish line and no defensible price.
  • Price left as TBD or "prevailing rates" with no rates attached to the document.
  • No reference to the original contract. A change order floating on its own is easy to dispute later.
  • Starting work before approval. Once the circuit is run and the wall is closed, you are negotiating from behind.
  • Missing the notice window. Blowing a 5-to-10-day notice requirement can waive the claim entirely.
  • No photos of concealed conditions. If you covered up the knob-and-tube without documenting it, your claim rests on your word alone.
  • Losing track of the cumulative change. Ten small adds that total 40% of the contract invite a constructive-change argument. Keep a running change log with a total.
  • Never updating the panel schedule or as-builts. Six months later, nobody can tell what was installed or when.

A Ten-Minute Routine on Every Job

  1. Photograph the condition before you touch anything, with something recognizable in the frame for location.
  2. Write the scope delta in one sentence a non-electrician can read.
  3. Price it off your standard rate sheet — do not guess in the driveway.
  4. State the schedule impact, even when it is zero.
  5. Get the signature on the spot, then email a copy before you leave the property.
  6. Log it against the job so the change total stays visible and the final invoice matches the paperwork.

That is the whole difference between getting paid for extras and eating them. The template does not have to be long — it has to be complete, signed, dated, and stored somewhere you can find it the day the invoice is questioned.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does an electrician change order have to be signed to be enforceable?

In most cases, yes. A written change order signed by someone with authority to approve money is the strongest evidence that both sides agreed to a new scope and price. A verbal approval or a text message can still be argued, but if your contract has a written-modification clause, an unsigned change order is much harder to collect on.

What is the difference between a change order and a work authorization?

A work authorization is a limited, same-day approval to proceed on time and material when the scope cannot be priced yet, usually capped at a dollar amount or a set number of hours. A change order is the priced document that replaces it once the scope is known, normally within a day or two.

How much markup should an electrician charge on a change order?

Most electrical contractors use 15% to 25% markup on change order labor and material, plus an administrative minimum of roughly $150 to $350 so small adds are still worth processing. That covers re-mobilizing, small-quantity ordering, rescheduling, and the paperwork.

How quickly do I need to submit a change order?

Check the notice provision in your contract first, because many GC agreements require written notice within 5 to 10 days of discovering the condition. As a working rule, submit the change order within 24 to 48 hours and never start the added work before it is approved.

Can an email or text message approve a change order?

It can, if the message clearly states the scope, the price, and the approval. A reply reading 'approved' on an email that contains your priced change order is strong evidence. A vague 'go ahead' in an unrelated thread is not. Save the entire thread and attach it to the job file.
