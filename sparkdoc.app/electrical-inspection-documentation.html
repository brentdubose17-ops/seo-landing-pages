Electrical Inspection Documentation That Survives Review

A signed inspection card is a receipt. The record is everything that lets a stranger reconstruct what you installed, when, and to which code — two years after the drywall went up.

What an electrical inspection record actually has to contain

Most contractors treat documentation as the paperwork that comes back from the inspector. That is the last page of it. The record is the whole file, and the test is simple: could a third party who has never been on site read this folder and know exactly what you did?

A complete record for a residential or light commercial job carries these items:

  • Permit number, jurisdiction, and the code edition in force on the inspection date. The 2020 NEC and the 2023 NEC give different answers on GFCI scope, emergency disconnects, and exterior receptacle requirements. Write down which one your AHJ had adopted.
  • Contractor license number and the master electrician of record for the permit.
  • Approved plan set with the revision number, plus the fixture schedule and any engineer's stamped sheets.
  • Load calculation for the service and for any subpanel you added or re-fed.
  • Scope-of-work narrative in plain language: what was new, what was replaced, what was reused.
  • Panel schedules that match what is physically in the panel on the day you finish.
  • A date-stamped photo log tied to room and circuit, not just a camera roll.
  • Correction log and re-inspection results, including failed items and what fixed them.
  • Test results: continuity, insulation resistance, grounding electrode conductor resistance, and torque values where the manufacturer specifies them.
  • Submittal cut sheets for the fixtures and gear actually installed — not what was originally specified.
Rule of thumb: if an item arrives at closeout as a verbal claim rather than a dated document, assume it will be challenged. A single photo with a timestamp ends the argument.

What inspectors and third-party reviewers look for

A municipal inspector works from a mental checklist shaped by a handful of Articles: 210 for branch circuits, 250 for grounding and bonding, 300 and 334 for wiring methods, 314 for boxes, 408 for panelboards. In practice, the same items come up over and over:

  • Cable support within 12 in. of a box and at intervals not exceeding 4.5 ft (334.30), and holes drilled through framing at the center of the stud face.
  • Nail plates wherever cable sits within 1-1/4 in. of the framing face.
  • Box fill checked against 314.16, especially on jobs where you pulled one more circuit into a crowded device box.
  • Working clearance at the panel: 30 in. wide, 36 in. deep, 6 ft 6 in. of headroom, nothing stored in front of it (110.26).
  • Bonding bushings, bonding jumpers across concentric knockouts, and a properly sized grounding electrode conductor.
  • AFCI protection on the branch circuits that require it and GFCI protection in bathrooms, garages, outdoors, kitchens, laundry, and within 6 ft of sinks.
  • Two 20A small-appliance circuits in the kitchen, a dedicated laundry circuit, and a legible panel directory.

Third-party reviewers are a different animal. An owner's rep, an insurer, or a forensic engineer hired after a fire is not checking your torque wrench habits. They are checking whether the paper trail is internally consistent. Does the photo date match the inspection date? Does the panel schedule match the breakers in the panel? Was the correction actually completed, or was it just marked done? Inconsistency in the file is what turns a warranty claim into a liability claim.

The pre-work and rough-in photo set

Your photo log does two jobs: it proves conditions you inherited, and it proves work that will be buried. Shoot them differently.

Pre-work: 10 to 20 photos on the first visit

  • The existing panel interior before you touch it — cover off, breakers labelled as found.
  • Service size, meter base, mast, and the grounding electrode conductor at the rod or water pipe.
  • Existing damage: missing covers, scorched terminations, undersized conductors, cut grounds, double-tapped breakers.
  • Water heater bonding, gas piping bonding, and any existing aluminum branch wiring.
  • Trench depth before backfill, slab and footing conditions before pour, and any conduit you are covering up.

Rough-in: 150 to 300 photos on a typical house

Every box in place before drywall, every home run, the panel with conductors dressed and identified, and every concealed splice or transition. Shoot wide first so the reviewer can orient, then close enough to read the cable jacket printing and count the conductors. Put something for scale in the frame when it matters — a tape measure on support spacing, your hand next to a box.

Name the file with the room and circuit: kitchen-north-wall-SA1-box-photo.jpg beats IMG_4471.JPG every time. On a commercial job, add the panel name and circuit number so a reviewer can walk straight from the schedule to the photo.

Recording corrections and re-inspections

Write the inspector's comment down word for word. Paraphrasing is how a clear red tag turns into a second failed inspection, because you fix something adjacent to what was actually written.

ItemInspector comment (verbatim)Location / fixRe-inspection
1 "Cable not protected where passing through top plate." Garage north wall, two runs. Steel nail plates added 3/12; photo ref C-01. Re-inspect 3/18 — PASS
2 "Panel directory missing circuit assignments." Main 200A panel. Typed schedule printed and installed in door 3/13; photo C-02. Re-inspect 3/18 — PASS
3 "Verify GFCI protection at garage receptacles." Garage circuit 14. GFCI receptacle installed 3/13, tested and reset; photo C-03. Re-inspect 3/18 — PASS

Keep a photo of the fix attached to every line. Then close the loop: record the re-inspection date and the result in the same row, not on a separate sheet. Many jurisdictions charge a re-inspection fee in the $50 to $150 range per trip, and a partial fix can earn you a repeat fail or a stop-work order. Walk the list, verify each item with your own eyes and a camera, and only then call it in.

Labelling, panel schedules, and circuit identification

Panel labelling is the single most common punch-list item on electrical closeouts, and it is entirely preventable. A typed schedule beats a handwritten one because it can be updated, re-printed, and handed over as a document.

Every line of the schedule should carry: circuit number, breaker size, breaker type (standard, GFCI, AFCI, dual-function), conductor size and type, the description of what it feeds, and its location. Mark unused spaces as SPARE rather than leaving them blank — a blank space reads as an oversight.

On the physical panel, number circuits consistently — odd on the left, even on the right — and update the printed schedule after every change order. Mark conductors at both ends so the next electrician can identify a feed without tracing it. Use the standard colour code, phase tape at 277/480V, and lock-off devices for PV, generator, or transfer-switch feeds. Anything that stays energized from another source gets a permanent "do not energize" tag with the source named. Update the schedule the same week you change the panel, not at closeout — that is where most packages fall apart.

Handing a clean closeout package to the GC or owner

The closeout package is what releases retainage, starts the warranty clock, and shifts liability. Build it once, in order, and hand it over at final walk rather than waiting on the invoice.

  1. Cover sheet: project name, address, permit number, license and master electrician, date range of work, and a one-line scope summary.
  2. Signed inspection card and all green tags, scanned flat.
  3. Correction log with before-and-after photos for anything that was ever red-tagged.
  4. Panel schedules, typed and current, one page per panel.
  5. Photo log PDF organized by area and circuit, with a table of contents.
  6. Test results and torque documentation for service and feeder terminations.
  7. Fixture and gear cut sheets for what was actually installed, plus warranty letters.
  8. As-built markups on the plan set: what moved, what was added by change order.
  9. Spares and O&M notes for the owner: breaker types, filter or lamp part numbers, and who to call.

Deliver one file, not a folder of loose scans. Name it ProjectName_Address_Electrical_Closeout_YYYY-MM-DD.pdf and keep it under about 50 MB so it emails without a link that expires. Index it at the front with page numbers so a superintendent can find the correction log in ten seconds. Keep your originals; send the copy.

When a fixture fails 18 months later and someone questions who installed it, the closeout package is the answer. It is also the fastest way to get paid on the last draw.

Frequently asked questions

What has to be in an electrical inspection documentation package?
At minimum: the permit number, the code edition your AHJ had adopted, the approved plan revision, your license and master electrician of record, the signed inspection card, the correction log, a date-stamped photo log, panel schedules, load calculations, and any test results. If it is not in the packet, it did not happen as far as a reviewer is concerned.
How many photos should a rough-in photo log have?
For a typical 2,500 sq ft house, plan on 150 to 300 photos. The rule of thumb is one wide shot plus two or three detail shots for every box, panel, and concealed run before drywall goes up. Extra photos cost you nothing; opening drywall to prove a staple was installed costs you a day.
What do inspectors look for that electricians most often miss?
Nail plates where cable sits within 1-1/4 in. of a framing face, cable support within 12 in. of a box and every 4.5 ft after that, a legible panel directory, working clearance at the panel, bonding bushings and jumpers, and AFCI and GFCI protection in the right rooms. Most failed rough-ins trace back to support, physical protection, and paperwork rather than wiring method.
How should corrections and re-inspections be documented?
Copy the inspector's comment word for word into a correction log, then add the location, the fix you made, who made it, the date, and a reference photo of the corrected work. Log the re-inspection date and result in the same row. Never schedule a re-inspection until every item on the list is finished and photographed.
What goes in an electrical closeout package for a general contractor?
A cover sheet with project name, address, permit number, license number, and date range; the signed inspection card; the correction log with photos; current panel schedules; test results; fixture cut sheets; as-built markups; warranty letters; and a labelled photo log. Deliver it as one indexed PDF named with the project and date, not as a folder of loose files.

Build the inspection record while the walls are still open — not the night before closeout.
