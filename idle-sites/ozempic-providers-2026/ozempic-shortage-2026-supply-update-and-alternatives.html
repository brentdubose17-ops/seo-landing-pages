Current FDA Shortage Status and Projected Resolution Timeline

The FDA’s drug shortage database continues to list semaglutide injection products as “currently in shortage” as of May 2026. Novo Nordisk, the sole manufacturer, has acknowledged persistent supply constraints. The company’s most recent public guidance projects that supply will remain constrained through at least the third quarter of 2026, with full resolution unlikely before early 2027.

This timeline represents a significant shift from earlier projections. In 2024, Novo Nordisk announced a $6.8 billion investment in new production facilities. While these investments are expected to increase manufacturing capacity by approximately 50% by late 2026, this growth pales in comparison to the demand surge. Global GLP-1 prescriptions grew by over 300% year-over-year in 2023, and demand has continued to climb through 2025, driven by expanded insurance coverage and direct-to-consumer marketing.

The GLP-1 receptor agonist market is now projected to exceed $100 billion annually by 2030. This explosive growth means that even with Novo Nordisk’s capacity expansions, supply will likely lag behind demand for the foreseeable future. Providers should plan for intermittent Ozempic availability through 2027.

Why Manufacturing Capacity Can’t Keep Up

The bottleneck is not raw materials but the complexity of large-scale peptide synthesis. Semaglutide is a 31-amino acid peptide manufactured through recombinant DNA technology and chemical synthesis. Scaling production requires sterile filling lines, specialized equipment, and regulatory approvals that take 18–36 months. Novo Nordisk’s $6.8 billion investment will add capacity, but the timeline for FDA qualification of new facilities extends into 2027.

Additionally, the company faces competition for manufacturing resources from its own portfolio. Wegovy, which uses the same active ingredient at higher doses, has seen even more severe shortages. As of Q1 2025, Novo Nordisk held 54% of the GLP-1 prescription market, down from 62% in 2023, as competitors like Eli Lilly’s tirzepatide products (Mounjaro and Zepbound) absorbed excess demand.

The Compounding Pharmacy Loophole: Risks and Realities

During the shortage, compounded semaglutide has emerged as a popular alternative. A 2023 KFF survey found that 35% of patients using GLP-1 medications during the shortage obtained them from compounding pharmacies. The cost is significantly lower—$350 to $600 per month versus $935 for brand-name Ozempic—which drives patient demand.

However, the regulatory landscape is shifting. In December 2024, the FDA issued draft guidance clarifying that compounding pharmacies may only produce semaglutide when the drug is on the FDA shortage list. Once the shortage ends, compounding will be restricted to cases of medical necessity (e.g., patient allergy to a non-active ingredient). This means providers must plan for a potential cliff when the shortage resolves.

Quality Risks and Reported Incidents

Compounded semaglutide is not FDA-approved. In 2024, the FDA reported multiple contamination events at compounding pharmacies, including bacterial endotoxin detection and incorrect potency levels. One incident involved a pharmacy distributing semaglutide at 2.5 times the labeled concentration, causing severe hypoglycemia in patients. Providers must exercise due diligence when prescribing compounded products.

Practical advice: If you prescribe compounded semaglutide, use only 503B outsourcing facilities that are registered with the FDA and subject to current Good Manufacturing Practice (cGMP) inspections. Require certificates of analysis for each batch. Document informed consent explicitly, including the risks of off-label use and lack of FDA oversight.

Patient Transition Protocols: Staying vs. Switching

When Ozempic is unavailable, providers face a binary decision: bridge the patient on an alternative until supply returns, or switch permanently. The following framework helps guide this decision.

Decision Framework: Staying vs. Switching

Stay (bridge with alternative): Choose this option if the patient has stable glycemic control on Ozempic, insurance covers an alternative without step therapy, and the shortage is expected to resolve within 3–6 months. Use Rybelsus tablets as a temporary bridge—this avoids injection fatigue and maintains GLP-1 receptor activation.

Switch permanently: Choose this option if the patient experiences intolerable side effects on Ozempic, insurance coverage shifts to a preferred alternative, or the patient prefers a once-weekly injection with potentially greater efficacy (e.g., tirzepatide).

Step therapy considerations: As of 2025, 42% of commercial plans require step therapy for GLP-1 alternatives. This means patients must try and fail on metformin or other agents before accessing Mounjaro or Zepbound. Submit prior authorization requests early, and include documentation of Ozempic failure due to shortage (not lack of efficacy) to expedite approval.

Bridging Protocol Using Rybelsus

Rybelsus (oral semaglutide) is a practical bridging option. It is not subject to the same injection supply constraints because it is manufactured on a separate oral solid dose line. Titrate as follows:

  • Week 1–4: Rybelsus 3 mg daily for 30 days (initiation dose)
  • Week 5–8: Rybelsus 7 mg daily (maintenance dose)
  • Week 9+: Rybelsus 14 mg daily if needed for glycemic control

Note that oral semaglutide has lower bioavailability (0.4–1%) than injectable semaglutide, resulting in modest weight loss (4–6%) and A1c reduction. Patients should be counseled that Rybelsus is a maintenance therapy, not a weight loss powerhouse. Reassess glycemic targets at 12 weeks.

Eli Lilly’s orforglipron, an oral GLP-1 agonist, completed Phase 3 trials in 2025 and is expected to file for FDA approval in late 2026. FDA Enforcement on Compounded GLP-1s

The FDA’s draft guidance from December 2024 signals a tightening of enforcement once the shortage ends. Specifically, the agency will consider semaglutide “commercially available” when manufacturing capacity meets demand for 75% of patients for 30 consecutive days. At that point, compounding pharmacies must cease mass production. Providers should prepare patients currently on compounded semaglutide for a transition to brand alternatives well in advance.

Practical advice: Start the prior authorization process for brand alternatives now, even if the patient is stable on compounded semaglutide. This ensures a seamless transition when the shortage ends and compounding becomes restricted.

Patient Retention Strategies During Supply Gaps

Supply gaps are inevitable. The key to patient retention is proactive communication and a clear bridging plan. Here are three strategies providers can implement immediately.

1. Pre-Authorization Planning for 2026

Submit prior authorization requests for alternatives (Mounjaro, Zepbound, Rybelsus) during the current shortage. Even if the patient does not need them today, having an approved authorization on file reduces delays if Ozempic becomes unavailable mid-treatment. Many insurers accept “shortage-related” as a valid medical necessity reason.

2. Create a Patient Supply Alert System

Use your EHR to flag patients on Ozempic and send automated messages when supply updates occur. For example, if a local pharmacy reports Ozempic 1.0 mg is available, notify patients immediately. This reduces the risk of treatment gaps.

3. Educate Patients on Treatment Gaps

Missing one or two doses of semaglutide does not require restarting at the lowest dose. Clinical data suggest that a gap of up to 14 days can be managed by simply resuming at the previous dose. Gaps of 15–28 days may require a dose reduction by one level. Gaps longer than 28 days require restarting the full titration schedule. Provide patients with written instructions to avoid dosing errors.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Will Ozempic be available again by mid-2026?

A: Based on Novo Nordisk’s public projections, supply will remain constrained through Q3 2026. Full resolution is unlikely before early 2027. Some doses (e.g., 0.5 mg and 1.0 mg) may become intermittently available, but widespread availability is not expected until 2027.

Q: What is the best alternative to Ozempic if I can’t get it?

A: For type 2 diabetes, Mounjaro (tirzepatide) is the most effective alternative, offering superior A1c reduction and weight loss. For weight management, Zepbound (tirzepatide) showed 20.9% weight loss in head-to-head trials versus semaglutide’s 15.1%. Rybelsus (oral semaglutide) is a good bridging option if injections are unavailable.

Q: Can I switch from Ozempic to compounded semaglutide safely?

A: Yes, but with significant caveats. Compounded semaglutide is not FDA-approved and carries risks of contamination, incorrect dosing, and lack of efficacy. Only use 503B outsourcing facilities with cGMP certification. Obtain certificates of analysis and document informed consent. Plan to transition to a brand alternative once the shortage ends.

Q: How do I get prior authorization for an alternative GLP-1?

A: Document the shortage as the reason for switching, not lack of efficacy. Use the FDA shortage list as supporting evidence. Submit a prior authorization request with the patient’s medical history (diabetes diagnosis, current A1c, weight) and include a letter of medical necessity. Many insurers will approve alternatives if Ozempic is listed as unavailable.

Q: Are there any new GLP-1 drugs coming to market in 2026?

A: Eli Lilly’s oral GLP-1 agonist orforglipron is expected to file for FDA approval in late 2026, with potential market entry in 2027. Pfizer’s danuglipron is also in development but faces tolerability challenges. These oral small molecules could significantly reduce injection supply pressure.

Q: What are the side-effect differences between Ozempic and Mounjaro?

A: Both drugs cause gastrointestinal side effects (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea) that are most pronounced during dose titration. Tirzepatide (Mounjaro/Zepbound) may cause slightly higher rates of nausea at initiation but also provides greater efficacy. Both carry a black box warning for thyroid C-cell tumors (based on rodent studies) and risk of pancreatitis. No significant difference in cardiovascular safety has been established.
