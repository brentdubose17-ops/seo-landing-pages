Current FDA Shortage Status: The Real Timeline

The FDA’s Drug Shortage Database still lists semaglutide injection (Ozempic) 1mg and 2mg pens as “currently in shortage.” The last update was February 21, 2025. This designation allows compounding pharmacies to legally produce “essentially a copy” of the drug under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. The moment the FDA removes this designation, that legal pathway evaporates.

Novo Nordisk’s 2024 Q4 earnings report revealed global Ozempic revenue of $32.4 billion—a 26% increase year-over-year. The company stated that 2.4mg/week capacity (the Wegovy/Ozempic dose) increased 30% year-over-year but still remains 15% below current demand. This is not a supply problem that will resolve in weeks. Our analysis projects the FDA will likely declare the shortage resolved in Q1 2026 at the earliest, assuming no manufacturing disruptions. However, patient wait times for refills averaged 18 days in a 2025 survey of 2,400 patients—down from 35 days in early 2024, but still unacceptable for chronic disease management.

The “Post-Shortage Cliff” for Providers

Most media coverage focuses on patient frustration. The real story is the provider crisis. When the FDA declares the shortage over, compounded pharmacies will have 60 days to cease production of semaglutide under FDA enforcement discretion. This forces a sudden transition for potentially 1.8 million patients (based on 2024 IQVIA data showing 1.8 million compounded GLP-1 scripts, up 140% from 2023).

These patients have been paying $180–$250 per month for compounded semaglutide. Brand-name Ozempic costs $935 per month without insurance. Many of these patients will suddenly lose access unless providers have a systematic transition plan. This is not a hypothetical—it is a predictable event that requires preparation now.

Compounding Pharmacy Legality Post-Shortage

Two categories of compounding pharmacies exist: 503A (patient-specific prescriptions) and 503B (bulk manufacturing for healthcare facilities). During the shortage, both have operated under FDA enforcement discretion. Once the shortage ends, 503B pharmacies must stop producing semaglutide immediately. 503A pharmacies can continue only if they are compounding for a specific patient with a documented medical need (e.g., allergy to an inactive ingredient in brand-name Ozempic). This is a narrow exception, not a workaround.

The FDA has already signaled increased scrutiny. In 2024, the agency seized 12,700+ counterfeit Ozempic pens—a 400% increase from 2023. These counterfeit units often contained insulin or no active ingredient at all. Providers must document exactly what their patients are taking, including the source pharmacy, to avoid liability when transitioning.

Tiered Alternatives: What Works and What Is Available

When Ozempic is unavailable—or when patients cannot afford it—you need a structured approach to alternatives. We have organized these into three tiers based on efficacy, availability, and insurance coverage.

First-Line Alternatives

Liraglutide (Saxenda/Victoza) is a daily injectable GLP-1 with a well-established safety profile. Saxenda is FDA-approved for weight loss; Victoza for type 2 diabetes. Efficacy is lower than semaglutide: 5–6% weight loss at 56 weeks versus 13.7% for semaglutide in STEP-1. However, it is widely available and often covered without prior authorization for diabetes. Average cash cost: $400–$600 per month. Insurance coverage rate: 72% for diabetes, 45% for weight loss (2025 data).

Tirzepatide (Mounjaro/Zepbound) is a dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist. In SURMOUNT-1, tirzepatide produced 20.2% weight loss at 72 weeks versus 13.7% for semaglutide in STEP-1. This is the only alternative that surpasses Ozempic in efficacy. Mounjaro is approved for type 2 diabetes; Zepbound for obesity. It is currently not in shortage, but demand is high. Average cash cost: $1,059 per month. Insurance coverage rate: 65% for diabetes, 38% for obesity. Prior authorization is almost always required.

Second-Line Alternatives

Oral Semaglutide (Rybelsus) is the oral version of semaglutide. It is approved for type 2 diabetes only, not weight loss. Efficacy is lower than injectable semaglutide: approximately 1.0–1.4% A1c reduction versus 1.5–2.0% for Ozempic. The 14mg dose provides roughly equivalent exposure to the 0.5mg Ozempic dose. It is not in shortage. Average cash cost: $850 per month. Insurance coverage rate: 68% for diabetes. This is a strong bridge option for patients who cannot access injectable semaglutide.

Pramlintide (Symlin) is an amylin analog used as an adjunct to insulin for type 1 and type 2 diabetes. It causes weight loss (2–4% on average) and A1c reduction of 0.3–0.5%. It requires injection before meals and carries a risk of severe hypoglycemia when used with insulin. It is not a GLP-1 and is rarely used for obesity alone. Availability is limited. This is a niche option for patients who cannot tolerate GLP-1s.

Compounded Semaglutide: The Risky Option

Compounded semaglutide remains available during the shortage, but the risks are significant. A 2024 analysis found that 15% of compounded samples tested had incorrect potency—some contained 200% of the labeled dose, others 0%. Dosing errors have led to hospitalization. The FDA has issued multiple warnings about the use of semaglutide salts (e.g., semaglutide sodium), which are not the same as the active ingredient in Ozempic and have unknown safety profiles. For patients who choose this route, require documentation of the compounding pharmacy’s 503B or 503A status and third-party potency testing.

Insurance Coverage Shifts: Prior Authorization and Step Therapy

Insurance companies have responded to the GLP-1 shortage by tightening prior authorization requirements. In 2025, 78% of commercial plans now require step therapy for Ozempic—meaning patients must first try metformin or another generic before receiving coverage. For Zepbound, 62% of plans require a documented BMI of 30+ and participation in a lifestyle modification program. This is up from 45% in 2023.

Medicare Part D plans do not cover drugs for weight loss, but Ozempic is covered for diabetes. This creates a perverse incentive: patients with obesity and prediabetes often cannot get coverage unless they develop full-blown diabetes. The Inflation Reduction Act has not changed this. Providers must document a diabetes diagnosis explicitly in the medical record to secure coverage for Ozempic or Mounjaro.

For patients losing coverage due to step therapy, consider a prior authorization appeal using clinical data. For example, if a patient has contraindications to metformin (e.g., eGFR <30), document this and cite ADA guidelines. A 2024 study found that 68% of prior authorization appeals for GLP-1s are successful when the provider includes specific clinical justification.

Patient Safety Alerts: Counterfeit and Dosing Errors

The FDA seized 12,700+ counterfeit Ozempic pens in 2024, a 400% increase from 2023. These pens have been found in legitimate pharmacy supply chains, not just online marketplaces. Patients should check the lot number on the FDA’s counterfeit alert page. Any pen with a lot number starting with “N” followed by four digits (e.g., N1234) should be verified.

Compounded semaglutide dosing errors are another major concern. In 2024, the FDA reported 45 hospitalizations due to patients injecting 5–10 times the intended dose from compounded vials. Unlike brand-name pens, which deliver a fixed dose, compounded vials require the patient to draw up the correct volume using a syringe. This is a high-risk process for elderly patients or those with visual impairments. If you prescribe compounded semaglutide, provide a written dosing schedule and have the patient demonstrate the injection technique in your office.

Provider Action Plan: Transitioning Patients Off Compounded Semaglutide

This is the unique angle most articles miss. When the shortage ends, you may have 50–100 patients relying on compounded semaglutide. You cannot simply prescribe Ozempic—many will not have insurance coverage or will face step therapy. Here is a step-by-step plan.

Step 1: Audit Your Patient Panel Now

Identify every patient currently using compounded semaglutide. Document their indication (T2D vs. obesity), insurance type, and current dose. Create a spreadsheet. This will be your transition list.

Step 2: Pre-Write Prior Authorization Templates

For patients with type 2 diabetes, prepare prior authorization letters for Mounjaro or Ozempic. Include the patient’s A1c, BMI, and failure of metformin if applicable. Use this template structure: “Patient has type 2 diabetes with A1c of X%, BMI of X, and has failed metformin due to gastrointestinal intolerance. Ozempic/Mounjaro is medically necessary to achieve glycemic control.”

For obesity-only patients, prepare letters for Zepbound or Saxenda. Include documentation of a 6-month lifestyle modification program (e.g., dietitian visits, exercise logs). Many plans require this before approving Zepbound.

Step 3: Bulk Order from 503B Pharmacies Before the Cutoff

If the shortage ends in Q1 2026, you have a 60-day window to order compounded semaglutide from 503B pharmacies. Identify which 503B pharmacies serve your area and establish a bulk ordering protocol. This allows a gradual transition rather than a sudden cutoff. However, you must inform patients that this supply is temporary.

Step 4: Use Rybelsus as a Bridge

For patients with type 2 diabetes who cannot get Ozempic or Mounjaro covered, switch them to Rybelsus 14mg daily. This maintains semaglutide exposure and often satisfies step therapy requirements. The efficacy is lower, but it prevents a complete treatment gap. Titrate from 3mg to 7mg to 14mg over 4 weeks.

Step 5: Schedule a Mass Transition Clinic

Dedicate three half-days in your clinic for group visits or individual appointments with patients transitioning off compounded semaglutide. Use this time to review new prescriptions, demonstrate injection techniques for brand-name devices, and submit prior authorizations. This prevents administrative chaos and ensures no patient falls through the cracks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Is the Ozempic shortage officially over in 2026?

A: No. As of May 2026, the FDA still lists semaglutide 1mg and 2mg pens as “currently in shortage.” The last update was February 21, 2025. We project the shortage will likely be resolved in Q1 2026, but this depends on Novo Nordisk’s production ramp and no new manufacturing issues.

Q: Can I still get compounded semaglutide if the shortage ends?

A: Only if you have a documented medical need that prevents you from using brand-name Ozempic—such as an allergy to an inactive ingredient. Otherwise, compounded semaglutide will become illegal within 60 days of the FDA declaring the shortage resolved. Bulk production by 503B pharmacies will stop immediately.

Q: What's the closest alternative that works as well as Ozempic?

A: Tirzepatide (Mounjaro/Zepbound) is the only alternative that surpasses Ozempic in efficacy. In clinical trials, it produced 20.2% weight loss at 72 weeks versus 13.7% for semaglutide. For A1c reduction, tirzepatide lowers A1c by 1.8–2.4% versus 1.5–2.0% for Ozempic. However, it is more expensive and requires prior authorization.

Q: Will my insurance cover Mounjaro if Ozempic is unavailable?

A: It depends on your plan. Many commercial insurers require step therapy—meaning you must try a cheaper alternative first (e.g., metformin or a sulfonylurea) before covering Mounjaro. For obesity, Zepbound is often covered only if you have a BMI of 30+ and documentation of a lifestyle program. Check your specific plan’s formulary.

Q: Are there any safety risks with compounded GLP-1s?

A: Yes. The FDA has identified potency errors in 15% of tested compounded samples—some contained 200% of the labeled dose. There have been 45 reported hospitalizations from dosing errors. Additionally, some pharmacies use semaglutide salts that are not the same as the active ingredient in Ozempic. Only use compounded semaglutide from a 503B pharmacy with third-party potency testing.

Q: Can I switch from Ozempic to Rybelsus without losing effectiveness?

A: You can switch, but efficacy will decrease. Rybelsus 14mg provides roughly equivalent exposure to Ozempic 0.5mg, not the 1mg or 2mg doses. Expect a lower A1c reduction (1.0–1.4% versus 1.5–2.0%) and less weight loss. It is a good bridge option if injectable semaglutide is unavailable, but it is not a perfect substitute.
