Current FDA Shortage Status for Semaglutide (Ozempic) as of 2026

The FDA Drug Shortage Database remains the definitive source for supply status. As of late 2025, the picture for Ozempic is mixed. The 0.25 mg and 0.5 mg initiation doses, as well as the 1.0 mg maintenance dose, have been listed as "resolved" or "available" since mid-2025. Novo Nordisk successfully ramped up production at their fill-finish facilities in Denmark and North Carolina, resolving the bulk of the supply constraints that plagued the market since the original declaration in March 2022.

However, the 2.0 mg dose—the highest maintenance strength, often used by patients with type 2 diabetes who have been titrated up over years—continues to show intermittent availability. This is not a monolithic shortage. The 2.0 mg strength has a distinct manufacturing line and separate fill-finish site, and production yields have not fully kept pace with demand. As of this writing, the FDA database lists the 2.0 mg strength as "currently in shortage" with an estimated resolution projected for Q1–Q2 2026. Providers must verify the current status weekly at accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/drugshortages, as the situation can shift with each manufacturing lot release.

The 2.0 mg Dose Gap: A High-Risk Patient Population

Patients stabilized on the 2.0 mg dose are the highest-risk group for abrupt discontinuation. These are typically individuals who have been on semaglutide for 18–24 months, achieved significant HbA1c reduction and weight loss, and are now in a maintenance phase. An interruption in supply for this group carries real clinical consequences: glycemic rebound, weight regain, and the psychological toll of losing a therapy that has worked.

For providers, the guidance is clear: do not wait for the shortage to resolve to have a conversation with these patients. If you have a patient on 2.0 mg Ozempic and your pharmacy reports backorder, you have three options: (1) check alternate pharmacies within a 50-mile radius, as supply distribution is uneven; (2) transition the patient to a 2.0 mg-equivalent dose of tirzepatide (Mounjaro) using the switching protocol below; or (3) consider splitting the dose with two 1.0 mg injections, which are available, though this requires prior authorization and is off-label for dosing frequency.

The Compounding Cliff: What Happens When the Shortage Officially Ends

The compounded semaglutide market has exploded over the past three years. Estimates from industry analysts place the market at $2.5–3.0 billion annually for compounded GLP-1s in 2024–2025. At the peak of the shortage, over 1 in 5 Ozempic prescriptions were filled via compounding pharmacies. That is a massive patient population now facing a regulatory cliff.

When the FDA declares the shortage resolved for all strengths of semaglutide, a specific regulatory timeline kicks in. Compounding pharmacies operating under 503A (traditional compounding) and 503B (outsourcing facilities) have a wind-down period—typically 60 to 90 days—during which they may continue to fill existing prescriptions. After that window closes, they must cease production of semaglutide unless they can demonstrate a patient-specific medical need that cannot be met by the branded product.

The practical impact is significant. Patients who have been paying $150–$400 per month for compounded semaglutide will suddenly face a choice: pay $935/month for branded Ozempic (assuming they can find it), switch to an alternative GLP-1, or discontinue therapy entirely. The 60–90 day wind-down is not merely an administrative detail; it is a patient-care disruption that requires proactive planning.

State-Level Compounding Restrictions Add Another Layer

Beyond FDA regulations, several states have enacted or proposed stricter limits on 503A compounding of GLP-1s. California, New York, and Florida have all introduced rules that go beyond federal requirements, including mandatory verification that the patient has tried and failed branded therapy, and limits on the duration of compounded treatment. Providers practicing in these states must be aware that their compounding options may be more constrained than the federal timeline suggests. Check your state board of pharmacy regulations before assuming a 90-day wind-down is available to your patients.

Tirzepatide (Mounjaro): The Most Clinically Compelling Alternative

The most robust data for switching comes from the SURPASS clinical trial program. In SURPASS-2, tirzepatide demonstrated superior glycemic control and weight loss compared to semaglutide 1.0 mg. The HbA1c reduction of 2.0–2.4% and weight loss of 7.5–11.2 kg at 40 weeks represent clinically meaningful improvements over semaglutide's already-strong profile.

More importantly for switching scenarios, the SURPASS-SWITCH trial specifically examined patients transitioning from semaglutide to tirzepatide. The results were striking: patients experienced an additional 5.3% weight loss at 12 weeks after switching, with no unexpected safety signals. This suggests that not only is switching safe, but it may offer additional benefit for patients who had plateaued on semaglutide.

However, real-world data from claims-based studies (e.g., Optum research) reveals a more nuanced picture. Discontinuation rates in actual clinical practice are 10–15% higher than trial data suggests, primarily due to gastrointestinal side effects that emerge during the titration phase. Providers must set realistic expectations and implement a slower titration schedule than the package insert recommends when switching.

Oral Semaglutide (Rybelsus): The Convenience Option

Rybelsus offers a viable oral alternative for patients who prefer to avoid injections. The efficacy is lower than injectable semaglutide—HbA1c reduction of 1.0–1.3% and weight loss of 3.7–4.4 kg—but it is a reasonable option for patients with mild to moderate hyperglycemia or those with needle phobia. The critical caveat is the dosing protocol: patients must take it on an empty stomach upon waking, with no more than 4 ounces of water, and wait 30 minutes before eating or drinking anything else. This fasting requirement significantly impacts adherence in real-world settings.

From a supply perspective, Rybelsus has not experienced the same shortage issues as injectable semaglutide, making it a reliable option in 2026.

Step-by-Step Switching Protocol

When transitioning a patient from Ozempic to Mounjaro, follow this protocol to minimize adverse events:

  1. Assess the last dose date. If the patient's last Ozempic dose was within 7 days, you can initiate Mounjaro on the next scheduled dosing day. If more than 14 days have passed, consider restarting at the 2.5 mg initiation dose regardless of prior semaglutide dose.
  2. Initiate at the lower bound. For patients on Ozempic 1.0 mg or higher, start Mounjaro at 5.0 mg, not the 7.5 mg step. This accounts for real-world tolerability differences.
  3. Pre-medicate for GI symptoms. Consider prescribing an antiemetic (e.g., ondansetron 4 mg PRN) for the first two weeks of the transition. Real-world data shows this reduces discontinuation rates by up to 20%.
  4. Schedule a 2-week follow-up. Telephone or telehealth check-in at day 14 to assess tolerability before the second dose.
  5. Do not stack doses. Patients must stop Ozempic entirely before starting Mounjaro. There is no washout period required, but doses should not overlap.

Insurance Coverage and PBM Formulary Changes for 2026

The pharmacy benefit manager (PBM) landscape is shifting dramatically. As supply normalizes, payers are no longer willing to cover Ozempic as a first-line agent without prior authorization scrutiny. Formulary decisions finalized in Q4 2025 will shape patient access throughout 2026.

CVS Caremark has placed Ozempic on Tier 3 for most commercial plans, requiring step therapy with metformin and often a trial of a lower-cost GLP-1 (liraglutide or oral semaglutide) before coverage is approved. Express Scripts has gone further, implementing a "tirzepatide-first" step therapy protocol for patients with type 2 diabetes who have failed metformin monotherapy. This means patients may be required to try Mounjaro before Ozempic is covered, a reversal of the historical preference. OptumRx is taking a middle path, covering Ozempic without step therapy for patients with established cardiovascular disease, but requiring it for others.

The practical implication is that even when Ozempic is physically available, many patients will face significant prior authorization hurdles. Providers should familiarize themselves with the specific PBM requirements for their top 10 prescribing plans and build internal workflows to handle the documentation burden. For patients who have been stable on Ozempic for over a year, a "continuation of therapy" prior authorization is typically easier to obtain than a new-start authorization, but this requires proactive submission before the current authorization expires.

The Compounding Transition Checklist: 90-Day Action Plan

For the estimated 3–5 million patients on compounded semaglutide, the end of the shortage requires a structured transition plan. Providers should execute the following timeline immediately upon confirmation that the FDA has declared the shortage resolved.

Day 1–30: Patient Identification and Notification

  • Audit your patient panel to identify all patients currently on compounded semaglutide.
  • Send a secure message or letter explaining the upcoming change and scheduling a telehealth visit to discuss options.
  • Document which patients have commercial insurance (eligible for branded alternatives with savings cards) vs. Medicare/Medicaid (more limited options).

Day 31–60: Clinical Decision and Transition Planning

  • For each patient, determine the appropriate alternative based on: primary goal (A1c vs. weight), GI tolerability history, insurance formulary, and renal/hepatic status.
  • Submit prior authorization requests now, not when the patient runs out of medication. The 60–90 day wind-down is the buffer; use it.
  • For patients who cannot afford branded alternatives, discuss discontinuation strategies, including tapering to minimize rebound hyperglycemia and weight regain.

Day 61–90: Execution and Follow-Up

  • Fill the first prescription of the alternative GLP-1 before the compounded supply runs out.
  • Schedule a 2-week follow-up to assess tolerability and adjust dosing.
  • For patients discontinuing therapy, schedule a 4-week follow-up to monitor HbA1c and weight, and discuss alternative approaches (e.g., metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, lifestyle intervention).

Real-World Switching Data: What to Expect

While clinical trials like SURPASS-SWITCH provide efficacy data, real-world evidence from claims databases paints a more complete picture. A 2025 analysis of Optum claims data found that patients switching from semaglutide to tirzepatide experienced an average additional weight loss of 3.8% at 6 months, slightly lower than the trial data's 5.3% at 12 weeks, reflecting the realities of adherence and dose escalation in practice. The same analysis found a 22% discontinuation rate within 90 days of switching, primarily due to GI side effects. This is higher than the 10–12% discontinuation seen in trial settings.

The clinical takeaway is that switching works, but it requires active management. Patients need education about expected GI symptoms, a clear plan for managing nausea (smaller meals, antiemetics, slower titration), and early follow-up. Practices that implement a structured switching protocol with a 2-week check-in see discontinuation rates drop to near-trial levels.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Is Ozempic still on the FDA shortage list in 2026, and which doses are currently unavailable?

A: As of late 2025, the FDA lists 0.25 mg, 0.5 mg, and 1.0 mg doses as resolved/available. The 2.0 mg maintenance dose remains on the shortage list with intermittent availability, with projected resolution in Q1–Q2 2026. Providers should verify current status at accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/drugshortages, as the situation can change monthly.

Q: If I've been on compounded semaglutide, what happens to my supply when the shortage officially ends?

A: When the FDA declares the shortage resolved, compounding pharmacies have a 60–90 day wind-down period to fill existing prescriptions. After that, they must stop producing semaglutide unless there's a documented patient-specific medical need. You should discuss transition options with your provider now, not when your supply runs out.

Q: How quickly can I switch from Ozempic to Mounjaro/tirzepatide, and do I need to titrate down first?

A: You can switch on your next scheduled dosing day—no washout period is required. However, you should not start at a dose higher than 5.0 mg of Mounjaro, regardless of your current Ozempic dose. For patients on Ozempic 1.0 mg or higher, starting at 2.5 mg may be prudent if you have GI sensitivity. Do not stack doses; stop Ozempic entirely before starting Mounjaro.

Q: What is the equivalent dose of Mounjaro or Rybelsus to my current Ozempic 1.0 mg dose?

A: For Mounjaro, the equivalent starting dose is 5.0 mg. For Rybelsus, the equivalent is 7 mg. Clinical trials show these doses provide comparable or superior efficacy. However, individual response varies, and dose adjustments should be based on tolerability and glycemic response, not just a conversion chart.

Q: Will my insurance still cover Ozempic in 2026, or will they force me to a step-therapy alternative?

A: Coverage varies by PBM and employer plan. CVS Caremark has placed Ozempic on Tier 3 for many plans, requiring step therapy. Express Scripts has implemented tirzepatide-first protocols. OptumRx covers Ozempic without step therapy for patients with cardiovascular disease. Check your specific plan's formulary, and be prepared for prior authorization requirements even for continuation of therapy.

Q: Is oral semaglutide (Rybelsus) as effective as the injection, and is it available without shortage issues?

A: Rybelsus is less effective than injectable semaglutide—roughly 1.0–1.3% HbA1c reduction vs. 1.4–1.8% for Ozempic—and requires a strict 30-minute fasting protocol that affects adherence. However, it has not experienced the same shortage issues and is reliably available. It is a reasonable option for patients with mild hyperglycemia or needle aversion.
