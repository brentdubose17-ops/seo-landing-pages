GLP-1 vs Bariatric Surgery: Weight-Loss Surgeries Are Down 34% — What It Means

Published August 21, 2026By ABD Legacy LLC

Quick answer: Between 2022 and 2024, GLP-1 use rose 140.4% while bariatric surgery fell 34.1% — a shift a new Wells Fargo report calls a rewiring of healthcare economics. For patients, that means more medication-first options, trickier insurance math, and a real drugs-vs-surgery decision. For providers and insurers, it means service lines built around late-stage obesity care are shrinking.

GLP-1 vs Bariatric Surgery: The Numbers Behind the Shift

The data comes from a research letter in JAMA Surgery (May 13, 2026) by Harvard Chan and Analysis Group researchers, covering claims from 11.7 million U.S. adults with obesity, overweight, or diabetes diagnoses. From 2022 to 2024:

  • GLP-1 receptor agonist use rose 140.4% — from 36.2 to 62.1 prescriptions per 1,000 people.
  • Metabolic bariatric surgery fell 34.1% — and the decline is accelerating: down 14.4% from 2022 to 2023, then down a further 23.0% from 2023 to 2024.
  • More than 90% of the study population received neither treatment — 9.2% filled a GLP-1 prescription, 0.4% had surgery.

An August 20, 2026 Wells Fargo Industry Insights report cites the study and frames the pattern as a direct substitution — patients choosing medications over the operating room. (The 34% and 140% figures are the study's; Wells Fargo relays them.) Surgical registries confirm the bariatric surgery decline has continued: data from the 2026 ASMBS meeting shows U.S. volumes fell in both 2024 and 2025, after seven years of growth.

Why Weight-Loss Surgery Is Declining

The decline isn't one cause; it's a convergence: GLP-1 prescribing has exploded and medication is tried first for many patients. Payers push the same direction, often requiring a GLP-1 trial or prior authorization before approving a surgical referral. As the JAMA Surgery authors note, "with increasing prior authorization requirements for GLP-1 RA by many payors," tracking surgery trends matters for keeping both pathways accessible.

It's also an access story: 40.3% of U.S. adults are considered obese and 9.4% severely obese, yet more than 90% of the study population received no treatment at all. As Robin Wenzel, head of Wells Fargo Industry Insights, put it: "Right now it is tough, it's expensive, insurance doesn't necessarily cover it unless you meet certain criteria. Because of all of that, it's not widely available."

Ozempic vs Weight Loss Surgery: Results and Cost

The honest comparison most coverage skips: the cost shift cuts both ways. A 2025 JAMA Surgery cohort study of 30,458 patients (Eid et al.) found that bariatric surgery was associated with greater weight loss than GLP-1 therapy AND roughly $11,689 lower ongoing costs over two years. Drugs shift spend from a one-time procedure to recurring prescriptions that continue as long as they're taken — and when they stop, weight often returns.

Comparing the two:

  • Surgery produces the largest average weight loss and, at two years, lower cumulative costs — but carries operative risk, recovery time, and permanent changes to the digestive system.
  • GLP-1s are less invasive, reversible, and increasingly covered — but they're ongoing expenses, and out-of-pocket cost varies widely by plan (see what Ozempic costs without insurance).

Neither is "free." Which trade-offs fit your health profile is a question for a qualified provider, not a coin flip.

Will GLP-1s Replace Bariatric Surgery?

Not completely — and surgeons say so themselves. ASMBS president Dr. Joe Northup told USA Today: "We're starting to see patients come back (for surgery) after having tried these medicines for a year or six months." That matches the study's finding that surgery patients are more medically complex (25.7% with four or more comorbidities vs 22.7% of GLP-1 users), and the declines may be concentrated among the highest-severity patients — the ones who often need surgery most.

For others, medications don't produce enough weight loss, cause intolerable side effects, or lose effect when stopped. The emerging consensus: a layered future — some patients on GLP-1s, some choosing surgery, some having surgery after a medication trial. What the Spending Shift Means for Providers, Patients, and Insurers

Providers and hospitals. Bariatric programs are seeing fewer cases, and Wells Fargo expects ripples through cardiology, joint replacement, sleep apnea, and liver disease service lines. The bank's advice: reposition, don't defend. As Wenzel told USA Today: "It shifts the profit centers from late-stage treatment and intervention to really being more proactive with the management of obesity."

Insurers and employers. GLP-1s are "a significant growth engine for drugmakers but a cost challenge for payers." The coverage picture is split: Medicare began covering GLP-1s for older adults on July 1, 2026, and nearly half of large employers covered them in 2025 — yet 6% of large employers plan to drop weight-loss coverage in 2026 and another 5% in 2027, and only 13 states covered GLP-1s for obesity through Medicaid as of early 2026 (four eliminated coverage on January 1, 2026). John Teasley of Wells Fargo's healthcare banking group: "The biggest takeaway from our research isn't that healthcare is shrinking, it's that healthcare is being rewired."

Patients. Your options and costs depend heavily on who pays. What hasn't changed: most people with obesity still receive no treatment, and both GLP-1s and surgery work under medical supervision.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Is Ozempic better than weight loss surgery?

A: It depends on the outcome. Surgery produces greater average weight loss and lower ongoing costs over two years in the largest comparison cohort (30,458 patients). Ozempic and other GLP-1s are less invasive and reversible, but results can fade if the medication stops. The right answer is personal — BMI, comorbidities, surgical risk, coverage — so decide with a clinician.

Q: Why are bariatric surgeries declining?

A: GLP-1 use rose 140.4% from 2022 to 2024 while bariatric surgery fell 34.1% — a direct substitution, per the Wells Fargo report. Payers increasingly require medication trials or prior authorization first; volumes fell in both 2024 and 2025 after seven years of growth.

Q: Will GLP-1s replace weight loss surgery?

A: No. Patients are returning for surgery after medication trials, and researchers don't expect GLP-1s to fully replace it — particularly for the most medically complex patients, the most undertreated group.

The Bottom Line

The 34.1% drop in bariatric surgery and 140.4% rise in GLP-1 use are real and accelerating — and they're reshaping who pays for obesity care. For patients, the shift brings more options and more coverage than ever. But neither path is one-size-fits-all, the cost math is plan-dependent, and both treatments require medical supervision.
