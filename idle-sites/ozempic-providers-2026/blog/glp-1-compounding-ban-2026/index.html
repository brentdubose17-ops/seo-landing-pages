GLP-1 Compounding Ban: What Novo & Lilly's FDA Push Means for Compounded Ozempic and Wegovy

Published August 20, 2026By ABD Legacy LLC

Novo Nordisk has formally asked the FDA to stop compounding pharmacies from making cheaper versions of its semaglutide-based therapies, joining Eli Lilly in a coordinated push to end the compounding era for the two biggest GLP-1 molecules. The news, reported by pharmaphorum on August 19, 2026, raises the question thousands of patients and prescribers are asking: is compounded Ozempic legal — and will it be banned?

Here is what the manufacturers are actually asking for, where the FDA's shortage list really stands, and what it means for access to compounded Wegovy and Ozempic in 2026.

What Novo and Lilly are asking the FDA to do

Novo has asked the FDA to place semaglutide on the agency's "Demonstrable Difficulties for Compounding" (DDC) lists — drugs that, even when in limited supply, are considered too complicated for compounders to make safely. Novo first filed the request in October 2024 (Docket FDA-2017-N-2562) and has kept it active as reporting renewed on August 19.

"These drugs are inherently complex to compound safely, and the risks they pose to patient safety far outweigh any benefits." — Novo Nordisk

Eli Lilly has pushed along a parallel track, asking the FDA to ban other parties from making copies of its tirzepatide therapies (Mounjaro for diabetes, Zepbound for obesity) and pressing enforcement through cease-and-desist letters to compounding pharmacies, weight-loss clinics, and medical spas. Novo has filed more than a dozen legal actions against clinics and pharmacies since late 2023, and in February 2026 sued telehealth giant Hims & Hers over its compounded semaglutide products.

The DDC mechanism matters because it is a permanent backstop: a drug on the DDC lists may not be compounded at all — even during a future shortage.

The shortage-list reality: Ozempic and Wegovy are not in shortage

Some coverage of this story repeats an outdated premise — that Wegovy and Ozempic remain on the FDA's shortage list. That is not accurate. The FDA determined the semaglutide injection shortage was resolved on February 21, 2025, after a roughly three-year shortage; the tirzepatide shortage was resolved in late 2024. Neither drug currently appears in the FDA's drug shortage database.

That distinction matters. The manufacturers are not pushing to end compounding because of an ongoing shortage — the shortage-based compounding pathway for these molecules is already closed. Their DDC and bulks-list requests would additionally foreclose compounding even in a future shortage, and they protect patent economics: generic semaglutide is blocked until at least 2032 and tirzepatide until 2036.

Three regulatory levers are closing at once

1. The shortage list (already closed). Under federal law (FD&C Act sections 503A and 503B), pharmacies could compound copies of FDA-approved drugs while they were on the shortage list. With semaglutide and tirzepatide resolved, that legal basis ended — and the FDA enforced wind-down deadlines through spring 2025.

2. The 503B bulks list (proposed closure, pending decision). On April 30, 2026, the FDA proposed excluding semaglutide, tirzepatide, and liraglutide from the list of bulk substances outsourcing facilities may compound, finding "no clinical need" for it. The public comment period closed June 29, 2026, and a final determination is pending.

"When FDA-approved drugs are available, outsourcing facilities cannot lawfully compound using bulk drug substances unless there is a clear clinical need." — then-FDA Commissioner Marty Makary

3. The DDC lists (Novo's request, pending). If the FDA grants Novo's nomination and adds semaglutide to the DDC lists, compounding pharmacies could not make it under any circumstances — shortage or not. The FDA has not yet acted on the request.

Is compounded Ozempic legal?

The honest answer: it depends on the pathway. Under 503A, a state-licensed pharmacy can still compound a patient-specific semaglutide prescription when a prescriber documents a medical need that an approved drug cannot meet. Under 503B, bulk compounding without individual prescriptions is what is closing — first via the resolved shortages, and potentially permanently if the bulks-list exclusion is finalized.

The FDA's own guidance is blunt: "Compounded drugs should only be used in patients whose medical needs cannot be met by an FDA-approved drug." The agency has documented real safety concerns with compounded GLP-1s — more than 455 adverse event reports linked to compounded semaglutide and more than 320 linked to compounded tirzepatide, many involving dosing errors from multidose vials, plus findings of under-strength product and unknown impurities. The FDA also warns that unapproved versions "do not undergo FDA's review for safety, effectiveness, and quality before they are marketed."

Will compounded GLP-1s be banned?

Nothing is banned today. But the trajectory is unmistakable: the shortage pathway is closed, the 503B exclusion would end bulk compounding permanently if finalized, and a DDC listing would ban semaglutide compounding outright. The FDA has not acted on either the bulks-list decision or Novo's DDC request — both remain the two decisions to watch. What should providers do?

  1. Start transition conversations now. If your patients are on compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide, the legal supply is narrowing. Branded Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, and Zepbound remain available — see our Ozempic vs Wegovy vs Mounjaro comparison.
  2. Manage cost with manufacturer programs. The biggest barrier to switching is price — compounded versions ran about $150–300/month versus $1,000+ for branded. Novo and Lilly both run savings programs, and newer low-cost channels (including tablet options around $149–299/month) have expanded access.
  3. Document any continued compounded use. For 503A compounding to stay defensible, you need a documented patient-specific medical need for the compounded product over the approved one.
  4. Screen your sources. The FDA has issued 55+ warning letters to online GLP-1 sellers and flagged counterfeit labels and improper shipping. Refer patients to state-licensed pharmacies only.
  5. Watch the two pending FDA decisions. The 503B bulks-list exclusion and the DDC nomination are the near-term catalysts; either could close what remains of the compounded market.

Bottom line

The GLP-1 compounding ban push is real, coordinated, and advancing on three regulatory fronts — but nothing is final yet. Compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide remain technically available through documented patient-specific pathways while the FDA decides the 503B exclusion and Novo's DDC request. For providers, the safe play is to start moving patients toward FDA-approved options now, lean on savings programs for cost, and keep one eye on the FDA's docket. FAQ: Common Questions About the GLP-1 Compounding Ban

Q: Is compounded Ozempic legal in 2026?

A: Patient-specific compounding under federal law 503A remains technically available when a licensed prescriber documents a medical need that an FDA-approved drug cannot meet. Mass bulk compounding under 503B is closing: the shortage pathway is already closed for semaglutide and tirzepatide, and the FDA has proposed permanently excluding all three GLP-1 APIs from the 503B bulks list.

Q: Will compounded semaglutide be banned?

A: Not yet, but the pathway is closing on three fronts. The shortage-based pathway is already closed, a pending FDA decision would end 503B bulk compounding permanently, and if the FDA grants Novo's request to place semaglutide on the DDC lists, compounding the drug would be prohibited even during a future shortage.

Q: Are Ozempic and Wegovy still on the FDA shortage list?

A: No. The FDA declared the semaglutide injection shortage resolved on February 21, 2025, and resolved the tirzepatide shortage in late 2024. Neither currently appears on the FDA drug shortage list. Novo's liraglutide products (Victoza, Saxenda) remain in shortage.

Q: What should providers do about patients on compounded semaglutide?

A: Transition patients to FDA-approved options where possible, use manufacturer savings programs to manage cost, document any continued compounded use with a clear medical need, and watch for the FDA's final decisions on the 503B bulks-list exclusion and Novo's DDC request.

Q: Why is compounded semaglutide cheaper than Ozempic?

A: Compounded versions have been priced at roughly $150 to $300 per month versus $1,000 or more for branded drugs, because compounders do not bear the development and marketing costs of approved products. That price gap is why the market grew during the shortage era — and why manufacturers are pushing to close it.
