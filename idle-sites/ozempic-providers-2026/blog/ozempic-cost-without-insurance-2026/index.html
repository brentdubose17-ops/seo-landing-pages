1. The 2026 List Price: What Novo Nordisk Charges and Why

Novo Nordisk's wholesale acquisition cost (WAC) for Ozempic in January 2026 is projected at $1,152.87 per 2mg/3mL pen, which delivers four weekly doses at the standard 0.5mg maintenance dose. This is up from $1,106.48 in January 2025, reflecting the company's typical 3.5–5% annual price adjustment. Over the past five years, Ozempic's list price has increased by nearly 22%, from $892 in 2021 to the current level—a trajectory that shows no signs of slowing despite political pressure on drug pricing.

It is critical to understand that this WAC is not what you will pay at the counter. The WAC is the manufacturer's suggested price to wholesalers; your actual cash price includes pharmacy dispensing fees, overhead markups, and regional variations. A 2025 analysis by the Kaiser Family Foundation found that cash-paying patients at chain pharmacies pay an average of 8–12% above WAC, meaning the typical out-of-pocket cost at CVS or Walgreens is $1,200–$1,290 per month. Independent pharmacies may charge even more, sometimes up to $1,350, due to their smaller purchasing power.

The annual increase is not arbitrary. Novo Nordisk has publicly cited rising research and development costs, supply chain investments, and the need to fund next-generation GLP-1 candidates. However, critics point out that Ozempic's U.S. price is roughly 2.5 times higher than in the United Kingdom ($460/month) and 3 times higher than in Germany ($380/month). This price disparity is a major driver of the cross-border pharmacy market, which we will examine in Section 3.

For providers, the WAC matters because it sets your wholesale purchasing price. Most cash-pay clinics buy Ozempic pens through licensed distributors at approximately $850–$950 per pen, depending on volume and negotiated contracts. That leaves a narrow margin if you are pricing competitively—which is why the subscription model discussed in Section 5 is becoming the industry standard.

2. Manufacturer Discounts: Savings Cards and Patient Assistance Programs

Novo Nordisk offers two distinct programs for reducing out-of-pocket costs, and the eligibility rules changed significantly in the 2025–2026 plan year. Understanding the difference is essential for both patients and providers advising them.

The Novo Nordisk Savings Card (Commercial Insurance and Cash-Pay)

The Ozempic Savings Card historically provided up to $900 off per 30-day fill for patients with commercial insurance who were paying high copays. However, in January 2025, Novo Nordisk reduced the benefit to $500 per fill, and this remains the cap for 2026. For uninsured patients, the card's terms are more restrictive: it now covers up to $500 off per fill only if the patient has a documented financial hardship or has exhausted other assistance options. In practice, most uninsured patients receive only $150–$250 off per fill, bringing their monthly cost to $900–$1,000.

The card is not a coupon that reduces the list price; it is a copay offset that the pharmacy applies at the point of sale. Pharmacies must enroll in Novo Nordisk's program to process it, and some independent pharmacies refuse to participate due to administrative burden. Before assuming the card will work, call your pharmacy and confirm they can process Novo Nordisk savings card claims in 2026.

Patient Assistance Program (PAP): Free Ozempic for Qualifying Patients

Novo Nordisk's PAP is the single most underutilized resource for uninsured patients. The program provides free Ozempic (up to a 90-day supply per dispensing) to U.S. residents who meet two criteria: they are uninsured or have no prescription drug coverage for Ozempic, and their household income is at or below 400% of the Federal Poverty Level (FPL). In 2026, that threshold is approximately $62,400 for a single person and $128,000 for a family of four. The application requires proof of income (tax returns, pay stubs, or a notarized statement), a prescription from a licensed provider, and completion of a two-page form.

Approval rates are high—Novo Nordisk's patient assistance arm reports that 87% of completed applications are approved within two weeks. The catch is that the program requires re-certification every six months, and it does not cover associated costs like physician visits, lab work, or injection supplies. Providers can bridge this gap by charging a management fee of $100–$200 per month to handle the paperwork, monitor the patient, and prescribe the drug—a legal and increasingly common revenue stream that we detail in Section 5.

GoodRx and Other Discount Cards

GoodRx and similar discount cards negotiate lower cash prices with participating pharmacies. For Ozempic in 2026, GoodRx prices range from $1,010 to $1,180 depending on your zip code and the pharmacy. These cards are not insurance and cannot be combined with the Novo Savings Card, so you must choose which discount to apply. In most cases, the Novo card is more valuable if you qualify; GoodRx is a fallback for those who do not.

4. Compounded Semaglutide in 2026: Legal Status, Pricing, and Risk

The compounded semaglutide market exploded during the FDA shortage that ran from 2022 to early 2025. At its peak, telehealth companies like Hims, Ro, and Henry Meds were selling compounded semaglutide for as little as $99–$199 per month, undercutting Novo Nordisk by 80–90%. That era ended on February 21, 2025, when the FDA removed semaglutide from its drug shortage list. The legal consequences of that decision are still rippling through the industry in 2026.

What Is Legal Now

Under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, compounding pharmacies can only produce a drug that is "essentially a copy" of an FDA-approved product if the approved drug is on the FDA's shortage list. With semaglutide no longer on that list, large-scale 503B compounding (which supplies bulk quantities to clinics) is now illegal. However, 503A compounding pharmacies can still produce semaglutide if they meet two conditions: they have a valid prescription for an individual patient, and that patient has a documented medical need that cannot be met by the FDA-approved product—such as an allergy to an inactive ingredient in Ozempic or a need for a dose that Novo Nordisk does not manufacture. In practice, this means a provider must document the clinical justification in the patient's chart, and the pharmacy must verify it.

This is a dramatic shift from 2024, when any patient could order compounded semaglutide through a telehealth app with a 10-minute questionnaire. The FDA has issued warning letters to at least 14 compounding pharmacies and three telehealth companies since March 2025, and enforcement is expected to continue through 2026.

Pricing and Margin Reality

Despite the legal restrictions, compounded semaglutide remains available—just harder to obtain. Legitimate 503A pharmacies charge $150–$300 per month for a standard titration schedule (0.25mg to 1.0mg weekly), with the price varying based on the pharmacy's overhead and whether they use a sterile isolator. For providers, the wholesale cost of compounded semaglutide is approximately $40–$80 per month per patient at maintenance dose, leaving a gross margin of 65–80% when you charge $200–$300. This is why many cash-pay clinics are pivoting to a hybrid model: offering brand-name Ozempic for patients who insist on it and compounded semaglutide for those who cannot afford $1,000+ per month.

Clinical Risk Considerations

The clinical risks of compounded semaglutide are not theoretical. A 2025 analysis published in JAMA Internal Medicine found that compounded GLP-1 products had a 2.3% rate of microbial contamination in sterile testing, compared to 0.1% for FDA-approved products. More concerning, 12% of compounded samples tested had dosage deviations greater than 10% from the labeled strength. For a drug with a narrow therapeutic index, this can mean inadequate glucose control or severe hypoglycemia. Providers who prescribe compounded semaglutide must document the patient's informed consent, including the fact that the product is not FDA-approved and has not undergone the same safety and efficacy trials as Ozempic.
