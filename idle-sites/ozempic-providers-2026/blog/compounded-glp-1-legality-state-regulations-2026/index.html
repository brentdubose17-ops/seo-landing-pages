Compounded GLP-1 Legality: State Regulations Providers Need to Know in 2026

Published August 06, 2026By ABD Legacy LLC

The compounded GLP-1 market has entered a new legal phase. Federal rules for compounded copies of semaglutide and tirzepatide are now reasonably settled — and restrictive. State boards are adding a second, uneven oversight layer. For clinics, telehealth practices, and medical spas, the question is what their state adds to the federal floor — and how aggressively it enforces. This guide walks providers through that split, based on an August 2026 Sheppard Mullin analysis of state regulation of compounded GLP-1s and peptides, flagging uncertainty where the law is still moving.

The Federal Baseline: Settled and Restrictive

The eligibility baseline for what may lawfully be compounded is fixed federally, chiefly by Sections 503A and 503B of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, and it is largely uniform nationwide. GLP-1 receptor agonists were never on the 503A or 503B Bulks Lists; their compounding was permitted only through the drug shortage pathway under Section 503A — which closed as the FDA resolved the tirzepatide and semaglutide shortages.

The FDA reinforced the closure through compliance guidance on “essentially a copy” compounding under Section 503A(b)(2), a proposal to exclude these molecules from the 503B Bulks List, and successive waves of warning letters to compounders and telehealth marketers. The substantive federal rules for GLP-1 copies are now, in the source’s words, “reasonably settled and restrictive.” Uncertainty flag: that describes the current posture, not a guarantee — FDA guidance can be revised and enforcement priorities shift. Verify current guidance before relying on it.

State GLP-1 Regulations: The Layer Above the Federal Floor

No state can fall below the federal compounding standard; a state board can only add stricter requirements. The question is how much each adds and how actively it enforces. Approaches fall into five groups:

  • The outlier — California: The only state with a codified standard stricter than federal law. Its regulations, effective October 1, 2025, require pharmacists to document a clinically significant, patient-specific difference, and a certificate of analysis naming the active ingredient manufacturer, not merely the distributor.
  • The enforcers — Ohio and Washington: Both issued GLP-1-specific board guidance largely restating federal law, yet both demonstrably enforce. Ohio’s July 2025 guidance treats semaglutide and tirzepatide as no longer compoundable as copies post-shortage, expressly prohibits the investigational peptides retatrutide and cagrilintide, and requires Ohio-licensed distributors; its board reports summarily suspending more than thirty clinics and medical spas since the beginning of 2025. Washington imposed a stop-service order on a GLP-1 sterile compounder for compounding and licensure failures.
  • The guidance states — Alabama, New Jersey, and others: Position statements typically prohibiting salt forms and requiring pharmaceutical-grade active ingredients from an FDA-registered source with a valid certificate of analysis. Alabama uniquely reached physician-office compounding via its medical board; West Virginia, Oregon, Kansas, and Kentucky issued similar statements.
  • The consumer protection route — Connecticut, Illinois, South Carolina: Connecticut’s pharmacy regulator has taken no GLP-1 position, but its Attorney General has run an active enforcement campaign under the unfair trade practices statute against distributors, med spas, and clinics — targeting marketing conduct regardless of the pharmacy board’s position.
  • The silent majority — Texas, Florida, and most states: No GLP-1- or peptide-specific board position; they regulate through general frameworks incorporating the “essentially a copy” limitation and USP chapters. Florida’s sterile rule expressly ties state standards to the FD&C Act, including Section 503A.

Compliance Implications for Providers

  • Risk tracks enforcement willingness, not rule text. In Ohio and Washington the primary risk is that the board acts — know its enforcement record, not just its guidance.
  • Sourcing discipline. Active ingredients should be pharmaceutical grade, never labeled “research use only,” from an FDA-registered establishment, with a valid certificate of analysis — in California, one naming the actual manufacturer.
  • Base form, not salt. Multiple state boards treat a salt form as a different active ingredient, which may render it ineligible for compounding.
  • Avoid investigational GLP-1s. Retatrutide and cagrilintide lack an approved reference product and are expressly prohibited in certain jurisdictions, including Ohio.
  • Document the difference. When a compounded product differs from an approved drug, document a clinically significant, patient-specific reason; trivial additions, like vitamin B12 alone, are unlikely to withstand “essentially a copy” scrutiny.
  • Marketing is in the crosshairs. Consumer protection and FDA actions have targeted promotions implying a compounded product is FDA-approved or therapeutically equivalent to the branded version.

The Private Enforcement Layer

State regulation is not the only second layer. Lilly and Novo Nordisk have filed trademark, patent, and false advertising suits across dozens of jurisdictions, and at least one compounder has countersued on antitrust grounds. This private enforcement operates independently of state posture — treat brand litigation risk as a constant.

Where Uncertainty Remains

Two open questions deserve explicit flags. First, the broader peptide category is in flux in a way that GLP-1 copies are not: in April 2026, the FDA removed twelve peptides from Category 2 of the interim 503A bulk scheme and scheduled Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee meetings to consider several for the 503A Bulks List. Removal is not authorization — those peptides sit in a regulatory gray zone. Second, because most states default to the federal standard, the federal trajectory drives the state trajectory: if the FDA loosens peptide restrictions, federal-default states loosen with it, no state action required. California, the enforcement-heavy states, and the consumer protection channel should remain distinct either way.

Uncertainty flag: State positions change frequently; this article reflects the source’s August 2026 snapshot — verify current board guidance in your state before deciding.

Bottom Line for Providers

The state layer is real but widely misread. For what may be compounded, almost everywhere the answer is the federal rules — California is the lone state to have written a stricter rule. The real variation lies in enforcement intensity, sourcing conditions, and, in a few states, a consumer protection posture outside compounding law altogether. Providers who anchor their programs to disciplined sourcing, documented clinically significant differences, avoidance of investigational substances, USP adherence, and restrained marketing will be best positioned to operate across jurisdictions. FAQ: Compounded GLP-1 Legality and State Rules

Q: Is compounded GLP-1 still legal after the drug shortages ended?

A: Yes, but the legal pathway is narrow. GLP-1 receptor agonists were never on the 503A or 503B Bulks Lists; compounding was permitted only through the drug shortage pathway under Section 503A, and that pathway has closed as the FDA resolved the tirzepatide and semaglutide shortages. The federal baseline for GLP-1 copies is now reasonably settled and restrictive, and states can only add stricter conditions on top.

Q: What makes California different from other states on compounded GLP-1s?

A: California is the only state with a codified standard stricter than federal law. Its compounding regulations, effective October 1, 2025, require pharmacists to verify and document a clinically significant, patient-specific difference and impose conditions such as a certificate of analysis that names the active ingredient manufacturer rather than merely the distributor. It is the only GLP-1-relevant state rule with the force of law.

Q: Which states enforce semaglutide compounding rules most aggressively?

A: Ohio and Washington. Ohio’s July 2025 guidance treats semaglutide and tirzepatide as no longer compoundable as copies post-shortage, expressly prohibits the investigational peptides retatrutide and cagrilintide, and requires active ingredients from an Ohio-licensed distributor; its board reports summarily suspending more than thirty clinics and medical spas since the beginning of 2025. Washington imposed a stop-service order on a GLP-1 sterile compounder for sterile compounding and licensure failures.

Q: Can adding vitamin B12 make a compounded GLP-1 product legal?

A: Generally not. The FDA has signaled that adding B12 alone is insufficient to escape essentially-a-copy treatment under Section 503A(b)(2). Compounders should instead document a clinically significant, patient-specific reason for any variation from an FDA-approved drug; trivial additions are unlikely to withstand scrutiny.

Q: Does a provider need to worry about state rules if their state has said nothing about GLP-1s?

A: Yes. States cannot relax the federal baseline, and the silent majority still regulates compounding through general frameworks that incorporate the essentially-a-copy limitation and USP chapters. Texas and Florida are representative. Providers also face a private enforcement layer: Lilly and Novo Nordisk have filed trademark, patent, and false advertising suits across dozens of jurisdictions, independent of any state regulatory posture.
