What Is the Ozempic Pill?

The Ozempic pill is an oral tablet form of semaglutide — the same GLP-1 receptor agonist in the Ozempic injection — approved for adults with type 2 diabetes. It is Novo Nordisk's reformulated version of Rybelsus, the oral semaglutide that has been on the market since 2019, now sold under the Ozempic name with updated tablet strengths: 1.5 mg, 4 mg, and 9 mg. The pill became available in the United States on May 4, 2026, in more than 70,000 pharmacies plus NovoCare Pharmacy and participating telehealth providers.

If you landed here because you searched "Ozempic pill," you are not alone — the term is a fast-rising one as patients discover there is now a daily oral option alongside the familiar weekly injection. Novo Nordisk's Healthline-reported launch coverage described the tablet as a reformulation of Rybelsus that delivers the same efficacy and safety profile at lower doses, and Medical News Today framed it as a potentially more affordable alternative to injectable GLP-1s. Who Is the Ozempic Pill For?

The Ozempic pill is approved for adults with type 2 diabetes, used together with diet and exercise. In addition to glucose control, it carries a cardiovascular indication: it reduces the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) — heart attack, stroke, and cardiovascular death — in adults with type 2 diabetes and established cardiovascular disease. That makes it one of the few oral diabetes medications with a proven cardiovascular benefit on the label.

It is important to be precise about what the Ozempic pill is not approved for: weight loss. The Ozempic brand has long been prescribed off-label for weight management, and the pill can support weight loss in practice, but the FDA-approved weight-management indication belongs to the Wegovy pill (oral semaglutide 25 mg), approved December 22, 2025 as the first oral GLP-1 for weight management. If your primary goal is weight loss, that is the product to discuss with your clinician. If your goal is type 2 diabetes management — with or without cardiovascular risk reduction — the Ozempic pill is the approved oral.

Ozempic Pill Dosing: 1.5 mg → 4 mg → 9 mg

The Ozempic pill is taken once daily. Treatment starts at the lowest strength, 1.5 mg, and is increased gradually to 4 mg and then 9 mg as needed and as tolerated. Novo Nordisk has said it expects an FDA decision on a higher 25 mg oral dose by the end of 2026, so the approved strengths may expand.

The tablets are not mg-for-mg interchangeable with the older Rybelsus tablets (3 mg, 7 mg, and 14 mg), which have been retired in the US. The reformulation is designed to deliver the same effect at lower doses because the smaller tablets absorb better. Never switch strengths or formulations on your own — a clinician should map any transition, including between the Ozempic pill and the Ozempic injection.

The Fasting Rule: SNAC and the 30-Minute Window

This is the single most important practical detail about the Ozempic pill. The tablet contains an absorption enhancer called SNAC (salcaprozate sodium) that helps semaglutide cross the stomach lining — and it only works on an empty stomach. The instructions are strict:

  • Take the tablet first thing in the morning, on an empty stomach
  • Swallow it with no more than 4 ounces of plain water
  • Wait at least 30 minutes before eating, drinking, or taking any other oral medication
  • Do not split, crush, or chew the tablet

Coffee counts as "drinking" — that morning cup has to wait. Miss the window and absorption drops sharply, which means a weaker effect and more stomach upset. This fasting routine is the main practical difference between the pill and injectable GLP-1s, and it is worth understanding before you start. Oral Semaglutide Side Effects

Because the Ozempic pill is semaglutide, its side effects are the familiar GLP-1 class effects. The most common are nausea, abdominal pain, constipation, and diarrhea, with nausea most likely during dose escalation. Gastrointestinal side effects typically ease as your body adjusts to each new dose — which is exactly why titration is gradual. Our Ozempic side effects explainer covers the class effects in more detail.

Two practical cautions specific to the pill: because absorption depends on the fasting routine, taking it incorrectly can make GI side effects worse or the dose less effective. And because oral semaglutide has lower bioavailability than injected semaglutide, side effects at equivalent doses are not directly comparable between forms — another reason transitions should be clinician-managed.

For a patient with type 2 diabetes, the choice between the Ozempic pill and the Ozempic injection comes down to delivery, routine, and coverage — not results. The evidence base for the injection is longer, but the pill delivers the same molecule with a cardiovascular benefit and no needles.

Ozempic Pill vs Wegovy Pill: Know the Difference

The Ozempic pill and the Wegovy pill both contain oral semaglutide, and the names are easy to mix up. The practical difference is the indication and the dose: the Ozempic pill (1.5/4/9 mg) is approved for type 2 diabetes, while the Wegovy pill (25 mg) is approved for weight management plus MACE reduction in adults with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease. If weight loss is your goal, the Wegovy pill is the approved option; if you have type 2 diabetes, the Ozempic pill is the one matched to your condition. Bottom Line

The Ozempic pill gives type 2 diabetes patients a real oral alternative to the weekly injection: the same semaglutide molecule, a cardiovascular benefit on the label, no needles, room-temperature storage, and cash pricing from $149 per month. The trade-offs are the daily fasting routine, the gradual 1.5 → 4 → 9 mg titration, and the fact that it is not approved for weight loss.
