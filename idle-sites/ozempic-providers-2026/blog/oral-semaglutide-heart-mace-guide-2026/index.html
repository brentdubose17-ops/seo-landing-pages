Does Oral Semaglutide Help Your Heart? What MACE Means and What to Ask Your Doctor

Published August 22, 2026 By ABD Legacy LLC

For most patients, oral semaglutide still reads as a weight-loss story. The biggest change of the past year has nothing to do with the scale. Both oral semaglutide products now on the US market — the Ozempic pill (for type 2 diabetes) and the Wegovy pill (for obesity) — carry FDA indications to reduce the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE): cardiovascular death, heart attack, and stroke. Here is what MACE means, what the trials showed, and why patients asking “does Ozempic help your heart” now have a real, evidence-based answer.

What is MACE?

MACE — major adverse cardiovascular events — is the standard composite endpoint in cardiovascular outcomes trials. It combines the outcomes patients care about most: cardiovascular death, nonfatal heart attack (myocardial infarction), and nonfatal stroke. Because each event is relatively rare in any one patient, researchers measure them together to determine whether a drug genuinely protects the heart.

The Evidence: The SOUL Trial

The cardiovascular case for oral semaglutide rests primarily on the SOUL trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine and covered extensively by the American Journal of Managed Care (AJMC). SOUL randomized 9,650 adults age 50 and older with type 2 diabetes and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and/or chronic kidney disease to once-daily oral semaglutide (max dose 14 mg) or placebo, on top of standard care.

Over a median follow-up of about 47.5 months, MACE occurred in 12.0% of the semaglutide group versus 13.8% with placebo — a 14% relative risk reduction (HR 0.86; 95% CI, 0.77–0.96; P = .0028) and roughly a 2% absolute reduction at three years. AJMC reported that the 26% reduction in nonfatal heart attacks was the primary driver, with nonfatal stroke down 12% and cardiovascular death down 7%. There was no increase in serious adverse events. The MACE benefit held regardless of heart failure history, AJMC separately reported.

The SELECT Trial and the Wegovy Pill

The oral story builds on the landmark SELECT trial of injectable semaglutide: 17,604 adults with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease — but no diabetes — who saw a 20% reduction in MACE. That evidence led the FDA to approve semaglutide to reduce cardiovascular events in this population — coverage AJMC has tracked. AJMC also reported a model suggesting broad use could prevent up to 2 million MACE over 10 years.

When the FDA approved the Wegovy pill in December 2025 — the first oral GLP-1 for obesity — it carried the same cardiovascular indication: reducing MACE in adults with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease. So the heart benefit is no longer tied to injectable therapy alone.

What the Ozempic Pill Adds

The Ozempic pill — the reformulated oral semaglutide (1.5/4/9 mg) that replaced Rybelsus in the US — launched May 4, 2026, approved for blood sugar control in type 2 diabetes and for reducing MACE risk in adults with type 2 diabetes at high risk for these events. As MPR noted in its launch coverage, the tablets are not milligram-for-milligram interchangeable with Rybelsus; patients on Rybelsus should talk to their clinician before switching. Self-pay pricing runs $149 (1.5 mg) to $299 (9 mg) per month. “Ozempic pill delivers strong blood sugar control and proven cardiovascular risk reduction, and people may also lose weight,” a Novo Nordisk executive said at launch.

Why This Matters Beyond Weight Loss

The cardiovascular indication changes three things for patients:

  • The goal of treatment broadens. Semaglutide is no longer just about glucose or weight — it is a drug shown to prevent heart attacks and strokes in high-risk patients, even regardless of how much weight was lost.
  • More people qualify. The Wegovy pill's heart indication covers patients with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease without diabetes. The Ozempic pill covers adults with type 2 diabetes at high cardiovascular risk — a group the FDA itself considers high priority.
  • Coverage math can change. Plans that exclude weight-loss therapy may still cover semaglutide when it is prescribed for cardiovascular risk reduction — though prior authorization is usually required. Medicare's GLP-1 Bridge Program offers the Wegovy pill at $50 per month for eligible members.

Questions to Ask Your Doctor

  1. Do I qualify for the heart-protection indication? — Ask whether your cardiovascular history (or your type 2 diabetes, if you have it) makes you eligible under the Ozempic pill or Wegovy pill labels.
  2. Is the benefit separate from weight loss? — The MACE reduction was seen regardless of how much weight patients lost; ask what that means for you.
  3. What is my actual risk? — Ask for your personal heart attack and stroke risk and how much a 14–20% relative reduction translates to in absolute terms.
  4. Pill or injection? — If you are deciding between forms, ask about the pill's dosing rules (empty stomach, no more than 4 ounces of water, 30-minute wait), side effects, and cost. Our pill vs injection guide can help you compare.
  5. Will my insurance cover it? — Ask whether your plan covers oral semaglutide for cardiovascular risk reduction and what prior authorization steps are needed.

The Bottom Line

Oral semaglutide is now approved to do more than lower blood sugar or drive weight loss — the Ozempic pill and the Wegovy pill both carry FDA indications to reduce the risk of heart attack, stroke, and cardiovascular death in the right patients. The SOUL and SELECT trials provide the numbers; your doctor decides whether you fit the profile. Frequently Asked Questions

Does the Ozempic pill reduce heart attack and stroke risk?

Yes. The Ozempic pill (oral semaglutide 1.5/4/9 mg) is FDA-approved to reduce the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events — cardiovascular death, nonfatal heart attack, and nonfatal stroke — in adults with type 2 diabetes who are at high risk for these events. The approval rests on the SOUL trial, in which oral semaglutide cut MACE risk by 14% versus placebo in 9,650 adults with type 2 diabetes and established cardiovascular or kidney disease.

Is the Wegovy pill approved for heart protection too?

Yes. When the FDA approved the Wegovy pill (oral semaglutide 25 mg) in December 2025 — the first oral GLP-1 for obesity — it also carried an indication to reduce MACE in adults with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease, mirroring the SELECT trial evidence for injectable semaglutide.

How much does oral semaglutide lower cardiovascular risk?

In the SOUL trial, oral semaglutide reduced MACE by 14% relative to placebo (HR 0.86; 12.0% vs 13.8% over a median follow-up of about 47.5 months). The reduction in nonfatal heart attacks was the main driver at 26%. For context, the SELECT trial showed a 20% MACE reduction with injectable semaglutide in people with obesity and heart disease but without diabetes.

Do I need to have diabetes to get the heart benefit?

No. The Wegovy pill's cardiovascular indication covers adults with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease even without diabetes — that was the SELECT trial population. The Ozempic pill's heart indication, by contrast, is specifically for adults with type 2 diabetes.

Will insurance cover oral semaglutide for heart protection?

Sometimes — and the cardiovascular indication can change the coverage math. Plans that exclude weight-loss therapy may still cover semaglutide when it is prescribed for cardiovascular risk reduction, though prior authorization is usually required. Medicare's GLP-1 Bridge Program offers the Wegovy pill at $50 per month for eligible members.

What are the main side effects of oral semaglutide?

The most common side effects are gastrointestinal — nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation — and they mostly occur during dose escalation. In the SOUL trial there was no increase in serious adverse events versus placebo. The pill must be taken on an empty stomach with no more than 4 ounces of plain water, waiting 30 minutes before eating or taking other medicines.
