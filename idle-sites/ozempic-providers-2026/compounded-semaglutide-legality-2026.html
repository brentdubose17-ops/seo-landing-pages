The FDA Shortage Status: The Single Most Important Legal Fact

The legality of compounded semaglutide hinges on one central fact: whether the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) is on the FDA's official drug shortage list. This is not a minor regulatory detail—it is the legal pivot point that determines whether bulk compounding is permitted under federal law.

Here is the timeline that matters:

  • May 2024: FDA removed semaglutide (Ozempic and Wegovy) from the shortage list, triggering a wave of enforcement actions.
  • August 2025: FDA reinstated semaglutide to the shortage list, creating a temporary legal reprieve for compounders.
  • December 2025: FDA announced the shortage was officially resolved, initiating the final compliance clock.

The December 2025 announcement is the legal event that matters most for you as a prescriber. When a drug is on the shortage list, compounding pharmacies can produce "essentially a copy" of the FDA-approved drug in bulk under Section 503A and 503B of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act). Once the drug is removed from the shortage list, that bulk production authority evaporates—except in very narrow circumstances.

Understanding the 60–90 Day Transition Window

Most regulatory summaries stop at "compounding is illegal now." But the reality is more nuanced and practically important. The FDA typically provides a transition period of 60 to 90 days after a shortage is declared resolved. During this window, compounding pharmacies may continue to fill prescriptions for patients who were already established on the medication.

This grandfathering window is not a blanket license to write new prescriptions. It applies to existing patient relationships and existing prescriptions. As of early 2026, the clock is ticking on this transition period for semaglutide. If you have patients currently on compounded semaglutide, you need to act now to transition them to FDA-approved alternatives or document their medical necessity for continued compounding in a way that survives legal scrutiny.

Key Insight: The legal risk after the shortage resolves shifts overwhelmingly from the pharmacy to the prescriber. Pharmacies can simply stop filling. You, as the prescriber, are the one who continues to write prescriptions—and you are the one who faces liability for prescribing an unapproved drug without documented medical necessity.

Section 503A vs. 503B: The Two Compounding Pathways

Understanding the difference between 503A and 503B compounding is essential for evaluating your legal exposure and choosing the right pharmacy partners.

503A: Patient-Specific Compounding

Under Section 503A, a licensed pharmacist or physician compounds a medication specifically for an individual patient based on a valid prescription. This is traditional compounding—the kind that has existed for decades. For 503A compounding to be legal:

  • The prescription must be patient-specific (not bulk production).
  • The drug must be compounded for an identified individual patient.
  • The pharmacy must be licensed by the state board of pharmacy.
  • The pharmacy must comply with USP <797> sterility standards.

When a drug is NOT on the shortage list, 503A compounding of an essentially identical copy of an FDA-approved drug becomes problematic. The FD&C Act requires that compounded drugs be "necessary" for the patient's treatment—meaning there is a medical reason the FDA-approved version won't work (e.g., allergy to an inactive ingredient, need for a different dosage strength).

503B: Bulk Production by Outsourcing Facilities

Section 503B allows registered outsourcing facilities to compound drugs in bulk without patient-specific prescriptions. These facilities operate more like manufacturers and must comply with current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMP). They are subject to FDA inspection, must report adverse events, and must meet stricter sterility requirements.

The critical legal distinction: 503B facilities can only compound a drug that is "essentially a copy" of an FDA-approved product if that drug is on the shortage list. Once the shortage is resolved, 503B facilities must stop producing that drug unless they have a valid clinical reason documented.

State-Level Enforcement: A Patchwork of Rules

While federal law sets the baseline, state pharmacy boards are the primary enforcers for 503A pharmacies. And the states are diverging significantly in how they handle compounded GLP-1s.

States with Specific GLP-1 Compounding Laws

As of January 2026, at least 12 states have passed or proposed laws specifically regulating compounded GLP-1 medications. These laws vary widely in their approach:

  • Texas (SB 1205): Imposes penalties up to $25,000 per violation for unauthorized compounding of GLP-1s. Requires pharmacies to verify that the patient has a documented medical necessity.
  • Florida: Requires 503B facilities to submit monthly reports on semaglutide production volumes and patient counts.
  • Ohio: Prohibits compounding of semaglutide except when the prescriber documents a specific clinical need (e.g., allergy to the branded product).
  • Louisiana: Requires pharmacies to obtain explicit informed consent from patients about the risks and legal status of compounded semaglutide.

The Novo Nordisk Litigation Landscape

Novo Nordisk has been aggressive in protecting its semaglutide patents. Between 2023 and 2025, the company filed over 15 lawsuits against compounding pharmacies and outsourcing facilities. The results have been decisive:

  • At least 3 permanent injunctions were granted in 2025, permanently barring specific facilities from producing semaglutide-containing products.
  • Multiple facilities have settled, agreeing to pay undisclosed sums and cease production.
  • Novo Nordisk has also targeted telehealth companies that facilitate compounded semaglutide prescriptions.

What Injunctions Mean for Prescribers

When a court issues an injunction against a compounding pharmacy, that pharmacy must immediately cease production. This creates a clinical problem for you: your patients may suddenly lose access to their medication. But it also creates a legal problem: if you continue to prescribe from a pharmacy that is under an injunction, you are arguably facilitating a violation of a court order.

The practical guidance here is straightforward: maintain a current list of pharmacies that have been subject to FDA warning letters or court injunctions, and do not refer patients to them. The FDA maintains a public database of warning letters, and court records are searchable. This due diligence is part of your standard of care.

Real-World Example: In late 2025, a 503B outsourcing facility in Florida was hit with a permanent injunction after Novo Nordisk proved the facility was producing semaglutide beyond the scope of the shortage exemption. Prescribers who had referred patients to that facility received no advance notice—they only found out when patients called asking why their medication was unavailable.

Clinical and Liability Risk for Prescribers

Your legal exposure as a prescriber is the most actionable angle for your practice. Here's what you need to know:

Malpractice Exposure

Prescribing compounded semaglutide carries inherent malpractice risk, which increases now that the shortage is resolved. The key legal questions in any malpractice case would be:

  • Was there a documented medical necessity for using a compounded product instead of the FDA-approved version?
  • Did the patient provide informed consent, acknowledging the product is not FDA-approved and the safety data is less robust?
  • Did you verify the pharmacy's compliance status before prescribing?
  • Did you monitor the patient appropriately for adverse effects?

If you cannot answer "yes" to all four questions, your malpractice exposure is significant. The standard of care in 2026 will be defined by what a reasonable, informed prescriber does—and that standard increasingly includes verifying pharmacy compliance and documenting medical necessity.

FDA Warning Letters to Telehealth Companies

The FDA has not just targeted pharmacies. Between 2023 and 2025, the agency issued over 40 warning letters to compounding pharmacies and telehealth companies. These letters often name the prescribers associated with the company, creating a public record that plaintiffs' attorneys can use in litigation.

If you prescribe through a telehealth platform, you need to verify that the platform itself is in compliance. The FDA has shut down several telehealth operations that were facilitating compounded semaglutide prescriptions without proper oversight.

Insurance and Coverage Issues

From a practical standpoint, insurance coverage for compounded semaglutide is nearly nonexistent. According to 2025 Pharmacy Benefit Management Institute data:

This creates a two-tier system: patients who can afford brand-name semaglutide or have good insurance get the FDA-approved product, while others turn to compounding—often without fully understanding the legal and safety implications.

The Adulteration Problem: Safety Data You Cannot Ignore

A 2024 FDA sampling study found that approximately 12% of tested compounded semaglutide products failed potency or purity standards. The results, released in October 2025, showed that some products contained significantly less semaglutide than labeled, while others contained impurities.

This is not just a regulatory issue—it is a clinical safety issue. If you prescribe a compounded product that fails potency standards, your patient may not receive adequate therapeutic effect. If the product is contaminated, your patient could suffer serious harm. And if any adverse outcome occurs, the legal liability will rest on you, the prescriber.

Decision Framework: Should You Prescribe Compounded Semaglutide in 2026?

Given the legal landscape, here is a practical decision framework for your practice:

Step 1: Check the Patient's Payer Status

If your patient is on Medicare or Medicaid, stop immediately. Compounded GLP-1s are explicitly denied coverage, and prescribing them to federal program beneficiaries raises potential False Claims Act issues. Do not prescribe compounded semaglutide to Medicare or Medicaid patients, period.

Step 2: Verify the Pharmacy's Legal Status

Before prescribing from any compounding pharmacy, verify:

  • Does the pharmacy hold a valid 503B registration with the FDA (if it is an outsourcing facility)?
  • Is the pharmacy licensed in your state?
  • Has the pharmacy received any FDA warning letters in the past 24 months?
  • Has the pharmacy been subject to any court injunctions?
  • Does the pharmacy provide certificate of analysis (COA) for each batch of semaglutide?

If you cannot verify all five items, do not prescribe from that pharmacy.

Step 3: Document Medical Necessity

For each patient, document in the medical record why the compounded version is medically necessary. Valid reasons include:

  • Documented allergy to an inactive ingredient in Ozempic or Wegovy.
  • Need for a dosage strength not available in the FDA-approved product.
  • Intolerance to the branded product's delivery device.

Financial reasons—"the patient cannot afford Ozempic"—are not a valid medical necessity under FDA rules. This is a critical distinction that many prescribers miss.

Step 4: Obtain Written Informed Consent

Have the patient sign a written informed consent form that explicitly states:

  • The product is not FDA-approved.
  • The product is compounded and may differ in potency or purity from the branded version.
  • The product is not covered by insurance.
  • The prescribing provider has explained alternative FDA-approved options.

3. Offer FDA-Approved Alternatives

Present all FDA-approved options:

If a patient insists on continuing compounded semaglutide despite your recommendation for an FDA-approved alternative, document that discussion and have the patient sign a refusal-of-treatment form.

5. Communicate the Timeline

Tell your patients now that the compounding window is closing. Do not wait until the last minute. The grandfathering window is finite, and once it expires, pharmacies will stop filling these prescriptions regardless of what you write.

FAQ: Compounded Semaglutide Legality in 2026

Q: Is compounded semaglutide still legal in 2026 now that the shortage is over?

A: The legal authority for bulk compounding of semaglutide expired when the FDA declared the shortage resolved in December 2025. A 60–90 day transition period allows existing patients to continue their prescriptions, but new prescriptions for compounded semaglutide generally do not meet the legal standard unless there is a documented medical necessity (e.g., allergy to an inactive ingredient). After the transition window closes, bulk compounding of semaglutide will be considered non-compliant with FDA regulations.

Q: Can I legally prescribe compounded semaglutide to my patients if Novo Nordisk has a patent?

A: The patent issue is separate from the compounding issue. The FDA's shortage list status is what determines whether compounding is allowed. However, Novo Nordisk's aggressive litigation against compounders means that working with a pharmacy that is under a court injunction is not viable. You can prescribe compounded semaglutide only if the pharmacy is compliant with FDA and state regulations, the patient has a documented medical necessity for the compounded version, and the prescription is filled during the transition window or under a recognized exception.

Q: What happens to my existing compounded semaglutide patients if the FDA issues a new enforcement policy?

A: You need a contingency plan. The FDA may issue new enforcement guidance at any time, and state boards may act faster. Your plan should include: identifying all patients on compounded semaglutide, transitioning them to FDA-approved alternatives (Ozempic, Wegovy, Zepbound, or Mounjaro), documenting all discussions, and obtaining informed consent if a patient insists on continuing the compounded product. Do not wait for enforcement action to start this process.

Q: What's the difference between a 503A pharmacy and a 503B facility—which should I work with?

A: A 503A pharmacy compounds patient-specific prescriptions and is primarily regulated by state pharmacy boards. A 503B facility is a registered outsourcing facility that produces drugs in bulk and is subject to FDA inspection and cGMP requirements. For compounded semaglutide in 2026, neither pathway provides a safe harbor for bulk production now that the shortage is resolved. If you must use a compounding pharmacy, a 503B facility with FDA registration and a clean inspection history is generally the safer choice from a quality assurance standpoint, but you must verify their current legal status before prescribing.

Q: If I prescribe compounded semaglutide and something goes wrong, am I personally liable?

A: Yes, you can be held personally liable. Malpractice claims, state board disciplinary actions, and even civil suits from patients can name you individually. The FDA's warning letters to telehealth companies frequently identify prescribers by name, and plaintiffs' attorneys can use this public record in litigation. Your liability is significantly reduced if you: document medical necessity, obtain written informed consent, verify pharmacy compliance, and monitor patients appropriately. If you prescribe outside the legal framework, your personal exposure is substantial.

Q: How do I verify that a compounding pharmacy is compliant with FDA and state regulations?

A: Start with the FDA's public database of warning letters and the FDA's list of registered outsourcing facilities. Check your state pharmacy board's website for license verification and disciplinary actions. Ask the pharmacy for their most recent inspection reports and batch-level certificates of analysis. Verify that the pharmacy has not been subject to any court injunctions related to semaglutide production. This verification process is part of your standard of care—document that you performed it.
