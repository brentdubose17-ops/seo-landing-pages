The 2026 Legal Status of Compounded Semaglutide: A Compliance Playbook for Providers

Compounded semaglutide has swung from a critical shortage solution to a legally precarious product, back to a temporarily protected one, and now sits in a state of appellate limbo. As of Q1 2026, the FDA’s appeal to the 9th Circuit in Outsourcing Facilities Association v. The FDA Shortage Status: A Timeline That Refuses to Settle

To understand the current legality, you must understand the ping-pong match between the FDA and the Outsourcing Facilities Association (OFA). Here is the precise timeline every provider needs to know:

  • March 2023: FDA places semaglutide on the drug shortage list due to manufacturing capacity issues at Novo Nordisk.
  • October 2, 2024: FDA declares the shortage resolved, citing increased manufacturing capacity. This immediately restricted 503B outsourcing facilities from making "essentially a copy" of semaglutide.
  • May 14, 2025: The U.S. District Court for the Western District of Texas rules in favor of the OFA, vacating the FDA's shortage termination. The court found the FDA's process flawed, effectively reinstating the shortage status and allowing compounding to resume.
  • Late 2025 – Q1 2026: FDA appeals the decision to the 9th Circuit. The appeal is pending, and no stay has been granted, meaning the district court's ruling (shortage active) is the operative legal reality as of this writing.

This is the crux of the 2026 "gray zone." Legally, the shortage is active due to the court ruling. Practically, the FDA still believes the shortage is over and has signaled it will enforce aggressively once the appeal concludes. You are operating in a window where the legal status is technically favorable to compounding, but the regulatory winds are blowing toward restriction.

Why the Shortage Status Matters Legally

The shortage status is the linchpin for 503B facilities. Under the FD&C Act, outsourcing facilities can only compound a drug that is "essentially a copy" of an FDA-approved drug if the drug is on the shortage list. When the FDA declared the shortage over in October 2024, 503Bs were legally barred from bulk production. The May 2025 court ruling vacated that decision, effectively turning the clock back to "shortage active."

For 503A pharmacies (traditional compounding), the shortage status is less critical but still relevant. 503A pharmacies are allowed to compound patient-specific prescriptions regardless of shortage status, provided they do not make "essentially a copy" of a commercially available product—unless there is a "significant difference" for the patient. The EAC guidance, which we will dissect next, is where most compliance failures occur.

The "Essentially a Copy" (EAC) Provision: The Devil in the Details

On September 30, 2024, the FDA released its final guidance, "Compounding and the FDA: Questions and Answers," which included a crucial section on the EAC definition. This is the legal battleground for 2026. The guidance states that a compounded drug is "essentially a copy" if it has the same API, same strength, same dosage form, and same route of administration as an FDA-approved drug, AND there is no "clinical difference" for the patient.

Here is where the "B12 loophole" comes under fire. Many compounding pharmacies have added vitamin B12, B6, or L-carnitine to semaglutide to argue it is not a copy. The FDA explicitly rejects this if the purpose is merely to circumvent the EAC rule. The guidance states that a "change in inactive ingredients" does not prevent a drug from being a copy unless it produces a "clinical difference" relevant to the patient's treatment.

So, is semaglutide with B12 legal? The answer is: only if you can document a medical necessity for the B12 (e.g., the patient has a documented B12 deficiency and oral supplementation is ineffective). If you are adding B12 solely to game the legal system, you are exposing yourself to significant liability.

The Salt Form Argument: Semaglutide Sodium vs. Semaglutide Base

A more sophisticated argument involves using different salt forms. Brand-name Ozempic uses semaglutide (base). Some compounders use semaglutide sodium or semaglutide acetate. The FDA's guidance is clear: a different salt form does not automatically exempt a drug from EAC status if the active moiety is the same and the pharmacokinetics are similar. The FDA considers the "active ingredient" to be the semaglutide molecule itself. In 2025, the FDA issued multiple warning letters to pharmacies using semaglutide sodium, stating that this is not a "different drug" but merely a different salt, and thus still a copy.

For 2026, assume that any semaglutide salt form is treated as a copy. The only legally defensible compounding scenario is a patient-specific prescription where the prescriber documents a specific clinical reason why the brand drug is inappropriate (e.g., allergy to an inactive ingredient in Ozempic) and why the compounded version with a specific additive is medically necessary.

Patient Safety Data: The Enforcement Driver

Regulatory action is not happening in a vacuum. The FDA's adverse event reporting system (FAERS) has been a primary driver of enforcement. As of mid-2025, public FDA presentations cited over 1,100 adverse event reports involving compounded semaglutide, including approximately 100 hospitalizations and 10+ deaths.

These reports include dosing errors (patients injecting 10x the intended dose due to confusing concentration labels), infections from non-sterile preparations, and adverse reactions to unknown additives. While the absolute number of events is small relative to the millions of prescriptions, the severity and the media coverage have fueled state board investigations.

For the prescriber, this data is a liability red flag. If a patient has an adverse event, the plaintiff's attorney will subpoena your records to see if you verified the pharmacy's license, the patient-specific nature of the script, and whether you documented the medical necessity for the compounded version over the brand. If you cannot produce that documentation, you are exposed.

The 2026 Compliance Roadmap for Providers

Here is the actionable framework. The "gray zone" is not a reason to panic; it is a reason to formalize your workflow. Use these four gates as a checklist for every single compounded semaglutide prescription you write in 2026.

Gate 1: Verify the Shortage Status (Today)

Check the FDA's drug shortage database before every new prescription. As of May 2026, the database lists semaglutide as "available" per the FDA, but the court ruling overrides that. Do not rely on memory; check the FDA shortage list and document the date you checked. If the shortage is active (per the court), 503B compounding is legal. If the FDA wins the appeal and the shortage is terminated, 503B compounding must stop immediately.

Gate 2: Verify the Pharmacy's License and Compliance

Do not prescribe to a pharmacy just because they have a website. Verify their state license, check for FDA warning letters on the FDA's website, and confirm they are either a 503A (patient-specific) or 503B (bulk) facility. Ask for their most recent inspection report. If they cannot provide it, move on. In 2026, the pharmacy bears the primary regulatory burden, but you share the liability.

Gate 3: Ensure the Prescription is Patient-Specific

This is non-negotiable. A 503A pharmacy cannot legally compound semaglutide without a valid, patient-specific prescription. This means you must write the prescription for a specific patient, with a specific dose, and the pharmacy must have that script on file before mixing. Do not send a "standing order" for a clinic to keep vials in stock. That is bulk manufacturing, and it is illegal.

Gate 4: Document the "Clinical Difference" (EAC Defense)

If you are prescribing compounded semaglutide for a patient who could take Ozempic, you need a documented reason. Examples that hold up to scrutiny:

  • Patient has a documented allergy to phenol (an inactive ingredient in Ozempic).
  • Patient has a B12 deficiency and cannot absorb oral supplements.
  • Patient requires a specific dose not available in the brand injector pens (e.g., 0.05 mg starting dose for a sensitive patient).

If the only reason is cost, that is not a clinical difference. The FDA guidance is explicit that cost is not a valid reason for EAC exemption. In 2026, prescribers who write "financial hardship" on the script are taking on significant risk.

The Dual-Scenario Playbook: Surviving the 9th Circuit Ruling

The biggest strategic error you can make is building your practice on the assumption that the court ruling will hold. Here is how to structure your practice to survive both outcomes.

Scenario A: FDA Wins the Appeal (Shortage Declared Over)

If the 9th Circuit reverses the district court, the FDA will almost immediately remove semaglutide from the shortage list. The moment that happens, 503B facilities must stop bulk production of "essentially a copy" products. 503A pharmacies can still compound patient-specific scripts, but only with the clinical difference documentation discussed above.

Your pivot: If you rely heavily on compounded semaglutide, you need a brand strategy. Here are your options:

  • Brand with Coupons: Novo Nordisk offers savings cards that can reduce out-of-pocket costs to $25/month for those with commercial insurance. For cash-pay patients, the list price is $935–$1,349, but many pharmacies offer discount programs.

  • Alternative GLP-1s: Tirzepatide (Mounjaro/Zepbound) is also on the shortage list intermittently. You can pivot to prescribing brand tirzepatide, which has a similar efficacy profile.

Scenario B: OFA Wins (Shortage Remains Active)

If the OFA prevails in the 9th Circuit, the shortage status remains active, and compounding can continue under the current rules. However, do not expect the FDA to relent. They will likely issue stricter guidance on what constitutes a "copy" and increase inspections of compounding facilities.

Your pivot: Scale your compounding practice safely. This means:

  • Building relationships with 2-3 verified 503B facilities to ensure supply chain redundancy.
  • Implementing a robust adverse event reporting protocol. If any patient has an issue, report it to FAERS immediately. This protects you and provides the FDA with data that may influence future policy.
  • Documenting every single prescription with the EAC defense rationale. Do not take shortcuts.

Insurance, PBMs, and the Cash-Pay Trap

Most legal analyses ignore the insurance angle, but it is critical to your exposure. In 2025, major Pharmacy Benefit Managers (PBMs) like CVS Caremark and Express Scripts began denying claims for compounded semaglutide, classifying it as "not medically necessary" or "unapproved." This means most patients are paying cash out-of-pocket.

Cash-pay medicine triggers a different set of state consumer protection laws. In California, SB 272 requires specific disclosures for cash-pay prescriptions, including informing the patient that the compounded product is not FDA-approved and that they have the right to request the brand name. Failure to provide these disclosures could result in fines and civil liability.

Even in states without specific cash-pay laws, the lack of an insurance claim means you are the sole "gatekeeper" of the medical necessity. This increases your duty of care. You must ensure the patient understands the risks, the lack of FDA approval, and the potential for dosing errors. Get informed consent in writing, specifically for the compounded product, not just a general consent to treat.

Telehealth Prescribing and Interstate Licensure

If you prescribe via telehealth, you have an additional layer of complexity. The DEA's rules on controlled substances do not apply here (semaglutide is not controlled), but state medical boards do. Some states, like Texas and Florida, have specific requirements for telehealth prescribing of weight-loss medications, including a physical exam or in-person visit requirement.

More importantly, the pharmacy you use must be licensed in the state where the patient is located, not just where the pharmacy is physically located. Many states require the pharmacy to have a "non-resident" license to ship into the state. If the pharmacy does not have that license, you are facilitating an illegal transaction.

Before prescribing across state lines, verify:

  1. The pharmacy holds a non-resident license in the patient's state.
  2. Your medical license allows telehealth prescribing in that state.
  3. The state pharmacy board has not issued a specific ban or restriction on compounded semaglutide.

FAQ: Compounded Semaglutide Legality in 2026

Q: Is compounded semaglutide still legal in 2026 after the FDA declared the shortage over?

A: Yes, it is currently legal due to the May 2025 federal court ruling that vacated the FDA's shortage termination. The FDA has appealed, and the 9th Circuit decision is pending. As of Q1 2026, the shortage is considered "active" per the court, allowing 503B facilities to compound. However, this is a temporary window. You must operate as if the scarcity could end at any moment.

Q: What is the difference between 503A and 503B compounding pharmacies, and which can legally make semaglutide?

A: A 503A pharmacy compounds patient-specific prescriptions based on a valid prescription from a provider. They cannot legally produce large batches of "pre-made" semaglutide. A 503B outsourcing facility can produce bulk quantities without a patient-specific prescription, but only if the drug is on the FDA shortage list and is not an "essentially a copy." In 2026, both can make semaglutide, but 503Bs are at higher risk of FDA enforcement if the appeal fails.

Q: Can I legally prescribe compounded semaglutide for weight loss if the patient has never used Ozempic?

A: Yes, you can prescribe it for a new patient, but you must document why the compounded version is clinically necessary over the brand. For a brand-new patient, this is harder to justify unless they have a specific allergy or intolerance to the brand's inactive ingredients. If the patient is simply uninsured and cannot afford Ozempic's $935/month list price, that is a financial reason, not a clinical one—and it does not meet the EAC exemption standard.

Q: What happens if the FDA wins the 9th Circuit appeal—will compounding stop immediately?

A: Not immediately, but quickly. If the FDA wins, they will remove semaglutide from the shortage list. 503B facilities must cease bulk production immediately upon the FDA's official announcement. 503A pharmacies can continue compounding patient-specific scripts, but they will be subject to immediate FDA inspections to ensure compliance with the EAC guidance. Expect a 30-60 day enforcement grace period, but do not rely on it.

Q: Are compounded versions with B12 or other additives actually legal, or is that a loophole the FDA rejects?

A: The FDA explicitly rejects the "B12 loophole." Adding B12, B6, or L-carnitine does not automatically make the drug a "non-copy" unless there is a documented clinical difference for the patient. If you prescribe semaglutide+B12 for a patient with normal B12 levels, you are prescribing an illegal copy. The only safe path is to document a specific medical need for the additive.

Q: What liability do I face if a patient has an adverse event from a compounded product?

A: You face civil liability (malpractice) and potential board disciplinary action. If a patient is hospitalized due to a dosing error or infection, the plaintiff's attorney will review your documentation. Without proof that you verified the pharmacy's license, the patient-specific nature of the script, and the clinical necessity, you will be found negligent. Additionally, some states require you to report adverse events to the state board; failure to do so can result in license suspension.
