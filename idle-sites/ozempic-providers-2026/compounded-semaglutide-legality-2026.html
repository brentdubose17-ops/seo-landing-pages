The FDA Regulatory Shift: From Shortage to Full Enforcement

The legal foundation for compounding semaglutide rested entirely on its shortage status. Under Section 503A (for patient-specific compounding) and Section 503B (for bulk production) of the FD&C Act, pharmacies could legally compound a drug that was on the FDA’s shortage list. That justification has been systematically dismantled.

The timeline is critical. The FDA removed semaglutide from the shortage list for the 0.25 mg and 0.5 mg strengths on February 21, 2025. The remaining strengths (1.0 mg and above) were removed on May 28, 2025. By the start of 2026, the shortage is fully resolved. There is no longer any federal exemption that allows compounding pharmacies to produce semaglutide in bulk without a valid, patient-specific prescription that includes documented clinical justification.

The FDA’s position is unambiguous. In their final guidance issued in late 2025, the agency clarified that compounded semaglutide can only be prescribed when the brand-name drug (Ozempic or Wegovy) is not "clinically appropriate" for the patient. This is a high bar. It requires more than a patient’s preference for a lower price or a desire for a different delivery device. It requires a documented medical reason why the FDA-approved version cannot be used.

The "Essentially a Copy" vs. "Necessary" Distinction

Under FDA rules, a compounded drug that is "essentially a copy" of an approved drug cannot be produced unless there is a documented "clinical need." The FDA’s interpretation of clinical need is narrow. It includes scenarios such as:

  • A documented allergy or hypersensitivity to an inactive ingredient in Ozempic or Wegovy (e.g., sodium phosphate, phenol).
  • A need for a dosage strength or form that is not commercially available (e.g., a dose lower than 0.25 mg for pediatric patients or patients with severe renal impairment).
  • A medical condition that requires a different route of administration (e.g., oral semaglutide compounded for patients with severe injection-site reactions).

Critically, financial reasons do not qualify. If a patient’s insurance denies coverage for brand-name semaglutide and they cannot afford the $900–$1,300 monthly list price, that is not a legal basis for compounding. This is the most common misconception we see among providers, and it is the primary reason the FDA and state boards have issued hundreds of warning letters over the past 18 months.

Third-Party Testing and Purity Requirements: USP <797> and <795>

The compounding industry’s reputation took a significant hit in 2025. In June 2025, two major compounding pharmacies—one in Texas and one in Florida—recalled over 12,000 vials of compounded semaglutide due to confirmed contamination with endotoxins and particulate matter. This followed a series of FDA inspections that found significant deviations from USP <797> (sterile compounding) and USP <795> (non-sterile compounding) standards.

In response, the FDA issued new guidance requiring 100% batch testing for all compounded GLP-1 medications. This means that every single batch produced by a 503A or 503B pharmacy must undergo third-party testing for purity, potency, sterility, and endotoxin levels. The pharmacy must provide a Certificate of Analysis (CoA) for each batch, and this CoA must be available to the prescriber upon request.

For providers, this creates a new due diligence obligation. Before you prescribe compounded semaglutide from any pharmacy, you should verify:

  • The pharmacy is registered as a 503A or 503B facility with the FDA.
  • The pharmacy has a current, unexpired FDA inspection status (check for Form 483s on the FDA’s website).
  • The pharmacy can provide a batch-specific CoA from an independent, ISO-accredited laboratory.
  • The pharmacy sources its raw API (semaglutide sodium) from a supplier with a valid Drug Master File (DMF) on file with the FDA. Avoid pharmacies that source from unregistered Chinese suppliers—this is the "gray market" and it is illegal under FD&C Act Section 501(a)(2)(B).
Expert Tip: Request the CoA for every batch you prescribe from. If the pharmacy hesitates or cannot provide it, that is a red flag. In 2026, prescribing from a non-compliant pharmacy is a direct liability for you, not just the pharmacy.

The "Clinical Necessity Documentation" Gap: Your Shield in 2026

Here is the unique angle that most competitors miss. The single biggest liability for Ozempic providers in 2026 is not whether you prescribe compounded semaglutide—it is how you document the medical necessity. The FDA and state boards are not looking for a checkbox. They are looking for a comprehensive, auditable clinical narrative that justifies why the brand drug cannot be used.

To survive an FDA audit or state board investigation, your patient chart must include the following elements. This is your documentation framework.

1. Specific ICD-10 Codes and Clinical Justification

Your chart notes must include the relevant ICD-10 codes that support the medical necessity of a compounded drug. For semaglutide, these include:

  • E11.9 (Type 2 diabetes without complications)
  • E66.01 (Morbid obesity due to excess calories)
  • E66.09 (Other obesity due to excess calories)
  • Z79.899 (Long-term drug therapy, other)

But codes alone are insufficient. You must write a narrative that explains why Ozempic or Wegovy is not clinically appropriate. For example: "Patient has a documented allergy to phenol, an inactive ingredient in Ozempic. Compounded semaglutide without phenol is necessary to avoid anaphylactic risk." This is a defensible clinical need.

2. Allergy Documentation

If you are prescribing compounded semaglutide due to an allergy or intolerance to an inactive ingredient in the brand product, you must document this with specificity. Include the exact ingredient, the patient’s reaction history, and any prior allergy testing or referral to an allergist. A vague note like "patient prefers compounded" will not withstand scrutiny.

3. Failed Brand Therapy Notes

If you are prescribing compounded semaglutide because the patient failed brand therapy, you must document the details. What dose did they take? For how long? What adverse effects did they experience? What was the clinical outcome? For example: "Patient titrated to 1.0 mg Ozempic over 8 weeks. Experienced severe gastrointestinal intolerance (vomiting, diarrhea) requiring discontinuation. Compounded semaglutide at 0.5 mg with a slower titration schedule is clinically necessary." This is a defensible position.

4. Dosing Flexibility Justification

The brand-name Ozempic pens are fixed-dose (0.25 mg, 0.5 mg, 1.0 mg, 2.0 mg). If a patient needs a dose of 0.75 mg or 1.5 mg, the brand product cannot provide it. This is a legitimate clinical need for compounding. Document the specific dose required and why the fixed-dose pens are inadequate. For example, a patient with severe renal impairment may need a dose of 0.25 mg every 10 days, which is not possible with a fixed-dose pen designed for weekly administration.

5. Documentation of Pharmacy Verification

Your chart should include a note confirming that you verified the compounding pharmacy’s 503B registration, current FDA inspection status, and batch-specific CoA. This demonstrates that you performed due diligence, which is a strong defense if the pharmacy is later found to be non-compliant.

Use this decision tree to evaluate every patient scenario before prescribing compounded semaglutide. If you answer "No" to any of the critical questions, do not prescribe the compounded version.

If you answered "No" to steps 2, 3, and 4, and the only reason for compounding is cost or patient preference, you must not prescribe compounded semaglutide. This is the clear legal line in 2026.

Transitioning Existing Patients: The Grandfathering Myth

If you have patients who have been stable on compounded semaglutide for years, you might assume they are "grandfathered" into legality. This is incorrect. There is no grandfather clause in the FDA’s shortage resolution. Every prescription for compounded semaglutide written after the shortage ended must meet the new, stricter clinical necessity requirements.

For existing patients, you have two options. The first is to transition them to brand-name semaglutide. This is the safest and most straightforward path. The second is to re-evaluate each patient and document a new clinical need that meets the 2026 standards. For example, if a patient has been on a 1.5 mg dose for 18 months and the brand pens only offer 1.0 mg or 2.0 mg, you can document that the 1.5 mg dose is necessary for optimal glycemic control and that the brand product cannot provide this dose. This is a legitimate clinical need.

However, do not assume this documentation is easy. You must conduct a new patient evaluation, update the chart, and have the patient sign an informed consent form that explicitly states they are receiving a compounded medication that is not FDA-approved. This informed consent is critical. It does not absolve you of liability, but it demonstrates transparency and reduces the risk of a successful lawsuit.
