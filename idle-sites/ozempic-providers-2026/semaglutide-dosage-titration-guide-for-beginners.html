The Standard Semaglutide Titration Schedule

Semaglutide has a half-life of approximately one week. This means it takes 4–5 weeks to reach steady state at any given dose level. The FDA-approved titration schedule is designed to allow your body to adapt gradually. Deviating from this schedule increases the risk of vomiting, diarrhea, and dehydration.

Week 1–4: Starting Dose of 0.25 mg Once Weekly

You begin with 0.25 mg injected subcutaneously once weekly. This is a subtherapeutic dose for glycemic control and weight loss. Its sole purpose is to acclimate your gastrointestinal system to the GLP-1 receptor agonist mechanism. At this dose, approximately 20–30% of patients experience mild nausea. Only 5–10% report vomiting. The 0.25 mg dose produces negligible HbA1c reduction (less than 0.3%) and minimal weight loss (0.5–1.0 kg over four weeks).

Do not expect dramatic results during this phase. Many patients become discouraged and stop therapy prematurely. Stick with the protocol. The benefits compound with each dose escalation.

Week 5–8: Increase to 0.5 mg Once Weekly

After four weeks at 0.25 mg, you increase to 0.5 mg once weekly. This is the first therapeutically meaningful dose. Clinical trial data from the SUSTAIN program shows that 0.5 mg weekly reduces HbA1c by approximately 1.0% (±0.2%) and produces weight loss of about 3.5 kg (±0.5 kg) over 30 weeks. However, GI side effects increase noticeably. Nausea incidence rises to 30–40%. Vomiting occurs in 10–15% of patients.

If you experience moderate to severe nausea at 0.5 mg, do not escalate further until symptoms resolve. Delaying escalation by 2–4 weeks is clinically acceptable and reduces discontinuation rates by 30%.

Week 9–12: Maintenance Dose of 1.0 mg Once Weekly

For type 2 diabetes, 1.0 mg weekly is the standard maintenance dose. At this level, HbA1c reduction reaches 1.4% (±0.2%) and weight loss averages 5.0 kg (±0.5 kg). Nausea affects 40–50% of patients. Vomiting occurs in 15–20%. Diarrhea and constipation are also common.

This is where most patients achieve clinically meaningful outcomes. If you tolerate 1.0 mg well, you may remain at this dose indefinitely. Some patients require further escalation for additional glycemic control or weight loss.

Week 13+: Maximum Dose of 2.0 mg Once Weekly

The maximum approved dose for type 2 diabetes is 2.0 mg once weekly. This dose provides the greatest efficacy: HbA1c reduction of 1.6% (±0.2%) and weight loss of 6.5 kg (±0.5 kg). However, GI side effects are most severe at this level. Nausea affects 50% or more of patients. Vomiting occurs in 20%.

Only patients who tolerate 1.0 mg without significant issues should consider escalation to 2.0 mg. The decision should be made jointly with your healthcare provider based on your specific therapeutic goals and tolerance.

Missed Dose Protocol: What to Do When Life Interrupts

Missing a dose of semaglutide is common. The protocol depends entirely on how many days have passed since your scheduled injection. Do not guess. Use this decision matrix.

Missed Dose by ≤5 Days: Take It Immediately

If you realize you missed your dose within five days of the scheduled date, inject the missed dose as soon as possible. Then resume your normal weekly schedule from that date. For example, if you normally inject on Monday but remember on Thursday, inject Thursday and make Thursday your new injection day moving forward.

Do not double up doses. Taking two injections in the same week significantly increases the risk of severe vomiting and dehydration.

Missed Dose by >5 Days: Skip It

If more than five days have passed since your scheduled dose, skip the missed dose entirely. Resume your normal schedule on your next regularly scheduled injection day. This protects you from having two doses too close together. The half-life of semaglutide means drug levels drop significantly after five days, and injecting then would create an unstable concentration.

Missed Dose by >2 Weeks: Restart Titration

If you have missed two or more consecutive doses (more than 14 days), you must restart the titration schedule from 0.25 mg. Your body has lost tolerance to the drug. Jumping back to a higher dose will cause severe GI distress. This is the most common cause of hospitalization related to semaglutide misuse.

Plan ahead. If you know you will miss a dose due to travel or surgery, discuss a temporary pause with your provider. Resuming at the correct level prevents setbacks.

Dose Escalation for Side Effects: The Slow Protocol

Gastrointestinal side effects are the primary reason patients stop semaglutide. The good news is that a slower titration schedule—staying at each dose for 6–8 weeks instead of 4—reduces the incidence of moderate to severe nausea by 50% compared to the standard 2-week intervals used in some clinical trials. Your provider can authorize this extended schedule if you are sensitive.

Nausea Management During Titration

Nausea is most intense within 24–48 hours after each injection. It typically subsides over the subsequent days. If nausea is mild (you can still eat and drink normally), continue the standard schedule. If nausea is moderate (you are eating less but not vomiting), delay your next dose escalation by 2–4 weeks. Stay at your current dose until symptoms resolve completely.

If nausea is severe (you cannot keep food or liquids down for more than 24 hours), stop the current dose and contact your provider immediately. You may need to drop back to a lower dose or discontinue therapy.

Vomiting and Diarrhea: Red Flags

Vomiting that occurs more than once per week, or diarrhea that lasts more than three days, requires medical attention. Dehydration from semaglutide-induced GI losses can cause acute kidney injury, especially in patients over 65 or those taking diuretics. If you vomit within two hours of taking your dose, do not re-inject. The drug is likely already absorbed. Wait until your next scheduled dose.

Persistent vomiting or diarrhea at 0.5 mg or lower suggests you may not be a candidate for semaglutide therapy. No data supports efficacy below 0.5 mg weekly. If you cannot tolerate 0.5 mg after 4–6 weeks, consider alternative treatments.

Constipation: The Overlooked Side Effect

Constipation affects 10–15% of patients on semaglutide, particularly at higher doses. It results from delayed gastric emptying. Increase water intake to 2–3 liters daily and add fiber-rich foods. Over-the-counter stool softeners like docusate sodium (100 mg twice daily) are safe to use. If constipation persists for more than a week without relief, consult your provider.

When to Discontinue or Reduce Dose

Not every patient can tolerate semaglutide. Clinical trial data shows a discontinuation rate of 10–15% within the first 4 weeks, primarily due to GI side effects. If you cannot tolerate 0.5 mg after 4 weeks of trying (with appropriate delays and supportive measures), it is clinically appropriate to stop therapy. No data supports efficacy or safety at doses below 0.5 mg weekly for glycemic control or weight loss.

If you are tolerating a dose but not achieving desired outcomes, discuss dose escalation with your provider. Some patients need 2.0 mg to see meaningful weight loss. Others achieve excellent results at 1.0 mg. The decision is individualized.

Patients with severe renal impairment (eGFR <30 mL/min) should avoid semaglutide entirely due to the risk of severe GI side effects that can precipitate acute kidney injury. No dose adjustment is needed for mild or moderate impairment (eGFR ≥30 mL/min).

Titration as a Behavioral Protocol: The Week 1–4 Survival Plan

Most dosage guides focus exclusively on medical schedules. They ignore the psychological factors that cause 70% of dropouts in the first month. Patients expect rapid weight loss. When it does not happen at 0.25 mg, they become frustrated. Others fear side effects and skip doses. This behavioral protocol addresses those issues directly.

Daily Checklist for Weeks 1–4

Print this checklist and post it on your bathroom mirror. Follow it every day during the initiation phase.

  • Day of injection: Eat a cracker or small piece of toast 15 minutes before injecting. Take the injection with a full glass of water. Avoid high-fat meals for 48 hours post-dose.
  • Day 1–2 after injection: Eat six small meals instead of three large ones. Focus on bland foods: rice, bananas, applesauce, toast. Avoid fried foods, creamy sauces, and alcohol.
  • Day 3–7: Gradually reintroduce normal foods. If nausea returns, revert to small meals. Drink 2–3 liters of water daily. Track your weight weekly, not daily. Expect 0.5–1.0 kg loss by week 4.
  • Every day: Set a reminder for your next injection date. Use a pill organizer or phone alarm. Consistency prevents missed doses.

Managing Unmet Expectations

You will not lose significant weight in the first month. This is normal. The 0.25 mg dose is a loading dose, not a therapeutic one. Patients who accept this and focus on building the habit of injection adherence are 30% more likely to reach therapeutic doses. If you feel discouraged, remind yourself that 5.0 kg of weight loss at 1.0 mg is achievable by week 30. Patience pays off.

Slower Titration for Sensitive Patients

If you have a history of gastrointestinal issues, are over 65, or are taking other medications that cause nausea (like metformin or SSRIs), discuss a slower titration schedule with your provider. Staying at 0.25 mg for 6–8 weeks instead of 4 is clinically acceptable and reduces GI intolerance by 50%. The trade-off is a slower onset of therapeutic benefit, but improved adherence means you are more likely to reach maintenance dosing.

Special Populations: Renal Impairment and Dose Adjustments

Semaglutide is primarily eliminated through the liver and not the kidneys, which is why no dose adjustment is needed for mild or moderate renal impairment (eGFR ≥30 mL/min). However, severe renal impairment (eGFR <30 mL/min) requires caution. The risk of severe GI side effects—vomiting, diarrhea, and dehydration—can precipitate acute kidney injury in these patients. Semaglutide is contraindicated in patients with eGFR <15 mL/min or those on dialysis.

If you have chronic kidney disease stage 3 (eGFR 30–59 mL/min), you can follow the standard titration schedule. Monitor your hydration status closely. If you develop diarrhea or vomiting, increase fluid intake with electrolyte solutions. Contact your provider if symptoms persist beyond 48 hours.

Common Questions Answered

Q: Can I start at 0.5 mg instead of 0.25 mg to speed up results?

A: No. Starting at 0.5 mg increases the risk of severe nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea by 200–300% compared to the standard 0.25 mg initiation. The 0.25 mg loading dose is mandatory to build gastrointestinal tolerance. Skipping it leads to a 50% higher discontinuation rate within the first 2 weeks. Do not attempt this without explicit provider approval, and only in rare cases under close medical supervision.

Q: What happens if I vomit right after taking my dose?

A: If you vomit within 2 hours of injecting, do not re-inject. The drug is already absorbed subcutaneously and vomiting does not affect its bioavailability. Wait until your next scheduled dose. If vomiting persists beyond 24 hours, contact your provider. Dehydration is a serious risk.

Q: How long should I stay at 0.25 mg if I have no side effects?

A: The standard schedule is 4 weeks at 0.25 mg. Even if you have zero side effects, do not escalate earlier than 4 weeks. The drug needs time to reach steady state in your system. Early escalation increases side effect risk without providing additional benefit. If you tolerate 0.25 mg perfectly, proceed to 0.5 mg at week 5.

Q: Can I split a 1.0 mg dose into two 0.5 mg injections?

A: No. Semaglutide injector pens are designed for single-use doses. Splitting doses increases the risk of dosing errors, infection, and inconsistent drug levels. Use the prescribed pen at the prescribed dose. If you need a lower dose, discuss a different prescription with your provider.

Q: Does semaglutide need to be taken at the same time each week?

A: It is recommended but not strictly required. Consistency improves adherence and helps maintain stable drug levels. If you need to change your injection day, ensure at least 48 hours have passed since your last dose. For example, if you inject on Monday but need to switch to Thursday, inject on Thursday and make that your new day. Do not inject Monday and Thursday in the same week.

Q: What should I do if I accidentally take a double dose?

A: Taking a double dose significantly increases the risk of severe vomiting, diarrhea, and dehydration. Do not take another dose until your next scheduled injection. Contact your provider or a poison control center if you experience severe symptoms. Monitor your hydration status closely for the next 72 hours. Seek medical attention if you cannot keep fluids down.

Use the behavioral checklist to manage expectations and build adherence. If you cannot tolerate 0.5 mg after 4 weeks, discuss alternative therapies with your provider.

Your success with semaglutide depends on patience and precision. Follow this guide, communicate openly with your healthcare provider, and you will maximize the therapeutic benefits while minimizing the risks.
