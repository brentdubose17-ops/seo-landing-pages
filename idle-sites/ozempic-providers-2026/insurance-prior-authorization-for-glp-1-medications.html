Step-by-Step: The Prior Authorization Submission Process

Knowing the exact workflow saves time and prevents errors. Here is the standard sequence you should follow for every GLP-1 prescription, based on CMS and commercial payer guidelines.

Step 1: The 48-Hour "Pre-PA" Verification

Most providers make the mistake of writing the prescription first and submitting the PA second. This is backwards. Before you even e-prescribe, call the patient’s insurance plan or use your EHR’s eligibility tool to confirm the specific coverage criteria. Ask three questions:

  1. Is a PA required for Ozempic (or the specific GLP-1)?
  2. What is the exact BMI threshold? (e.g., ≥30, or ≥27 with comorbidity)
  3. Is step therapy required? (e.g., metformin trial of 90 days)

This "pre-verification" inquiry takes 10 minutes and reduces first-pass denials by up to 40%. It is the single highest-leverage action you can take. Document the date, time, and name of the insurance representative you spoke with in the patient’s chart.

Step 2: Gather the Clinical Documentation Bundle

Do not submit a bare PA form. Attach a clinical documentation bundle that includes:

  • BMI value with the date of measurement (must be within the last 12 months)
  • HbA1c result with the lab report date (for T2D indications)
  • Progress notes showing a 3–6 month trial of metformin (if step therapy applies)
  • Cardiovascular risk factor documentation (hypertension, dyslipidemia, ASCVD history)
  • Current medication list

Remember the "clinical documentation gap": a PA is denied when the BMI is listed but the date is missing, or when the HbA1c is mentioned but the actual lab report is not attached. Treat the PA as a legal submission—if it isn’t documented, it didn’t happen.

Step 3: Submit the PA Electronically

Use CoverMyMeds or your EHR’s integrated PA system. If you must use a paper form, ensure you use the payer-specific form (e.g., Caremark, Express Scripts, OptumRx). Submit via the portal for a timestamped record. Standard processing time is 72 hours for non-urgent requests, but automated approvals often clear in 1.1 business days (CAQH, 2023). For urgent cases (e.g., a patient with severe hyperglycemia), request expedited review—CMS mandates a 24-hour turnaround for urgent requests.

Step 4: Track the Submission

Set a calendar reminder for 72 hours after submission. If you haven't received a determination, call the payer’s provider line. Do not wait. A "pending" status is not an approval. The longer a PA sits in limbo, the more likely the patient abandons the prescription due to cost.

Step 5: The Peer-to-Peer Review (Before You Appeal)

This is the most underutilized tool in the PA process. Most providers don’t realize they can request a peer-to-peer (P2P) review with a payer physician before a formal appeal is filed. The P2P is a 15-minute phone call where you discuss the case with a medical director. It has a 60–70% overturn rate for GLP-1s because you are speaking to a physician, not a claims processor.

How to trigger it: When you receive a denial notification, call the payer’s provider line and say, "I would like to request a peer-to-peer review with the reviewing medical director." You must do this within 5–10 business days of the denial. Have your patient’s chart open, and lead with the evidence: "My patient has a BMI of 32, an HbA1c of 8.2% despite metformin and Jardiance, and a history of ASCVD. Here is the lab report."

Documentation Requirements: The Exact Clinical Criteria

Payers do not have uniform criteria, but there are common threads across the top 10 insurers. Use this as a baseline checklist.

For Type 2 Diabetes Indication (Ozempic, Trulicity, Mounjaro)

  • HbA1c: Typically ≥ 7.0% (some plans require ≥ 7.5%)
  • Failed conservative therapy: Document a trial of metformin (or metformin plus a sulfonylurea) for at least 90 days
  • Contraindication to metformin: If the patient cannot take metformin (e.g., eGFR < 30), document this clearly—it bypasses step therapy
  • BMI: Usually not the primary criterion for T2D, but some plans require BMI ≥ 25

For Weight Loss Indication (Wegovy, Saxenda)

  • BMI: ≥ 30 kg/m² (obesity) OR ≥ 27 kg/m² with at least one weight-related comorbidity (hypertension, dyslipidemia, obstructive sleep apnea, or cardiovascular disease)
  • Failed weight management program: Many commercial plans now require documentation of a 6-month structured diet/exercise program
  • Exclusion of secondary causes: Some plans require documentation that hypothyroidism or Cushing’s syndrome has been ruled out

The "Medical Necessity Letter" That Wins Appeals

Generic letters fail. You need a letter that cites specific guidelines. A 2023 analysis found that using language from the American Association of Clinical Endocrinology (AACE) and the American Diabetes Association (ADA) increases approval rates by 35%. Your letter should include:

"Per the 2023 AACE/ADA guidelines, this patient meets criteria for GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy due to [specific reason]. The patient has an HbA1c of 8.2% despite 6 months of metformin 2000mg/day. Additionally, the patient has established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), and the ADA recommends GLP-1 RA therapy for cardiovascular risk reduction regardless of HbA1c. Denial of this therapy would place the patient at elevated risk for major adverse cardiovascular events."

That last sentence—"denial would place the patient at risk"—is powerful. It shifts the conversation from cost to liability.

Payer-Specific Differences: Commercial vs. Medicare vs. Medicaid

You cannot use one PA strategy for all patients. The requirements differ significantly across insurance categories. Here is the breakdown.

Commercial Insurance (UnitedHealthcare, Aetna, Cigna, BCBS)

These plans are the most variable. They often require step therapy (45% of Medicare Part D plans require it, but commercial plans range from 30–50%). They also impose strict formulary tiers. If Ozempic is not on the plan’s formulary, you may need to request a formulary exception—a separate process from a PA. The exception requires documentation that the preferred GLP-1 (e.g., Trulicity) is contraindicated or caused adverse effects.

Medicare Part D

Medicare Part D covers Ozempic for T2D but not for weight loss alone. You must document a diagnosis of Type 2 diabetes (E11.9) and an HbA1c ≥ 7.0% on maximal metformin therapy. The PA process for Medicare is stringent: 45% of Part D plans require step therapy, and many require a 1–3 month trial of metformin before they will consider a GLP-1. Standard PA review takes up to 14 days for Medicare, so submit early. The patient will pay 20% co-insurance; with Ozempic’s list price, that is roughly $187/month out-of-pocket unless they qualify for Extra Help.

Medicaid

Medicaid coverage for GLP-1s is highly state-dependent. Many states cover Ozempic for T2D but explicitly exclude Wegovy for obesity. Some states require prior authorization for all GLP-1s, with strict BMI criteria (≥ 35 with comorbidity). Check your state’s Medicaid Preferred Drug List (PDL). If the drug is non-preferred, you will need to submit a prior authorization with a "medical justification" that explains why the preferred agent is not suitable.

The Denial Rate Problem: Statistics and Root Causes

According to the American Medical Association (2023), 25% of all PA claims for GLP-1s are denied on first submission. This is not because insurers are malicious—it is because the submission is incomplete. Here are the top five reasons for denial, ranked by frequency:

  1. Missing or outdated labs: The HbA1c is > 90 days old, or the BMI has no date.
  2. Wrong ICD-10 codes: Using E66.9 (obesity, unspecified) when the plan requires E66.01 (morbid obesity).
  3. Step therapy not documented: The patient was on metformin, but you did not include the prescription history or dates.
  4. Lack of cardiovascular disease documentation: For patients with ASCVD, you must note this explicitly—it is a separate indication for GLP-1 use.
  5. Off-label prescribing: Prescribing Ozempic for weight loss when the patient does not have T2D. This is an automatic denial on most plans.

The Appeals Process: Your Second (and Third) Chance

A denial is not a final answer. Statistically, 58% of internal appeals overturn denials, and 89% of external appeals succeed (Kaiser Family Foundation, 2023). Here is the exact timeline you must follow.

Internal Appeal (Level 1)

You have 60 days from the date of the denial to file an internal appeal with the insurance company. Submit a written letter plus any new clinical documentation. The insurer must respond within 30 days for a standard appeal, or 72 hours for an expedited appeal (if the patient’s health is in jeopardy). Include your "medical necessity letter" template here.

External Appeal (Level 2)

If the internal appeal is denied, you have 4 months from the date of the internal denial to request an external review by an independent third-party organization (accredited by CMS or the state). This is where the 89% success rate comes from—the external reviewer is not incentivized to save the insurer money. File this via your state’s Department of Insurance or CMS portal.

The "Continuation of Care" Exception

Here is a loophole most providers miss. If a patient was already on Ozempic with a previous insurer (or a previous employer’s plan), most payers have a 30–90 day continuation-of-care policy. This bypasses the PA entirely. You must call the new insurer, provide proof of the previous prescription (a claims history or pharmacy printout), and request a temporary continuation. This is rarely publicized but is standard practice for plan transitions.

FAQ: Your GLP-1 Prior Authorization Questions, Answered

Q: How long does Ozempic prior authorization take?

A: Standard PA processing takes 24–72 hours for commercial plans. Automated approvals often clear in 1.1 business days, while manual review takes up to 2.5 days (CAQH, 2023). Medicare Part D is slower—allow up to 14 days. If the patient has an urgent medical need (e.g., HbA1c > 9%), request an expedited review for a 24-hour turnaround.

Q: What BMI and HbA1c levels qualify for GLP-1 coverage?

A: For weight loss (Wegovy), most commercial plans require a BMI ≥ 30, or ≥ 27 with a comorbidity like hypertension or dyslipidemia. For T2D (Ozempic), the HbA1c threshold is typically ≥ 7.0%, though Aetna requires ≥ 7.5%. Medicare does not cover GLP-1s for weight loss—only for T2D with an HbA1c ≥ 7.0% despite metformin therapy.

Q: Can I get Ozempic approved for weight loss if the patient is not diabetic?

A: Rarely. Ozempic is only FDA-approved for T2D. Most payers will auto-denied off-label prescriptions. If your patient needs GLP-1 for weight loss, prescribe Wegovy (same molecule, approved for obesity). Be prepared for a tougher PA process—Wegovy has a 5/5 difficulty rating.

Q: What happens if my PA is denied?

A: You have 60 days to file an internal appeal, and 4 months to request an external review. Internal appeals overturn 58% of denials; external appeals succeed 89% of the time (KFF, 2023). Your best first move is a peer-to-peer review with the payer’s medical director—this takes 15 minutes and has a 60-70% success rate.

Q: Does my insurance require step therapy before Ozempic?

A: 45% of Medicare Part D plans require a trial of metformin first. Commercial plans vary: UnitedHealthcare and Cigna require it for T2D, while Aetna does not. Always verify via a pre-PA call. If the patient is metformin-intolerant (e.g., GI side effects), document this clearly to bypass the step therapy requirement.

Q: What ICD-10 codes should I use for GLP-1 prior authorization?

A: For T2D, use E11.9. For obesity, use E66.01 (BMI ≥ 40) or E66.9 (BMI 30-39.9). Always add secondary codes for comorbidities (e.g., I10 for hypertension, E78.5 for dyslipidemia) to meet the "≥27 + comorbidity" criteria. Using the wrong code is an automatic denial.
