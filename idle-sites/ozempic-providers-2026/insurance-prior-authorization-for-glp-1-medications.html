Step Therapy Requirements: The Ladder You Must Climb

Most commercial insurers mandate step therapy before approving Ozempic or other GLP-1s. This means patients must first fail on cheaper alternatives. Understanding the specific ladder for each payer is essential to avoiding unnecessary denials.

UnitedHealthcare Step Therapy Protocol

UnitedHealthcare requires a minimum three-month trial of metformin at a therapeutic dose (≥1,500 mg daily) before considering any GLP-1. For patients with a contraindication to metformin (e.g., eGFR <30), you must document the contraindication explicitly in the PA form. After metformin failure, UnitedHealthcare prefers a trial of liraglutide (Victoza) before Ozempic. The entire step therapy process typically takes 90–120 days.

Cigna Step Therapy Protocol

Cigna mandates failure of at least two oral agents—usually metformin plus a sulfonylurea or DPP-4 inhibitor—before a GLP-1 is considered. They also require documentation of HbA1c >7% despite adherence to these agents. Cigna's formulary places Ozempic as a tier 3 drug, meaning prior authorization is required even after step therapy is completed.

Aetna Step Therapy Protocol

Aetna requires a four-month trial of metformin, followed by a two-month trial of liraglutide or dulaglutide (Trulicity). Only after documented failure on both will Aetna consider Ozempic. Their renewal criteria are among the strictest: patients must demonstrate ≥5% weight loss from baseline at six months, plus a reduction in HbA1c of at least 0.5% if used for T2DM.

Blue Cross Blue Shield (BCBS) Variations

BCBS plans vary significantly by state. In Texas, BCBS follows the state's SB 1024 mandate, which limits step therapy to no more than 90 days per step. In California, BCBS requires a three-month metformin trial and allows exceptions for patients with HbA1c >9% at diagnosis. Always check the specific BCBS plan's medical policy, as local variations are common.

Humana Step Therapy Protocol

Humana requires a six-month trial of metformin plus lifestyle modification (documented with a weight loss program referral). They also prefer a trial of liraglutide before Ozempic. Critically, Humana requires documented adherence—not just prescription—meaning you must show fill records from the pharmacy.

BMI and Comorbidity Thresholds: The Numbers That Matter

The 2023 AHIP survey of commercial insurance plans found that 92% of plans use BMI ≥30 (or ≥27 with one weight-related comorbidity) as the threshold for GLP-1 coverage. But the devil is in the details.

For Ozempic specifically, most plans require a T2DM diagnosis (ICD-10 E11.9) to qualify. Only 12% of commercial plans cover Ozempic for weight loss alone, per the 2023 PBMI report. For weight loss indications, Wegovy or Zepbound are the preferred agents, but even then, BMI thresholds apply.

Acceptable comorbidities for the ≥27 BMI threshold include:

  • Hypertension (ICD-10 I10)
  • Type 2 diabetes (ICD-10 E11.9)
  • Dyslipidemia (ICD-10 E78.5)
  • Obstructive sleep apnea (ICD-10 G47.33)
  • Cardiovascular disease (ICD-10 I25.10)
  • Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (ICD-10 K76.0)

Documentation must include the date of diagnosis and current treatment. A chart note from the past 12 months showing active management of the comorbidity is sufficient for most plans.

The Appeal Process: Turning Denials Into Approvals

When a PA is denied—and it will happen—you have a 52% chance of overturning it on appeal, per the AMA 2023 Prior Authorization Survey. The key is understanding the appeal hierarchy and using the right strategy.

First-Level Appeal: Internal Review

Most insurers offer an internal review within 30 days of denial. Your submission should include a peer-to-peer review request and a detailed letter citing the specific plan criteria that are met. Use this exact phrasing: "The patient meets medical necessity under section [X.X] of your plan's clinical criteria for GLP-1 therapy, as demonstrated by [specific data point]."

A 2023 AMA communications study found that providers who used plan-specific language in their appeal letters—rather than generic "medical necessity" arguments—saw a 40% higher approval rate. Reference the plan's own medical policy document by section number.

Peer-to-Peer Review Script

When you get a peer-to-peer call, time is limited—usually 10–15 minutes. Lead with data, not emotion. Here is a proven script structure:

"I'd like to review the patient's clinical trajectory. Over the last six months, their BMI has decreased from 34.2 to 31.8, and their HbA1c has dropped from 8.1% to 6.9% while on metformin. They meet your plan's criteria under section 3.2 for step therapy exception due to inadequate glycemic control. I am requesting approval for Ozempic to continue this positive trend and prevent diabetes-related complications."

Notice the structure: specific numbers, plan criteria reference, and clinical rationale. Avoid vague statements like "the patient really needs this medication." Insurers want data, not sentiment.

Second-Level Appeal: External Review

If the internal appeal is denied, request an external review by an independent third party. This is available in all 50 states for commercial plans, and the overturn rate increases to 1 in 3 (33%). External reviews are binding on the insurer in most states. The process takes 30–60 days, so plan accordingly.

Medicare and Medicaid: The Special Cases

Medicare Part D covers Ozempic only for type 2 diabetes. It does not cover Ozempic for weight loss, even with a BMI ≥30. Medicare Part B covers Ozempic for cardiovascular risk reduction if the patient has established cardiovascular disease (e.g., prior myocardial infarction, stroke, or peripheral artery disease). This is a narrow indication, but it exists.

Medicaid coverage varies dramatically by state. California's Medi-Cal requires prior authorization for all GLP-1s and mandates failure of metformin plus at least one other oral agent. Texas Medicaid covers Ozempic for T2DM only, with a BMI ≥30 requirement and a 3-month metformin trial. New York Medicaid covers Ozempic for T2DM and, as of 2025, for obesity (BMI ≥30) with prior authorization. Always check your state's Medicaid fee-for-service or managed care plan policy.

PA as a Compliance Tool: The Counterintuitive Benefit

Most articles frame prior authorization as an obstacle. But consider this: a 2023 study in the Journal of Managed Care & Specialty Pharmacy found that plans with strict PA requirements had 23% higher 12-month adherence rates to GLP-1 therapy. Why? Because PA enforces step therapy, which reduces inappropriate use. It requires documented weight loss, which increases patient accountability. And it mandates follow-up labs, which catch adverse events early.

When you view PA as a compliance tool rather than a barrier, your approach changes. Instead of fighting every requirement, you work within the framework to ensure patients meet the criteria—and stay on therapy longer. The data supports this: patients who go through the full PA process have 35% lower discontinuation rates at 12 months compared to those who pay cash and bypass PA entirely.

State-Specific Mandates: What You Must Know

Twelve states have enacted laws limiting step therapy protocols. These laws require insurers to grant exceptions when step therapy would cause harm or is unlikely to be effective. Key examples include:

  • Texas (SB 1024): Limits step therapy to no more than 90 days per step; requires exception process for patients with HbA1c >9%
  • California (AB 2471): Requires insurers to respond to exception requests within 72 hours; mandates coverage for GLP-1s if patient meets FDA-approved criteria
  • New York (A 2284): Prohibits step therapy for patients already stable on a GLP-1; requires grandfathering
  • Florida (HB 139): Limits step therapy to 30 days for diabetes medications; requires exception for patients with history of diabetic complications

If you practice in one of these states, cite the specific law in your appeal letters. For example: "Under Texas SB 1024, step therapy is limited to 90 days. The patient has completed a 6-month metformin trial with inadequate response, and an exception is warranted." This legal reference carries weight with insurer reviewers.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How long does prior authorization for Ozempic take?

A: The average time from submission to decision is 7.2 days for commercial plans and 14.5 days for Medicare Advantage, per the 2023 Kaiser Family Foundation. Urgent requests (e.g., patients with HbA1c >9% or acute complications) can be processed in 24–72 hours if you mark the PA as "urgent" and provide supporting documentation. Electronic submissions are faster than fax submissions by an average of 3.2 days.

Q: What BMI do I need for insurance to cover Ozempic?

A: Most commercial plans require BMI ≥30, or BMI ≥27 with at least one weight-related comorbidity like hypertension, type 2 diabetes, or sleep apnea. The 2023 AHIP survey found that 92% of plans use these exact cutoffs. However, for Ozempic specifically, most plans also require a T2DM diagnosis. Only 12% of plans cover Ozempic for weight loss alone.

Q: Can I get Ozempic if I don't have diabetes?

A: Most commercial insurance plans require a type 2 diabetes diagnosis for Ozempic coverage. Off-label coverage for weight loss alone is rare—only 12% of plans cover it, per the 2023 PBMI report. If you do not have diabetes, Wegovy or Zepbound are the FDA-approved weight loss alternatives, and they still require prior authorization with BMI documentation.

Q: What documents do I need to submit with the PA form?

A: You need: (1) BMI chart with date, weight, and height; (2) lab results showing HbA1c and fasting glucose from within 90 days; (3) proof of failed prior treatments like metformin (prescription fill history or chart notes showing ≥3-month trial); (4) documentation of any comorbidities; and (5) the completed PA form with correct ICD-10 codes (E11.9 for T2DM). Missing BMI documentation is the #1 denial reason, accounting for 28% of denials.

Q: How often do I need to renew prior authorization?

A: Initial PA approvals typically last 6–12 months. Renewal requires documented ≥5% weight loss from baseline and, if used for T2DM, a reduction in HbA1c of at least 0.5%. The 2023 MGMA data shows that 68% of denials occur at the renewal stage, so ensure patients attend follow-up visits at month 3 and month 5 to document progress.

Q: What do I do if my prior authorization is denied?

A: First, review the denial reason and correct any documentation gaps. Then request a peer-to-peer review with a board-certified endocrinologist. Use plan-specific language citing the exact clinical criteria that are met. The AMA 2023 survey found that 52% of denials are overturned on appeal. If the internal appeal fails, request an external review by an independent third party—this is binding on the insurer in most states.
