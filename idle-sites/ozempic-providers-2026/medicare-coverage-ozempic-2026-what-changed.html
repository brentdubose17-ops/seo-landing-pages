Part D Redesign in 2026: The $2,000 Cap Is Now Fully Matured

The Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) fundamentally restructured Medicare Part D, and the changes were phased in over 2024 and 2025. By January 1, 2026, the redesign is fully operational—meaning there are no more transition rules, no partial benefit phases, and no lingering donut hole mechanics. For Ozempic prescribers, this creates a predictable—if expensive—financial environment for patients.

The Donut Hole Is Dead: What This Means for Your Patients

The coverage gap, historically known as the "donut hole," was eliminated as a distinct cost phase starting January 1, 2025. In 2026, Part D beneficiaries move through exactly three phases: the deductible phase, the initial coverage phase, and the catastrophic phase (now rebranded as the $2,000 out-of-pocket cap). The bizarre middle zone where patients paid 25% of drug costs while manufacturers provided discounts is gone.

For Ozempic, this is significant. In 2023, the average Medicare beneficiary with diabetes hit the coverage gap by roughly August if they filled Ozempic monthly. In 2026, that same patient will instead hit the $2,000 TrOOP (true out-of-pocket) threshold much earlier—often by March or April—and then pay $0 for the remaining 8–9 months of the year. This is a massive improvement in cost predictability, even if the initial months of the year carry a heavier financial load.

How the $2,000 Cap Actually Works for Ozempic in 2026

The $2,000 cap is calculated on true out-of-pocket costs (TrOOP). This includes deductibles, copayments, and coinsurance paid by the beneficiary, plus the Enhanced Part D Low-Income Subsidy (LIS) payments made on their behalf. It does not include premiums. Once a patient's TrOOP reaches $2,000, they automatically qualify for the catastrophic benefit and pay $0 for covered Part D drugs for the rest of the calendar year.

Here is the key calculation for Ozempic in 2026. With a monthly list price of approximately $935.77, a patient without LIS will hit the $2,000 cap after roughly 2.1 months of fills (assuming a standard 30-day supply and a tier 3 copay). Even with a high deductible plan ($590 in 2026), the math is straightforward: deductible + copays ≈ $2,000 by month 3 or 4. After that, Ozempic is free for the patient for the remaining 8–10 months.

Actionable Insight: For patients who struggle with the upfront cost, consider a "front-loading" strategy. Fill a 90-day supply in January and February. This accelerates TrOOP accumulation, helping patients hit the $2,000 cap faster and reducing their total annual exposure.

Premium Stability: A Rare Piece of Good News

CMS projected a modest 1.0% compound annual growth rate for Part D premiums in 2026. The average monthly Part D premium is expected to land around $46.50, up only slightly from approximately $46.00 in 2025. This stability is directly attributable to the IRA's premium stabilization provision, which caps premium increases at 6% annually. For Ozempic providers, this means patient retention is less likely to be disrupted by premium shock—but that stability comes with a hidden cost: the federal government is subsidizing the gap, and that money has to come from somewhere (ultimately, provider reimbursement and pharmacy margins).

The 2027 MFP Looming: What We Know and What to Expect

On January 17, 2025, CMS announced that semaglutide (the active ingredient in both Ozempic and Wegovy) was selected for the second round of Medicare Drug Price Negotiation. The negotiated Maximum Fair Price (MFP) was published in November 2025 and officially takes effect on January 1, 2027. For the entire year of 2026, you will still prescribe Ozempic at the current list price—but the clock is ticking.

In 2026, the rules remain unchanged from the landmark 2024 CMS ruling—but the implications are still poorly understood by many providers.

The Wegovy Exception: A Narrow Door for Obesity Coverage

CMS's March 8, 2024 final rule allows Part D plans to cover anti-obesity medications (AOMs) when used for cardiovascular risk reduction. This applies specifically to Wegovy, which received FDA approval for reducing cardiovascular death, heart attack, and stroke in adults with established cardiovascular disease and a BMI ≥ 27 kg/m². In 2026, this remains the only pathway for Medicare coverage of a GLP-1 for weight-related indications.

For your patients with obesity but without documented cardiovascular disease, the door remains firmly closed. This affects roughly 6 million Medicare beneficiaries (approximately 10% of the program) who would become newly eligible if the "Treat and Reduce Obesity Act" (TROA) were passed. The bill has been re-introduced in Congress and is actively being lobbied in 2026. If it passes, the coverage landscape changes overnight—but as of now, it is still pending.

Provider Tip: For patients who qualify for Wegovy under the CV-risk indication, document the specific cardiovascular diagnosis (e.g., prior MI, stroke, peripheral arterial disease) in the chart. Plans will deny claims without explicit ICD-10 codes for ASCVD.

Prior Authorization & Step Therapy in 2026: Tighter or Looser?

If you have been prescribing Ozempic for Medicare patients for years, you know the prior authorization (PA) drill. In 2026, expect continued stringency—but with subtle shifts driven by the IRA's drug negotiation program. Plans are under pressure to demonstrate appropriate utilization to CMS, which means PA criteria are not loosening.

Standard 2026 PA Criteria for Ozempic (Part D)

  • Confirmed Type 2 Diabetes diagnosis (ICD-10 E11.x). Prediabetes or insulin resistance does not qualify.
  • A1c threshold: Most plans require a baseline A1c ≥ 7.0%. Some regional MA-PDs use 7.5%.
  • Metformin requirement: Documentation of metformin use for at least 90 days, or a documented contraindication (e.g., eGFR < 30, severe GI intolerance).
  • No concurrent GLP-1: Plans will deny if the patient is already on Wegovy, Mounjaro, or Rybelsus.
  • Quantity limits: Typically 1 box (4 pens) per 28 days. 90-day fills require special approval.

Step Therapy: The Hidden Barrier

In 2026, more plans are adding step therapy requirements for Ozempic. This means the patient must first try a cheaper alternative—often a sulfonylurea (glipizide), a TZD (pioglitazone), or even insulin—before the plan will approve Ozempic. This is a cost-control measure, but it creates a significant administrative burden for your practice. You will need to submit a formal step therapy exception request with clinical rationale.

The good news: the $2,000 cap provides a safety net. Even if step therapy delays access by 2–3 months, the patient's TrOOP accumulation is delayed accordingly, meaning they may hit the cap later in the year. This is not necessarily bad—it spreads costs more evenly.

State-Level Medicaid & Dual-Eligible Changes in 2026

For your dual-eligible patients (those with both Medicare and Medicaid), the 2026 changes are significant. The IRA's expansion of the Low-Income Subsidy (LIS) program eliminated the partial subsidy tier, meaning all dual-eligible beneficiaries now receive full LIS benefits. This translates to:

  • No deductible
  • No coverage gap
  • Maximum copays of $4.90 for generics and $12.15 for brand-name drugs (2026 figures)
  • Automatic enrollment in the Extra Help program

For Ozempic, this means dual-eligible patients will pay a maximum of $12.15 per 30-day fill—a dramatic reduction from the hundreds of dollars they might have paid pre-2025. However, state Medicaid programs still have their own rules for GLP-1 coverage, and these vary widely.

The 2026 Prescribing Decision Framework: A 4-Step Approach

When your Medicare patient asks about Ozempic in 2026, use this streamlined framework to determine the right course of action.

Step 1: Confirm the Diagnosis

Verify Type 2 diabetes with a documented A1c ≥ 6.5% (or fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL). If the patient has obesity without diabetes, Ozempic is off-label and will be denied. Consider Wegovy only if they have established cardiovascular disease.

Step 2: Assess Cardiovascular Status

If the patient has Type 2 diabetes and ASCVD (prior MI, stroke, PAD) or heart failure, Wegovy may be the better choice—it offers the same glycemic benefits with an additional CV-risk indication. This opens the door to Part D coverage for weight management if diabetes control is not the primary goal.

Step 3: Evaluate Out-of-Pocket Exposure

Calculate the patient's likely TrOOP for 2026. With Ozempic at ~$935/month, they will hit the $2,000 cap in roughly 2–3 months. If they are LIS-eligible, their copays are capped at $12.15/month. If not, plan for a high early-year cost burden.

Step 4: Compare Formulary Alternatives

If Ozempic is non-preferred on the patient's 2026 plan (check the formulary released October 15, 2025), compare Mounjaro (tirzepatide) or Rybelsus (oral semaglutide). These may be on a lower tier with lower copays, even if the list price is similar.

Legislative Watch: The "Treat and Reduce Obesity Act" (TROA)

No 2026 analysis is complete without addressing the elephant in the room: the Treat and Reduce Obesity Act. Re-introduced in Congress with bipartisan support, TROA would explicitly expand Medicare Part D coverage to include anti-obesity medications for patients with obesity (BMI ≥ 30) without requiring a comorbid condition like diabetes or cardiovascular disease.

If TROA passes in 2026, the impact on Ozempic and Wegovy prescribing would be explosive. KFF estimates that ~6 million additional Medicare beneficiaries with obesity but without diabetes or CV disease would become newly eligible for GLP-1 coverage. This would likely trigger:

  • Massive demand surge for semaglutide and tirzepatide
  • Supply chain strain reminiscent of the 2023 shortages
  • Potential expansion of compounding pharmacy utilization
  • A flood of new patients seeking prescriptions from providers like you

As a provider, you should monitor TROA's progress through committee hearings. If it passes, be prepared to pivot your patient education materials and intake processes to handle a wave of new obesity-focused consultations. The 2026 bridge year could become the launchpad for a dramatically expanded GLP-1 practice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Will Medicare cover my Ozempic in 2026?

A: Yes, if you have Type 2 diabetes. Ozempic is covered under Medicare Part D (either a standalone prescription drug plan or a Medicare Advantage plan with drug coverage). You will need to meet prior authorization requirements, typically including an A1c ≥ 7.0% and documentation of metformin use or intolerance. Off-label weight loss use remains uncovered.

Q: I'm on Medicare and hit the $2,000 cap—do I stop paying for Ozempic in January 2026?

A: Yes. Once your true out-of-pocket costs (TrOOP) reach $2,000 in a calendar year, you automatically enter the catastrophic phase and pay $0 for all covered Part D drugs, including Ozempic, for the rest of the year. For most Ozempic users, this happens by March or April if you fill monthly.

Q: Is Wegovy covered by Medicare in 2026 for weight loss?

A: Only if you have established cardiovascular disease. CMS's 2024 rule allows Part D plans to cover Wegovy when used to reduce cardiovascular risk in patients with ASCVD and a BMI ≥ 27. If you have obesity without heart disease, stroke, or peripheral arterial disease, Wegovy is not covered by Medicare in 2026.

Q: Will the negotiated price make Ozempic cheaper for me in 2026?

A: No. The negotiated Maximum Fair Price (MFP) for semaglutide takes effect on January 1, 2027. Throughout 2026, you will still pay based on the current list price (~$935/month) minus your plan's tier copay. The MFP is projected to be 40–60% lower, but you will not see that savings until 2027.

Q: Does Medicare require prior authorization for Ozempic in 2026?

A: Yes. Most Part D plans require prior authorization for Ozempic. Typical criteria include a Type 2 diabetes diagnosis, A1c ≥ 7.0% (some plans use 7.5%), and a documented trial or contraindication to metformin. Some MA-PD plans also require step therapy, meaning you must try a cheaper alternative first.

Q: What happens if my plan switches Ozempic to a higher tier in 2026?

A: Your copay will increase, but you are still protected by the $2,000 out-of-pocket cap. If Ozempic moves to a non-preferred tier, you may pay more per fill, but your total annual exposure remains capped. Check your plan's 2026 formulary (released by October 15, 2025) and consider switching plans during Open Enrollment (October 15 – December 7, 2025) if a competitor offers better tier placement.
