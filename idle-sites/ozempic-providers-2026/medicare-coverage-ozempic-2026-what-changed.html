The 2026 CMS Rule Change: Removing the “Weight Loss Only” Exclusion

What the CMS Final Rule (CMS-4201-F) Actually Does

For years, Medicare Part D was prohibited from covering drugs prescribed solely for weight loss under Section 1860D-2(e)(2)(A) of the Social Security Act. The 2026 rule does not repeal this exclusion entirely. Instead, it creates a critical exception: anti-obesity medications (AOMs) like Ozempic can now be covered when prescribed for FDA-approved cardiovascular risk reduction or when a patient has a “clinically significant comorbidity” related to obesity.

This distinction matters because Ozempic is not FDA-approved for weight loss—that indication belongs to Wegovy (also semaglutide, but at a higher dose). However, Ozempic is FDA-approved for reducing cardiovascular risk in adults with Type 2 diabetes (T2D) and established cardiovascular disease (CVD). The 2026 rule explicitly allows Part D plans to cover Ozempic for this cardiovascular indication without running afoul of the weight loss exclusion.

Effective January 1, 2026, CMS added a new ICD-10 code—Z86.72 (personal history of cardiovascular disease)—that automatically qualifies a patient with T2D for Ozempic coverage without prior authorization. This change covers an estimated 1.2 million Medicare beneficiaries who have both T2D and a history of myocardial infarction, stroke, or revascularization.

The “Obesity-as-Comorbidity” Loophole Most Providers Miss

Here is the angle that only 12% of provider blogs mention, according to a March 2026 CMS stakeholder analysis. Section 1860D-2(e)(2)(D) of the Inflation Reduction Act allows Part D plans to cover Ozempic for patients with obesity (BMI >30) who have a “clinically significant comorbidity” even if they do not have T2D. The CMS rule defines these comorbidities as hypertension, dyslipidemia, sleep apnea, and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH).

This is not an off-label loophole—it is a codified exception. In 2026, an estimated 340,000 Medicare beneficiaries with BMI >30 and hypertension could qualify for Ozempic under this provision. The key requirement is that the prescribing provider must document the comorbidity using specific ICD-10 codes (Z68.30–Z68.45 for BMI, plus the relevant comorbidity code) and submit a medical exception request using the new CMS Form 10962-B.

Prior Authorization and Step Therapy: The 30% Reduction Mandate

New IRA Requirements for Formulary Restrictions

The Inflation Reduction Act’s Part D redesign includes a phased mandate to reduce formulary restrictions for GLP-1 agonists. In 2026, the benchmark is a 30% reduction in prior authorization and step therapy requirements compared to 2025. This does not mean plans must eliminate restrictions—it means they must reduce the number of prescriptions subjected to them.

CMS data from January 2026 shows that 78% of stand-alone Part D plans and 92% of Medicare Advantage Prescription Drug (MA-PD) plans now include Ozempic on their preferred tier. This is down from 95% in 2025, but the nature of restrictions has changed. In 2025, 62% of plans required prior authorization for any new Ozempic prescription. In 2026, that number has dropped to 43%.

Step Therapy Elimination for High-Risk T2D Patients

A specific 2026 CMS mandate eliminates step therapy requirements for Ozempic when the patient has a prior history of T2D with an HbA1c >7.0%. This applies to approximately 23% of new Ozempic users in Medicare—roughly 180,000 beneficiaries who previously had to fail metformin or sulfonylureas before accessing a GLP-1 agonist.

For providers, this means if your patient has a documented HbA1c >7.0% within the past 12 months, you can bypass step therapy entirely. The CMS mandate overrides any plan-level step therapy protocol for this specific patient population.

The Medical Necessity Override: How to Win an Appeal in 2026

New CMS Provision for “Off-Label” Coverage

One of the most consequential changes in 2026 is the “medical necessity override” provision. Under this rule, Part D plans can approve coverage for Ozempic prescribed for obesity (without T2D) if the plan’s medical exception committee determines that the drug is medically necessary. This is not a blanket approval—it requires a formal request process.

Data from the CMS Part D appeals database for Q1 2026 shows that 67% of medical exception requests for Ozempic (obesity-only) are approved, up from 41% in 2025. This 26-percentage-point increase reflects both the new CMS guidance and the pressure on plans to comply with the 2026 rule changes.

The 4-Step Framework for a Successful Medical Exception

Step 1: Document BMI >30 using ICD-10 codes Z68.30–Z68.45. Step 2: Identify and document a clinically significant comorbidity—hypertension (I10), dyslipidemia (E78.5), sleep apnea (G47.33), or NASH (K75.81). Step 3: Complete CMS Form 10962-B, which is the new 2026 standardized form for medical exception requests. Step 4: Cite CMS Rule 2026-01 Section 1860D-2(e)(2)(D) in the justification letter.

Providers who follow this framework see approval rates above 80%, according to a February 2026 analysis by the Medicare Rights Center. Those who submit generic prior authorization forms without the specific CMS citation see rates below 50%.

Decision Framework: Does Your Patient Qualify for Ozempic Under Medicare in 2026?

Start with the T2D diagnosis. If the patient has Type 2 diabetes, proceed to check for cardiovascular disease history. If they have a documented MI, stroke, or revascularization (ICD-10 Z86.72), Ozempic is automatically covered without prior authorization. If they have T2D but no CVD history, standard prior authorization applies, but step therapy is eliminated if HbA1c >7.0%.

If the patient does not have T2D, check BMI. If BMI >30, look for a clinically significant comorbidity—hypertension, dyslipidemia, sleep apnea, or NASH. If present, submit a medical exception request using CMS Form 10962-B. If no comorbidity exists, the patient does not qualify for Ozempic under Medicare but may qualify for Wegovy if they have CVD risk factors.

This decision tree applies to approximately 4.1 million Medicare beneficiaries who could potentially qualify for Ozempic in 2026, up from 2.3 million in 2025—a 78% increase in the eligible population.

FAQ: Medicare Coverage for Ozempic in 2026

Q: Does Medicare cover Ozempic for weight loss in 2026? What changed from 2025?

A: Medicare still does not cover Ozempic for weight loss alone. However, the 2026 CMS rule allows coverage if the patient has a “clinically significant comorbidity” related to obesity, such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, sleep apnea, or NASH. This is a new exception that did not exist in 2025. For pure weight loss without comorbidities, Wegovy may be a better option under the new cardiovascular risk reduction indication.

Q: Will my Part D plan still require prior authorization for Ozempic in 2026? How do I bypass it?

A: Prior authorization rates dropped from 62% of plans in 2025 to 43% in 2026. To bypass prior authorization, ensure the patient has a documented HbA1c >7.0% (eliminates step therapy) or a history of cardiovascular disease (ICD-10 Z86.72, eliminates prior authorization entirely). If prior authorization is still required, use CMS Form 10962-B for medical exception requests.

Q: How much will I pay out-of-pocket for Ozempic in 2026 under the new $2,000 cap?

A: Most patients will pay $0 per month after reaching the $2,000 annual cap, which typically occurs after 2–3 months of Ozempic use. The total annual cost is capped at $2,000, compared to an average of $3,240 in 2025. Even with the premium increase to $37.50 per month, the average patient saves over $9,000 annually.

Q: Can my doctor prescribe Ozempic for prediabetes in 2026? Is there a new ICD-10 code?

A: Ozempic is not FDA-approved for prediabetes, and Medicare does not cover it for this indication. However, if a patient with prediabetes also has a BMI >30 and a comorbidity (e.g., hypertension), they may qualify under the obesity-as-comorbidity exception. The new ICD-10 code Z86.72 is for cardiovascular disease history, not prediabetes.

Q: What is the “medical necessity override” for Ozempic in 2026? How do I apply?

A: The medical necessity override allows Part D plans to approve Ozempic for obesity without T2D if the plan’s medical exception committee deems it necessary. To apply, your provider must submit CMS Form 10962-B with documentation of BMI >30 and a clinically significant comorbidity. The approval rate in Q1 2026 was 67%, up from 41% in 2025.

Q: Are Medicare Advantage plans covering Ozempic differently than standard Part D in 2026?

A: Yes. MA-PD plans have a 92% formulary inclusion rate for Ozempic (vs. 78% for stand-alone Part D plans). However, MA-PD plans are also subject to stricter CMS audit penalties—the $5,000 per-beneficiary fine applies to all Part D plans, but CMS is auditing MA-PD plans more aggressively in 2026. MA-PD plans also tend to have narrower networks, so check specific plan formularies.

Q: What happens if my plan drops Ozempic from its formulary in 2026? Can I appeal?

A: If your plan drops Ozempic, you have the right to request a formulary exception. Under 2026 rules, the plan must respond within 72 hours (24 hours for expedited requests). If the plan denies the exception, you can appeal to an independent review entity (IRE). CMS data shows a 58% success rate for formulary exception appeals in Q1 2026. You can also switch plans during the annual open enrollment period (October 15–December 7).
