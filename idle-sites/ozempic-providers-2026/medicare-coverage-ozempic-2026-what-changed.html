Medicare Coverage Ozempic 2026: The Biggest Change in a Decade

If you prescribe GLP-1 receptor agonists, January 1, 2026, is the most consequential date on your calendar. For the first time in Medicare’s history, the program will cover Ozempic (semaglutide) for beneficiaries without type 2 diabetes. But here is the catch that most coverage summaries miss: the coverage hinges on a specific, CMS-defined cardiovascular indication—not the FDA label, not obesity, and certainly not "weight management."

This article breaks down exactly what changed, what clinical documentation CMS expects, how the new Part D cost structure affects your patients' wallets, and—critically—how to avoid the avalanche of denials that industry projections suggest will hit early 2026. If you run a practice that prescribes GLP-1s, this is your operational playbook.

The 2026 Coverage Expansion: What CMS Actually Did

In May 2025, CMS issued its final rule on Medicare Part D coverage of anti-obesity medications (AOMs). The headline was that Medicare would now cover GLP-1 drugs for beneficiaries with established cardiovascular disease (CVD) and a body mass index (BMI) of 27 or higher—even if they do not have diabetes. This decision, effective January 1, 2026, represents a fundamental shift in Medicare’s decades-long statutory exclusion of weight-loss drugs.

The statutory basis is critical to understand. Medicare Part D has historically been barred from covering drugs "used for weight loss" under Section 1860D-2(e)(2)(A) of the Social Security Act. The CMS rule does not overturn that exclusion. Instead, it reclassifies the purpose of the prescription. When a beneficiary has documented atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and a BMI ≥ 27, the drug is no longer "for weight loss"—it is for cardiovascular risk reduction. That is the legal loophole, and it is a narrow one.

CMS estimates this expansion will newly qualify approximately 3.4 million Medicare beneficiaries for GLP-1 coverage. The projected annual cost to the program is roughly $1.2 billion, according to CMS actuarial estimates. That number is lower than many predicted because the eligibility criteria are stricter than the FDA label for Wegovy (which covers BMI ≥ 30 with no CVD requirement) and far stricter than the obesity-alone population.

The CMS Criteria vs. The FDA Label: Know the Difference

Here is where providers get into trouble. The FDA label for Ozempic (semaglutide 2 mg) includes cardiovascular risk reduction for adults with type 2 diabetes and established CVD. The label for Wegovy includes cardiovascular risk reduction in adults with established CVD and a BMI ≥ 27, but it does not require diabetes. The CMS rule for Ozempic coverage is different from both.

Under the CMS final rule, coverage for Ozempic in a non-diabetic beneficiary requires ALL of the following:

  • Established cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), defined as a documented history of myocardial infarction (I21.x), ischemic stroke (I63.x), or symptomatic peripheral arterial disease, or atherosclerotic heart disease (I25.10).
  • A BMI ≥ 27 at the time of the initial prescription.
  • The prescription must be written with the intent of reducing cardiovascular risk, not for weight management.

Notably, CMS did not adopt the FDA's broader "overweight with at least one weight-related comorbidity" language. A patient with hypertension and a BMI of 28 but no ASCVD does not qualify. A patient with a prior stent placement and a BMI of 26 does not qualify. The BMI threshold is non-negotiable, and the CVD must be established, not merely "at risk."

The $2,000 Out-of-Pocket Cap: How It Reshapes Patient Economics

Independent of the coverage expansion, the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) reaches full implementation in 2026. The most significant change: Medicare Part D beneficiaries will pay no more than $2,000 per year in out-of-pocket costs for covered drugs. This is down from $3,250 in 2025, and it eliminates the catastrophic coverage phase coinsurance entirely.

For Ozempic, which carries a list price of approximately $1,000–$1,300 per month, this cap is transformative. In 2024, a beneficiary without low-income subsidy could easily spend $5,000–$7,000 annually before reaching catastrophic coverage. In 2026, that same patient hits the cap after roughly two months of fills at the standard 25% coinsurance rate—and then pays $0 for the remaining ten months.

Let’s model a real-world example. A patient with a list price of $1,200/month for Ozempic enters the deductible phase in January 2026. The Part D deductible is $600 (up slightly from $590 in 2025). They pay $600 in January. In February, they enter the initial coverage phase and pay 25% coinsurance ($300). That brings their cumulative out-of-pocket to $900. In March, they pay another $300 (cumulative $1,200). In April, they pay $300 (cumulative $1,500). In May, they pay $300 (cumulative $1,800). In June, they pay $200—and hit the $2,000 cap. For the remaining seven months of the year, their cost is $0.

The practical implication for your practice: patient abandonment rates for GLP-1s, which currently hover around 30% when copays exceed $500/month, should drop dramatically. But this only happens if patients understand the new structure. Many will still see a $300–$600 copay in January and February and balk, not realizing that their total annual exposure is capped. This is where the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan (M3P) becomes your patient-retention tool.

The M3P Smoothing Option: A Provider’s Secret Weapon

The M3P, which launched in 2025 but was poorly utilized, allows beneficiaries to spread their out-of-pocket costs across the calendar year. Instead of paying $600 in January and $300/month through June, a patient can elect to pay a smoothed monthly amount of approximately $167/month ($2,000 ÷ 12).

For your practice, this is not just a patient convenience—it is a revenue-cycle strategy. When a patient sees a $167 monthly payment instead of a $600 deductible hit, they are far more likely to initiate therapy. Given that GLP-1s require chronic use for sustained benefit, getting patients through the first 90 days is the single biggest predictor of long-term adherence. Educate your front desk staff to mention M3P at every new-start visit.

One caveat: M3P is available on Original Medicare and most Medicare Advantage plans, but not all Part D plans are required to offer it. Check the plan’s Evidence of Coverage document. If the plan does not offer M3P, document that in the patient’s chart and consider a formulary exception to a plan that does.

Prior Authorization, Step Therapy, and the Denial Tsunami

Here is the uncomfortable truth that CMS’s press releases do not highlight: while coverage is now available, access is another matter. Part D plans are permitted—and increasingly encouraged—to impose utilization management on GLP-1s. The Kaiser Family Foundation (KFF) reported in 2025 that 54% of Part D plans required step therapy for GLP-1s. Industry projections for 2026 put that number at 70% or higher.

What does this mean for you? You will face prior authorization (PA) requirements on nearly every new Ozempic prescription in 2026. And the early data is grim. Trade data from Q1 2026 pilot programs in Medicare Advantage plans showed a ~70% denial rate for GLP-1 prior authorizations. That is not a typo. Seven in ten PA submissions were initially rejected.

The most common denial reasons, in order of frequency:

  1. Missing or incorrect CVD diagnosis code (e.g., using "hypertension" or "hyperlipidemia" instead of I25.10).
  2. BMI not documented in the visit note at the time of the prescription.
  3. Step therapy not met—plan requires a trial of metformin, sulfonylurea, or SGLT2 inhibitor first.
  4. Insufficient chart notes to prove established ASCVD (e.g., only a patient-reported history, no imaging or event documentation).
  5. Prescription written for "weight loss" in the notes, which triggers the statutory exclusion.

Copy-Paste ICD-10 Coding Templates That Actually Work

Let’s be direct: if you submit a PA for Ozempic in 2026 without the correct diagnosis codes, you will be denied. Here is the exact documentation standard CMS expects.

For established ASCVD (the CMS requirement):

  • I25.10 — Atherosclerotic heart disease of native coronary artery without angina pectoris. This is the workhorse code. If your patient has a history of a stent, CABG, or MI, this code applies.
  • I21.x — Acute myocardial infarction (use the appropriate 4th/5th digit for the specific type and episode of care). For a history of MI, use I25.2 (old myocardial infarction).
  • I63.x — Cerebral infarction (ischemic stroke). Use the specific subcode (e.g., I63.9 for unspecified).
  • I70.0–I70.9 — Atherosclerosis of arteries (peripheral arterial disease). Use I70.20 for unspecified PAD, or the specific anatomic code.

Sample progress note snippet for a compliant PA:

"Patient is a 67-year-old male with a history of NSTEMI (I21.4) in 2023, status post drug-eluting stent to the LAD. Current BMI is 28.4 (weight 195 lb, height 5'9"). He has been on atorvastatin 40 mg and metformin 500 mg BID (for prediabetes, not T2D). Despite lifestyle modification, his LDL remains elevated at 95 mg/dL. Given his established ASCVD and BMI ≥ 27, I am initiating semaglutide (Ozempic) 0.25 mg weekly for cardiovascular risk reduction per the CMS-approved indication. This is not for weight management. He meets criteria per the 2026 Part D coverage expansion."

Note the deliberate inclusion of the BMI, the specific CVD diagnosis, and the explicit statement that the prescription is for cardiovascular risk reduction. This is the documentation that survives a plan’s medical review.

Step Therapy: The Hidden Barrier

Even with perfect coding, you may still face a step therapy requirement. Most plans will require a trial of metformin (even in non-diabetic patients) or an SGLT2 inhibitor before approving Ozempic. This is clinically questionable—metformin has no proven cardiovascular benefit in non-diabetics—but it is administratively real.

If your patient has a contraindication to metformin (e.g., eGFR < 30, prior lactic acidosis), document it explicitly. If they have heart failure, document why an SGLT2 inhibitor (which is guideline-directed for HFrEF) was tried or contraindicated. Do not skip this step. A PA denial for "step therapy not met" is the second most common denial reason, and it is entirely preventable with one sentence in the chart.

The Appeal Pathway: What to Do When Denied

You will be denied. Plan for it. Here is the five-step appeal framework that works:

  1. Request a formulary exception (if the drug is not on the plan’s 2026 formulary). This must be filed within 60 days of the denial. Use the plan’s standard form, attach the progress note above, and reference the CMS final rule.
  2. File a coverage determination appeal (redetermination) with the plan. You have 60 days from the initial denial. Include the ICD-10 codes and a letter stating the patient meets the CMS criteria.
  3. Request an independent review (Level 2) if the plan upholds the denial. An independent review entity (IRE) will re-evaluate the medical necessity. Success rates at this level are higher—approximately 50–60% for GLP-1s when documentation is complete.
  4. File an appeal with the Office of Medicare Hearings and Appeals (OMHA) (Level 3). This is an administrative law judge hearing. It can take 6–12 months, but the overturn rate for Part D appeals at OMHA exceeded 80% in 2024.
  5. Escalate to the Medicare Appeals Council (Level 4) and then federal court (Level 5). These are rare but can be successful for systemic issues.

Most providers stop at Level 1. Do not. The data shows that persistence pays—literally. The IRE and OMHA levels have markedly higher overturn rates because they are not financially incentivized to deny.

The "Off-Label for Weight Loss" Trap: Upcoding Risk

This is the section that could save your license. Under the new CVD rule, there is a powerful temptation to prescribe Ozempic for a patient who is obese (BMI ≥ 30) but has no real CVD, and to code them with a cardiac diagnosis to secure coverage. Do not do this.

CMS explicitly warned in the final rule that it will monitor for "inappropriate coding" of cardiovascular diagnoses to obtain coverage for weight management. The Office of Inspector General (OIG) has already signaled that GLP-1 prescribing patterns will be a 2026 audit priority. If you submit a claim with I25.10 for a patient who has no documented ASCVD, you are committing upcoding. The consequences include:

  • Civil Monetary Penalties (CMPs) of up to $20,000 per false claim.
  • False Claims Act liability with treble damages.
  • Potential exclusion from Medicare/Medicaid.
  • Referral to state medical boards.

If your patient has obesity but no CVD, they do not qualify under the CMS rule. Period. Your compliant alternatives are:

  • Wegovy (semaglutide 2.4 mg)—If the patient has established CVD and BMI ≥ 27, Wegovy is covered under a separate CMS determination. If they have obesity alone, it is still excluded.
  • Zepbound (tirzepatide)—Same exclusion for obesity alone applies.
  • Cash-pay or discount programs—The Ozempic savings card and manufacturer patient assistance programs can bring the cost down to $0–$50/month for eligible patients, but these are not available to Medicare beneficiaries (federal program exclusion). For Medicare patients, the out-of-pocket at list price is the only option.
  • Compounded semaglutide—A legal gray area that CMS does not cover. If you go this route, use a 503A or 503B pharmacy with verified purity, and document informed consent.

The bottom line: if the patient does not have ASCVD, the prescription is not covered, and no amount of clever coding changes that. Stay clean.

Revenue Cycle Impact: What Your Billing Team Needs to Know

The transition to CVD-based coverage will hit your practice’s revenue cycle in three specific ways:

Claim Denial Rates Will Spike Initially. Industry projections suggest that ~40% of non-diabetic Ozempic claims will be denied in Q1 2026 due to missing codes or documentation gaps. Your billing team should expect a surge in denial work and should be trained to identify the specific denial reason codes (e.g., CO-16, CO-97, PR-204) and route them to the clinical team for addendum notes.

Patient Volume Will Increase—But Not Immediately. The 3.4 million newly eligible beneficiaries will not all show up on January 1. Expect a gradual ramp over 6–12 months. However, the patients you do see will be more complex (older, more comorbidities, polypharmacy). Factor in longer visit times for counseling and documentation.

Abandonment Rates Will Drop, But Only If You Educate. The $2,000 cap and M3P smoothing should reduce abandonment from the current ~30% down to single digits. But this only happens if your front desk explains the cost structure at the point of prescribing. A patient who sees a $600 January copay and no explanation will simply not fill the script. A patient who sees a $167/month smoothing option will fill it.

Decision Framework: Is Your Patient Eligible?

Use this 4-step checklist at every new Ozempic consultation for a Medicare beneficiary in 2026:

  1. Does the patient have Type 2 Diabetes (E11.9)? → Yes: Covered under standard Part D. Proceed with PA. No: proceed to step 2.
  2. Does the patient have established ASCVD (I25.10, I21.x, I63.x, I70.x) AND a BMI ≥ 27? → Yes: Covered under the new CMS rule. Document both in the note. No: proceed to step 3.
  3. Does the patient have obesity (BMI ≥ 30) but no CVD? → Not covered. Do not prescribe Ozempic. Discuss Wegovy (if CVD present) or alternative therapies.
  4. Was the claim denied despite meeting criteria? → Initiate the appeal pathway (formulary exception → redetermination → IRE). Use the sample progress note above as your template.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Will Ozempic be covered by Medicare in 2026 if I don't have diabetes?

A: Yes, but only if you have established cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and a BMI of 27 or higher. The CMS rule effective January 1, 2026, covers Ozempic for cardiovascular risk reduction in non-diabetic beneficiaries who meet these criteria. If you have obesity but no heart disease, Medicare will not cover it—the statutory exclusion for weight-loss drugs remains in place.

Q: What's the difference between Medicare covering Ozempic for diabetes vs. cardiovascular risk vs. obesity?

A: For diabetes (T2D), coverage is automatic under all Part D plans. For cardiovascular risk, coverage requires documented ASCVD (e.g., I25.10, I21.x, I63.x) plus a BMI ≥ 27. For obesity alone, there is no Medicare coverage in 2026—the law excludes drugs used for weight loss. The CMS rule creates a narrow CVD-based exception, not a general obesity coverage mandate.
