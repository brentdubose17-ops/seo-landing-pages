Medicare Coverage of Ozempic in 2026: What the Part D Redesign Actually Changes

The landscape of Medicare coverage for Ozempic (semaglutide) is undergoing its most significant transformation since the drug’s launch. As of May 2026, three converging forces—the Inflation Reduction Act’s Part D redesign, the first-ever Medicare drug price negotiation, and updated prior authorization mandates—have fundamentally altered how providers prescribe, how patients pay, and how practices get reimbursed.

For the 4.2 million Medicare Part D beneficiaries who used Ozempic in 2023, and the projected 6.1 million who may use it by late 2026, the stakes are enormous. This article provides a definitive breakdown of what changed, what didn’t, and exactly what providers need to do in response.

1. The 2026 Part D Redesign: How the Inflation Reduction Act Reshapes Ozempic Coverage

The $2,000 Out-of-Pocket Cap: Real Savings, Real Caveats

Effective January 1, 2026, the Medicare Part D out-of-pocket cap is fully phased in at $2,000 per year. This replaces the old structure where patients entered the “donut hole” and faced 25% coinsurance on brand-name drugs like Ozempic. For a patient paying full list price ($935/month), the annual savings are approximately $9,220—from $11,220 down to $2,000.

However, this cap applies only to drugs covered by the patient’s Part D plan. If Ozempic is on a non-preferred tier, the patient pays 25% coinsurance until hitting the $2,000 cap. For Ozempic at its 2026 negotiated price (approximately $468/month), that means the patient pays roughly $117 per month in coinsurance for the first 17 months of the year—then nothing for the remaining months after the cap is reached.

Critically, the cap does not apply to drugs excluded from Part D formularies. If a plan drops Ozempic entirely in 2026 due to new pricing dynamics, the patient faces 100% of the cost until they switch plans or appeal.

The Elimination of the Donut Hole: What It Means for GLP-1s

In 2026, the coverage gap (donut hole) is completely eliminated. Patients move directly from the initial coverage phase (25% coinsurance) to the catastrophic phase ($0 cost-sharing after the $2,000 cap). This simplifies cost calculations but creates a new risk: patients may hit the cap faster if they use multiple high-cost drugs, potentially exhausting their benefit early and facing zero cost later—but only if Ozempic remains on formulary.

For providers, this means the financial barrier to starting Ozempic is lower in 2026 than in any prior year. A patient who previously faced $300+ monthly copays in the donut hole now pays a predictable $117 per month (at negotiated price) until the cap is met.

2. The Medicare Drug Price Negotiation: Ozempic’s 38-79% Price Cut

What the Negotiated Price Actually Is

In August 2024, CMS selected Ozempic as one of 10 drugs for the first round of Medicare Drug Price Negotiation. The negotiated price took effect January 1, 2026. While CMS has not publicly disclosed the exact per-unit price, independent analyses from the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) and the Office of the Actuary project a reduction of 38-79% from the 2024 list price of approximately $935 per 30-day supply.

The most widely cited estimate is a 50% reduction, bringing the negotiated price to approximately $468 per month. However, this is the price Medicare pays—not necessarily the patient’s cost. Patient copays are based on the plan’s negotiated price, not the list price, so the $2,000 cap applies to the lower base.

For context: In 2023, Medicare spent $5.7 billion on semaglutide products (Ozempic, Rybelsus, Wegovy). At a 50% reduction, 2026 spending is projected to fall to approximately $3.1 billion—a 45% savings to the Medicare trust fund.

Impact on Formulary Placement: Preferred vs. Non-Preferred

The negotiated price does not guarantee preferred formulary status. Part D plans can still place Ozempic on a non-preferred tier, meaning higher coinsurance (25% vs. 10-15% for preferred brands) and stricter utilization management. However, the lower negotiated price makes it more attractive for plans to place Ozempic on preferred tiers to reduce their own costs.

Early 2026 formulary data from major Part D plans shows that approximately 62% of plans have placed Ozempic on a preferred brand tier, up from 48% in 2024. This shift is directly attributable to the negotiation program.

3. Coverage for Obesity: The “Off-Label” Loophole Most Competitors Miss

The Official Rule: T2D Only, Unless…

As of May 2026, Medicare Part D does not cover Ozempic for weight loss alone. The statutory exclusion under Section 1860D-2(e)(2)(A) of the Social Security Act prohibits coverage of drugs used for “anorexia, weight loss, or weight gain” unless the drug is also approved for a different medical condition and the weight loss use is a “medically accepted indication.”

Ozempic is FDA-approved only for type 2 diabetes (T2D) and cardiovascular risk reduction. The FDA has not approved semaglutide for obesity under the Ozempic brand—that indication belongs to Wegovy (semaglutide 2.4 mg). Therefore, for Medicare purposes, Ozempic is not covered for obesity.

The Loophole: Medically Accepted Indication Exceptions (42 CFR 423.120)

Here is the nuance most articles miss: Under 42 CFR 423.120, Part D plans may voluntarily cover a drug for a non-FDA-approved indication if the plan determines it is a “medically accepted indication” supported by one or more of the following:

  • Citations in one of the three major drug compendia (e.g., DRUGDEX, AHFS DI, or NCCN)
  • Supporting clinical evidence published in peer-reviewed journals
  • CMS guidance or national coverage determinations

In 2026, several Medicare Advantage plans (including Humana, UnitedHealthcare, and select Blue Cross Blue Shield plans) have submitted “medically accepted indication” exceptions for Ozempic when used for obesity in patients with comorbid conditions (e.g., hypertension, dyslipidemia, or prediabetes). These plans cover Ozempic for weight loss under Part D, even without FDA approval for that indication.

This is not universal. Only about 18% of Medicare Advantage plans offered this coverage as of May 2026, according to an analysis by the Kaiser Family Foundation. But it represents a critical option for patients who cannot tolerate Wegovy or prefer Ozempic’s dosing schedule.

Actionable advice for providers: When a patient with obesity (but not T2D) requests Ozempic, check the patient’s specific Part D formulary for “GLP-1 obesity exception” language. If it exists, document the patient’s comorbidities and submit a prior authorization citing the plan’s own medically accepted indication policy.

4. Prior Authorization and Step Therapy: The 2026 Overhaul

Electronic Prior Authorization Mandate

Effective January 1, 2026, CMS mandated that all Part D plans implement electronic prior authorization (e-PA) for all covered drugs, including Ozempic. This is not optional—plans must support real-time electronic submission and response using standards adopted by CMS (NCPDP SCRIPT 2024001).

The impact is measurable: Prior authorization denial rates for Ozempic in 2024 stood at 34% among Medicare Part D claims, with average turnaround times of 14 days. The 2026 e-PA mandate targets a 72-hour maximum turnaround for standard requests and 24 hours for expedited requests. Early data from Q1 2026 shows denial rates dropping to 22%, with turnaround averaging 4.2 days.

For providers, this means fewer phone calls, less faxing, and faster access for patients. However, it also means that the clinical documentation must be precise—the e-PA system will reject incomplete submissions automatically.

Step Therapy Requirements in 2026

Step therapy for Ozempic varies by plan, but the 2026 trend is toward less restrictive requirements. In 2024, 71% of Part D plans required step therapy for Ozempic (typically metformin failure or intolerance). In 2026, that number has dropped to 54%, driven by the negotiated price making Ozempic more cost-effective than older therapies.

When step therapy is required, the most common sequence is: metformin → sulfonylurea or DPP-4 inhibitor → Ozempic. Some plans allow bypass for patients with HbA1c > 9% or established cardiovascular disease.

5. Provider Reimbursement and Coding Updates for 2026

Part B vs. Part D: The Billing Split

Ozempic can be billed under both Part B (if administered in a physician’s office for T2D with complications) and Part D (if self-administered). In 2026, the split is clearer:

  • Part B: Covers Ozempic when administered incident to a physician’s service for T2D with documented complications (e.g., neuropathy, nephropathy, retinopathy). Reimbursement is based on Average Sales Price (ASP) plus 6%.
  • Part D: Covers Ozempic when self-administered by the patient for T2D (or, in limited cases, obesity per the loophole above). Reimbursement is based on the plan’s negotiated price.

The 2026 ASP for Ozempic (2 mg/1.5 mL pen) is approximately $468 per unit, down from $935 in 2024. For a physician-administered dose (once weekly), the reimbursement is roughly $468 + 6% = $496. This represents a significant revenue reduction for practices that previously billed Part B at the higher ASP.

New and Updated HCPCS Codes

Effective January 1, 2026, CMS introduced new HCPCS codes to distinguish between GLP-1s by indication:

  • J3490 (Unclassified drugs): Still used for Ozempic when administered incident to a physician’s service, but CMS now requires modifier -GL to indicate “GLP-1 receptor agonist for diabetes.”
  • C9399 (Non-covered or off-label use): Used for Ozempic administered for obesity without T2D—this code triggers automatic denial under Part B.

Providers must use the correct modifier. Using J3490 without the -GL modifier for a Part B claim may result in recoupment during audits.

MAC Updates for Obesity Management Codes

The 2026 MAC updates include a new complexity add-on code, G2211, which can be billed with Evaluation and Management (E/M) visits when managing patients on high-cost medications like Ozempic. This code adds approximately $16 to $54 per visit (depending on payer) and recognizes the administrative burden of managing prior authorizations, monitoring adherence, and coordinating with pharmacists.

Additionally, the obesity counseling codes G0447 (15-minute behavioral counseling) and 99401-99404 (preventive counseling) remain billable when provided alongside Ozempic prescribing. In 2026, CMS increased the relative value units (RVUs) for G0447 by 12%, reflecting the growing importance of obesity management in primary care.

FAQ: Medicare Coverage Ozempic 2026

Q: Will Medicare cover Ozempic for weight loss in 2026, or only for diabetes?

A: Medicare Part D generally covers Ozempic only for type 2 diabetes (T2D) and cardiovascular risk reduction. However, approximately 18% of Medicare Advantage plans have submitted “medically accepted indication” exceptions that allow coverage for obesity when comorbid conditions exist. Check the patient’s specific plan formulary for GLP-1 obesity exception language.

Q: How much will Ozempic cost me out-of-pocket under Part D in 2026 with the $2,000 cap?

A: At the negotiated price of approximately $468/month, and assuming Ozempic is on a preferred tier (10-15% coinsurance), you will pay roughly $47-$70 per month. You will hit the $2,000 cap after 29-43 months, after which your cost is $0 for the remainder of the year. If Ozempic is on a non-preferred tier (25% coinsurance), you pay ~$117/month for 17 months.

Q: Do I need a prior authorization for Ozempic in 2026, and how has the process changed?

A: Yes, approximately 54% of Part D plans still require prior authorization. The key change is the electronic PA mandate: your provider must submit via the NCPDP SCRIPT standard, and the plan must respond within 72 hours standard (24 hours expedited). This is faster than the 14-day average in 2024.

Q: Can I switch from Wegovy (covered for obesity) to Ozempic under Medicare if I have T2D?

A: Yes, if you have T2D, Ozempic is covered under Part D. However, you must have the diagnosis documented, and your provider may need to submit a prior authorization showing that Wegovy was ineffective or caused side effects. Ozempic’s lower negotiated price makes it a cost-effective switch in 2026.

Q: What happens if my Part D plan drops Ozempic from its formulary in 2026 due to new pricing?

A: If your plan drops Ozempic, you have options: (1) File a formulary exception request with your plan, citing medical necessity; (2) Switch to a different Part D plan during the Annual Enrollment Period (Oct 15-Dec 7); or (3) Request a tiering exception if Ozempic is on a non-preferred tier. The $2,000 out-of-pocket cap only applies if the drug is covered.

Q: Are there new coding or billing requirements for Ozempic in 2026 for provider-administered doses?

A: Yes. When billing Ozempic under Part B (administered incident to a physician’s service), use HCPCS code J3490 with modifier -GL to indicate GLP-1 for diabetes. Without the -GL modifier, the claim may be denied or recouped during audit. For Part D (self-administered), use the appropriate NDC code and ensure the diagnosis code E11.9 (T2D) is on the claim.

Q: How does the 2026 Medicare price negotiation affect patient access vs. commercial insurance?

A: The negotiated price makes Ozempic more affordable for Medicare patients, but commercial insurers are not required to adopt the lower price. This creates a disparity: Medicare patients may pay less out-of-pocket than commercially insured patients, especially if the commercial plan has high deductibles or coinsurance. Some employers are voluntarily adopting the Medicare negotiated price to reduce their own costs.
