Telehealth Compounded Peptides: The Hims & Hers Signal for Providers

Published August 15, 2026 · Last checked August 15, 2026By ABD Legacy LLC

Hims & Hers Health told investors on its Aug. 10, 2026 Q2 earnings call that it plans to offer already-permitted peptides — NAD+, sermorelin, and glutathione — before the end of 2026.[1][2] For telehealth platforms and compounding-focused providers, the move is a market signal: the boundary between the two channels is dissolving.

What Hims & Hers announced

CEO Andrew Dudum said the company is building a “best-in-class peptide experience” with U.S.-manufactured products, clinical-led guidance, and ongoing blood testing, and will offer “already allowed peptides with safe and well-established supply chains” before year-end.[3][4] Six additional peptides recommended by the FDA’s Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) in July — including BPC-157 — await final FDA rulemaking, with validation and stability testing already underway at its Menlo Park facility.[1]

Already legal to compound; GLP-1s remain contested

The legal distinction matters for providers. NAD+, sermorelin, and glutathione are already lawful to compound under the FDA’s 503A framework — but “permitted” does not mean FDA-approved. Medical Daily notes the move pushes a nearly three-million-subscriber company “into a wellness category built largely on claims that clinical trials have not tested.”[5]

Compounded GLP-1s are a different story. The FDA moved to restrict GLP-1 active pharmaceutical ingredients in February 2026, Novo Nordisk sued Hims over compounded “knock-off” Wegovy before settling into a March partnership, and compounded semaglutide now operates on a limited scale.[6][7] Providers should not conflate the two categories when advising patients — the year-end peptides are permitted compounds, not an FDA-approved GLP-1 channel.

What the overlap means for compounding-focused providers

Hims & Hers brings roughly three million subscribers into the peptide market. Analysts estimate the broader peptide market at $2.2 billion (Leerink) to $3.3 billion (Needham & Co), though they caution the year-end offering is “unlikely to drive revenue growth this year.”[1][8]

For telehealth platforms already selling compounded GLP-1s, the entry adds a well-capitalized competitor with its own manufacturing facility. For compounding pharmacies and clinics, the overlap cuts both ways: more patients asking about peptides, but more competition from direct-to-consumer platforms — and more regulatory scrutiny on how compounds are marketed, with the FTC filing suit against Hims & Hers on July 29.[2]

Provider guidance to update

  • Separate “permitted” from “approved.” NAD+, sermorelin, and glutathione are lawful to compound; they are not FDA-approved treatments. Tell patients the difference.[5]
  • Apply the same verification standard to peptide compounding you use for semaglutide: USP <797> certification, batch-specific potency and sterility certificates, product liability insurance (only 23% of compounding pharmacies carry it), and a clean state board record. See our compounding pharmacy safety guide.
  • Document medical necessity and informed consent for any compounded product, including wellness peptides.
  • Watch the FDA rulemaking clock. A final decision on the six PCAC peptides — and the contested GLP-1 API question — will determine the next growth wave for telehealth compounded peptides. Track the legal landscape in our compounded GLP-1 legality by state guide.

Bottom line

Hims & Hers entering compounded peptides is the clearest sign yet that telehealth platforms and compounding markets are converging. The year-end launch is limited to already-legal compounds, but the direction of travel is unmistakable — and it raises the stakes for quality verification across the compounding channel.
