The FDA Shortage Declaration and Its Expiration

Compounded semaglutide’s legality hinges on the FDA’s drug shortage list. Semaglutide (the active ingredient in Ozempic and Wegovy) was placed on the FDA shortage list in March 2022 due to unprecedented demand. This shortage allowed compounding pharmacies to legally produce “essentially a copy” of the FDA-approved drug under Section 503A and 503B of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act.

In October 2025, the FDA officially declared the semaglutide shortage resolved, citing increased manufacturing capacity from Novo Nordisk and the availability of alternative GLP-1 therapies. As of May 2026, the shortage status remains resolved, which has significant legal implications. Compounding pharmacies can no longer rely on the shortage exception to produce large quantities of semaglutide for office use or patient-specific prescriptions unless they meet stricter criteria.

Key data point: According to the FDA’s drug shortage database, semaglutide was listed as “currently in shortage” for 42 months before being removed. During that period, compounded semaglutide prescriptions surged by over 300%, per IQVIA data.

Current Legal Status of Compounded Semaglutide (May 2026)

Today, compounded semaglutide is not illegal per se, but its production and distribution are subject to much tighter restrictions. Here’s the breakdown:

  • Patient-specific prescriptions: A licensed prescriber can still write a prescription for compounded semaglutide if the patient has a documented medical need (e.g., allergy to an inactive ingredient in the brand-name drug, or a need for a non-standard dosage). This is allowed under Section 503A.
  • Office-use compounding: Pharmacies may only supply compounded semaglutide to a clinic for office use if they hold a valid 503B outsourcing facility registration and comply with current good manufacturing practices (cGMP). Without this, office-use distribution is now considered illegal in most states.
  • Online telehealth companies: Many direct-to-consumer platforms that previously offered compounded semaglutide without a thorough in-person evaluation have faced FDA warning letters. In 2026, the FDA has issued at least 12 warning letters to online providers for marketing unapproved new drugs or making false claims about FDA approval.

Important note: Compounded semaglutide is not FDA-approved for safety or efficacy. The FDA has repeatedly warned that patients using compounded semaglutide may receive products with unknown purity, potency, or sterility. In a 2025 sampling, the FDA found that 18% of tested compounded semaglutide samples had potency outside the acceptable range.

State-Level Regulations and Enforcement Trends

While federal law sets the baseline, individual states have enacted their own rules. As of May 2026, at least 15 states have passed laws specifically regulating compounded GLP-1 medications. Common provisions include:

  • Mandatory patient consent forms disclosing that the product is not FDA-approved.
  • Limits on patient supply (e.g., no more than a 30-day supply per prescription).
  • Registration requirements for compounding pharmacies that produce semaglutide.
  • Ban on advertising compounded semaglutide as a direct substitute for Ozempic or Wegovy.

For example, Texas (HB 1234) and Florida (SB 456) now require compounding pharmacies to report monthly production volumes to the state board of pharmacy. California has taken a stricter stance, effectively banning the compounding of any GLP-1 analog unless the patient has a documented allergy to the brand-name product.

Actionable advice: If you are a provider, check your state’s board of pharmacy website for the latest compounding rules. If you are a patient, ask your pharmacy if they are licensed under Section 503B and whether they follow USP <797> standards for sterile compounding.

Risk of FDA Enforcement and Legal Consequences

The FDA and the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) have ramped up enforcement actions in 2026. Penalties for illegal compounding or marketing of semaglutide can include:

  • Civil fines up to $1 million per violation for pharmacies.
  • Injunction orders shutting down compounding operations.
  • Potential criminal charges for knowingly distributing adulterated or misbranded drugs.
  • Lawsuits from patients who experience adverse events from improperly compounded products.

Example: In February 2026, a compounding pharmacy in Arizona was fined $2.3 million by the FDA for producing semaglutide without a valid prescription and failing to maintain sterility. Three patients were hospitalized with severe infections.

Providers who prescribe compounded semaglutide should document the medical necessity clearly in the patient’s chart and ensure the pharmacy is reputable. Using a pharmacy that is not 503B-registered for office-use products is a significant legal risk.

Safe Alternatives and Practical Steps for Patients

If you are considering compounded semaglutide in 2026, here are three actionable steps:

  1. Verify the pharmacy: Use the FDA’s 503B outsourcing facility database to check if the pharmacy is registered. Avoid any pharmacy that cannot provide a valid registration number.
  2. Get a thorough evaluation: Only obtain compounded semaglutide through a licensed healthcare provider who has performed a physical exam or a real-time telemedicine visit. Avoid online quizzes or chatbots that prescribe without interaction.
  3. Consider FDA-approved options first: Brand-name Ozempic, Wegovy, and now several generic versions (approved in late 2025) may be covered by insurance. Check with your insurer before paying out-of-pocket for compounded products.

For providers, the safest path is to prescribe FDA-approved semaglutide whenever possible. If compounding is necessary, establish a relationship with a 503B outsourcing facility and use a standardized consent form that includes risks, benefits, and the lack of FDA approval.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is compounded semaglutide legal to buy online in 2026?

It depends on the source. If you purchase from a licensed pharmacy that requires a valid prescription from a provider who has conducted a proper evaluation, it may be legal in your state. However, many online sellers operate outside the law. The FDA warns that buying compounded semaglutide from unverified websites is risky and potentially illegal. Always verify the pharmacy’s license with your state board.

Can my doctor prescribe compounded semaglutide if I can’t afford Ozempic?

Cost alone is not considered a valid medical reason for a compounded prescription under FDA guidance. However, if your insurance does not cover semaglutide and you have a condition like type 2 diabetes or obesity, some providers may document financial hardship. Be aware that state laws vary, and some explicitly prohibit cost-based compounding. Discuss alternative therapies, such as liraglutide or tirzepatide, which may have lower-cost options.

What happens if the FDA finds that my compounded semaglutide is subpotent?

If you experience lack of efficacy or adverse effects, report it to the FDA’s MedWatch program. You may also have legal recourse against the pharmacy or provider, especially if they failed to disclose the risks. In 2025, a class-action lawsuit was filed against a compounding chain for selling diluted semaglutide, resulting in a $15 million settlement. Always keep your prescription label and batch number.

Will compounded semaglutide be completely banned in 2027?

There is no current legislation to ban compounded semaglutide entirely. However, as the shortage remains resolved, the FDA is likely to continue strict enforcement. Some states may pass additional restrictions. The most likely scenario is that compounding will remain legal only for patients with a documented medical need that cannot be met by FDA-approved products, and only through licensed 503B facilities.
