AI Agents Acting on Behalf of Users: What Rocket Money's Rowan Shows Us

Published August 25, 2026By ABD Legacy LLC

On August 25, 2026, Rocket Money — the personal finance app used by more than 10 million members — launched Rowan, an AI agent that goes beyond analysis. Instead of showing you a dashboard and hoping you act, Rowan watches your spending around the clock, texts you when it spots a way to save, and handles the task when you reply in plain language.[1][2] Powered by Anthropic, it renegotiates recurring bills, cancels subscriptions, and creates automated savings transfers.[1]

Rowan matters beyond personal finance: it's the clearest 2026 proof that AI agents have crossed from "chatbots that suggest" to "agents that act." AI agents are becoming the economy's biggest customers, and the technology that cancels a forgotten streaming trial is starting to handle customer service, bookkeeping, and sales follow-up for businesses like yours.

AI agents are software that don't just answer questions — they take action on your behalf. Rocket Money's Rowan, for example, watches your spending, cancels unused subscriptions, negotiates bills, and moves savings automatically after you approve it by text. In 2026, AI agents are moving from chat assistants to workers that complete real tasks.

What is Rowan, and what does it actually do?

Rowan is built into the Rocket Money app, but you use it through text, not a dashboard: it sends a text when it spots a savings opportunity, and you respond in normal words — no logging in, no hold music.[1] Rocket Money calls it a shift from "here's what you spent" to "here's how I can help."[1]

The forgotten streaming trial

You sign up for a free streaming month, use it twice, and forget it. About three weeks in, Rowan texts to ask if you want to cancel before the charge. You reply "cancel." Rowan contacts the service, cancels the trial, and confirms when it's done — one word, and the task is finished.[1]

The coffee round-up

You text Rowan: "Every time I buy a coffee, round up to the nearest dollar and put the extra change in my high-yield savings account." From then on, every coffee purchase triggers an automatic rounding into savings.[1] Set the rule once; the agent executes it daily — plus weekly spending summaries, low-balance alerts, and price-rise notices.[2]

How it's built — Anthropic, an agent stack, and human verification

Rowan is powered by Anthropic and built on "a sophisticated stack of AI agents trained with Rocket Money data."[1][3] Aaron Dignan, VP of Agentic Products: "It finds the money slipping through the cracks, the forgotten subscriptions, the bills that creep up, and it actually does something about them. That's the difference between knowing and acting."[1]

It isn't fully autonomous. Chase Adams, VP of AI Engineering: "We built it with adaptable agents where you need flexibility and strict code where you need certainty, and our team is engaged throughout to provide human verification."[3]

What "acting on your behalf" really means

Chatbot vs agent: the key difference

A chatbot suggests. An agent does. Ask a chatbot how to cancel a subscription and it writes instructions you still have to follow. Tell an agent to cancel it and it contacts the service, completes the cancellation, and confirms the result. That's the difference between knowing and acting — and why AI agents that act on your behalf change what software can do.

One-off approval vs standing permission

Rowan mixes both: a cancellation or negotiation is a one-off task you approve by text when proposed; a rule like "round up my coffee purchases and save the difference" is standing permission, set once and executed continuously.

Agentic AI for business: 4 use cases that work now

Customer service agents

Intercom's Fin 2 answers questions and takes actions for customers — updating a shipping address or adjusting a subscription on their behalf — with a 51% average resolution rate, 99.9% accuracy, and $0.99-per-resolution pricing charged only when Fin resolves something.[10] For the full math, see the cost-benefit case for AI customer service.

Finance & back-office agents

Rowan's model is the template: an agent that watches accounts and acts when it spots a problem. Starling Bank's August 2026 business assistant follows commands like "put 20% of last month's earnings aside for VAT" and flags invoice-fraud warning signs — in a year when invoice fraud cost UK businesses over £41m.[7] QuickBooks ships AI agents that automate bookkeeping, payroll, bill pay, and sales tax alongside your accountant.[8]

Sales / lead-response agents

Calendly's Callie is an AI assistant you add to an email thread; it checks availability, proposes times, and books the meeting once everyone agrees.[9] That removes a whole category of back-and-forth from the sales cycle — the same pattern as lead-response agents that qualify and schedule in minutes.

The risks and guardrails of agents that act

Security: when tools move from reading to acting

Microsoft's security team frames it precisely: when AI tools move from reading to acting, the blast radius changes. An agent that can send email, pay bills, or edit records is a bigger target than a chatbot that only answers questions — Microsoft's guidance treats agent access as a real security boundary, not a demo feature.[4]

Legal accountability: who is liable?

The short answer: it's still being figured out. Lawyers told Reuters in August 2026 that liability questions around AI agents are new territory, with the emerging view that liability follows the party directing the agent.[5] In practice, that likely means your business — not the vendor — is on the hook, so keep a human approving consequential actions.

Guardrails that work — Rowan's human-verification model

Rowan shows a workable middle path: agents propose, humans approve, agents execute and confirm. Adams' principle — adaptable agents where you need flexibility, strict code where you need certainty, with human verification throughout — is a good default posture for any business deploying agents.[3] If your agents touch money, read about AI agents that move money need guardrails and vet your AI vendors before you deploy.

How to decide if agentic AI is right for your business

Ask three questions before you buy anything:

  1. Is there a repetitive, rule-based task eating hours every week? If yes, that's the place to test an agent first.
  2. Can a mistake be contained? Cheap-to-fix errors are ideal early use cases; anything touching money, customer data, or public statements needs a human gate.
  3. Do you know what it actually costs? Agentic pricing is not always what it looks like — check what agentic AI really costs and measure automation ROI for small business before you scale.
