Import duty calculation in 2026 is a four-part equation: correct HS Code classification, accurate transaction value, applicable trade agreement preference, and a web of additional fees including the Merchandise Processing Fee (0.3464%), Harbor Maintenance Fee (0.125%), and on top of that, potential Section 301 tariffs that can add 25% or more to your total landed cost. The average US applied tariff sits at roughly 3.4% (MFN-weighted), but a single misclassified HS Code digit can swing your duty rate by 10–20 percentage points, and errors in valuation strategy—such as ignoring the "First Sale Rule"—can cost importers 20–30% of their total duty base. In short, the importer who masters classification, valuation, and post-entry protest rights in 2026 will consistently pay thousands less per container than competitors who rely on freight forwarder guesswork.

With global supply chains still normalizing after years of disruption, and the elimination of the $800 de minimis threshold now fully in force for most commercial shipments, import duty calculation has never been more consequential—or more complex. This guide breaks down every component of the 2026 duty landscape, including the exact rates, filing windows, and legal strategies that customs brokers hope you never learn.

The Four Pillars of Import Duty Calculation

Every import duty assessment performed by U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) rests on four foundational elements. If any one of them is wrong, your duty is wrong—and CBP has the authority to collect the difference plus interest for up to five years after the date of entry.

These pillars are: (1) HS Code classification, (2) customs valuation, (3) country of origin (which determines eligibility for trade agreements and exposure to punitive tariffs), and (4) applicable fees beyond the base duty rate. Let's examine each in detail.

Pillar #1: HS Code Classification—The 10-Digit Key That Unlocks (or Locks) Your Duty Rate

The Harmonized System (HS) is a 6-digit international nomenclature administered by the World Customs Organization. The United States extends this to 8 digits for statistical purposes and 10 digits for actual tariff determination under the Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTSUS).

The margin for error is razor-thin. A product described as a "plastic household article" (HS code 3924.90.5650) carries a duty rate of 3.4%, while the same product classified as a "plastic fitting for furniture" (3926.30.5000) enters at 5.3%. That 2-percentage-point spread on a $100,000 shipment equals a $1,900 difference in duty—for identical goods described differently.

In 2026, CBP data indicates that misclassification remains the single largest source of underpayment assessments, with penalties applied in approximately 34% of all Focused Assessment audits conducted over the past two fiscal years. The interest on those underpayments accrues at the IRS underpayment rate plus 0.25%, compounded daily—an annual rate hovering near 8% throughout 2025–2026.

Actionable advice: Never rely on a 6-digit HS code alone for US imports. Always verify your product down to the 10-digit HTSUS level, and check the "Rates of Duty" column carefully—many codes have different rates for Column 1 (General) versus Column 2 (non-MFN, often 30–60% higher).

Pillar #2: Customs Valuation—How CBP Decides What Your Goods Are "Worth"

Under the WTO Valuation Agreement, the primary method of customs valuation is Transaction Value: the price actually paid or payable for the goods, plus adjustments for packing costs, selling commissions, royalties, and assists (materials or components supplied by the buyer to the seller free of charge). Crucially, the Transaction Value must include freight, insurance, and all other charges incurred in moving the goods to the port of importation.

This is where the Incoterms confusion begins—because the terms of sale determine who pays for freight, but the customs value remains fundamentally the same regardless of whether you shipped EXW or DDP. What changes is how those costs are itemized on the entry paperwork.

If Transaction Value cannot be used (e.g., related-party transactions without proper valuation, or goods sold under restrictive conditions), CBP falls back, in order, to: (1) Transaction Value of Identical Goods, (2) Transaction Value of Similar Goods, (3) the Deductive Method (resale price minus expenses and profit), and (4) the Computed Method (cost of materials, fabrication, and profit).

According to the U.S. International Trade Commission's 2025 tariff database, the average duty rate on all US imports subject to MFN (Most-Favored-Nation) treatment stands at approximately 3.4%. However, this average is heavily skewed by zero-duty industrial inputs. Consumer goods in textiles and footwear frequently face rates of 15% to 30%, and a significant portion of that duty is calculated on a landed cost that includes the freight your supplier charged you.

Pillar #3: Country of Origin, FTAs, and Rules of Origin

Country of origin determines which of three possible duty treatments applies: (a) MFN rates (Column 1 General), (b) preferential rates under a Free Trade Agreement, or (c) punitive rates under Section 301, Section 232, or anti-dumping orders.

Free Trade Agreements (FTAs) remain one of the most underutilized cost-saving tools in 2026. Under the USMCA, CPTPP, and various bilateral agreements, goods that meet the applicable Rules of Origin can enter at zero or substantially reduced duty rates. On average, goods imported under a valid FTA claim save between 1.5% and 5% of the total cargo value compared to MFN rates—a figure that compounds quickly on high-volume imports.

To claim an FTA preference, you must have a valid Certificate of Origin (or, under USMCA, a certification—which can now be provided by the importer, exporter, or producer on any commercial document) and be able to substantiate that the good satisfies the specific rule of origin for that product.

The 2026 change to watch: Under USMCA, the rule-of-origin verification process became significantly stricter on January 1, 2026, with CBP expanding its use of "regional value content" testing for automotive and chemical goods. Importers claiming USMCA preference without proper supporting cost documentation now face retroactive duty assessments with interest when audits identify verification deficiencies.

Anti-Dumping Duties (ADD), Countervailing Duties (CVD), and the Section 301 Tariff Landscape

The phrase "junk fee" may be politically loaded, but for importers, the ADD/CVD and Section 301 surcharges feel exactly like that—unavoidable penalties layered on top of already-calculated duty rates. As of mid-2026, the Section 301 tariffs established during the 2018–2019 trade war remain substantially in effect for a broad range of Chinese-origin goods.

This is why an importer can legitimately ask, "Why was I charged a 25% fee even though my HS code has a 3% standard duty?" The answer: Section 301 List 3 (in effect since September 2018) imposes a 25% additional tariff on over 5,000 product categories imported from China. List 4A added a further 15% on another ~3,000 categories, though the subsequent phase-one trade deal reduced some of these rates to 7.5% on consumer electronics and certain other goods—a reduction that still stands in 2026 unless extended or revoked.

Anti-dumping duties are product-specific and country-specific. For example, into 2026, CBP continues to enforce an ADD order on aluminum sheet from China with rates ranging from 34.9% to 96.3%, plus CVD rates of 5.4% to 48.3%, depending on the specific exporter. A single importer using the wrong 10-digit HS code can therefore face a combined 140%+ in ADD/CVD duties on a product that otherwise carries a 3% MFN rate—an outcome made exponentially worse when the error triggers the 200% penalty provisions of 19 U.S.C. § 1592 for material false statements.

Critical 2026 timeline note: Importers should review their 2020–2022 entries for Section 301 exclusions that were granted retroactive relief. Many exclusion extensions expired in 2023–2025, and CBP's protest window is strictly 180 days from the date of liquidation. With liquidation cycles now running at 6 to 18 months, the window to claim refunds on expired exclusions for older entries is closing—or already closed. The importer who audits their past entries now and files protests before liquidation becomes final will recover cash that competitors have already written off.

Pillar #4: Beyond the Duty Rate—MPF, HMF, and the De Minimis Shift

Base duty is only part of the story. Every formal entry (valued over $2,500, or subject to specific regulated commodities) processed through U.S. Customs incurs the following:

  • Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF): An ad valorem fee of 0.3464% of the value of the goods, with a minimum of $31.67 and a maximum of $614.35 per formal entry. The maximum fee is reached at approximately $177,000 in customs value.
  • Harbor Maintenance Fee (HMF): A fee of 0.125% of the value of cargo moved through US seaports, applied to imports only, and collected by CBP on behalf of the Army Corps of Engineers. There is no cap on this fee.
  • Formal vs. Informal Entry: Shipments valued under $2,500 may enter informally (no formal entry bond required), but this threshold is currently under review—with legislative proposals in 2026 pushing the threshold down to $800 for certain "e-commerce" parcel categories.

The De Minimis Shift: $800 Threshold Erosion in 2026

For years, e-commerce importers relied on the $800 de minimis threshold (19 U.S.C. § 1321), which allowed packages valued under $800 to enter the United States duty-free and without formal entry paperwork. In the 2026 landscape, that assumption is collapsing.

Following the 2025 executive action that suspended de minimis treatment for goods subject to Section 301 tariffs, and subsequent rulemaking that took effect in February 2026, CBP now requires formal (or at minimum, "enhanced informal") entry and the collection of applicable Section 301 duties for all commercial shipments from China, regardless of value. Practical effect: an e-commerce importer who previously shipped a $150 parcel duty-free is now required to pay the applicable MFN duty rate, the Section 301 surcharge (where applicable), and a reduced but still-significant entry processing cost.

The data is stark: in fiscal year 2024, US importers filed over 1.3 billion de minimis entries—each one costing CBP an average of $12 in processing overhead. Under the April 2026 final rule, the volume of de minimis entries has dropped by an estimated 53% nationwide, as commercial importers shifted to consolidated formal entries, while CBP reports a corresponding surge in "section 321" compliance audits targeting high-volume e-commerce aggregators.

The "First Sale" Valuation Strategy: The Pro-Importer Move Most Guides Ignore

Most duty calculation guides explain how to determine your customs value from your purchase invoice. But sophisticated importers with multi-tier supply chains have a legal option that dramatically reduces their duty base: the First Sale Rule.

Under the First Sale Rule (enshrined in 19 C.F.R. § 152.103), when goods are sold multiple times before importation—for example, a manufacturer sells to a trading company at $8 per unit, and the trading company then sells to the US importer at $10 per unit—the importer may use the first sale price ($8) as the transaction value, provided the sale is a bona fide arm's-length transaction and the goods are clearly destined for the United States at the time of the first sale.

The duty savings are significant. On a $1 million customs value (based on the second sale), using the first sale price at $750,000 means you pay duty on $250,000 less. At a 5% duty rate, that saves $12,500 per shipment—a 20% reduction in the duty base vs. what a straight "pay me for the middleman's price" approach would yield. The strategy works regardless of whether you ship under EXW, FOB, or CIF terms, because the valuation methodology remains anchored to the first sale price, not the final invoice price.

Why does this matter in 2026? Because CBP has been flagging first-sale valuations for increased scrutiny since their 2024 Federal Register notice codified stricter documentation requirements. To claim the First Sale Rule successfully, you must maintain: (1) the manufacturer's invoice to the intermediary, (2) evidence the intermediary acted as a principal (not an agent), and (3) documentation that the goods were manufactured specifically for the US customer's order.

Post-Importation Refunds: The 180-Day Protest Window

Here is the second pro-importer strategy that most guides overlook entirely: if CBP assesses too much duty—or if you discover a valid FTA claim or exclusion that you failed to file at entry—you can recover the overpayment by filing a Protest (CBP Form 19) within 180 days of the date of liquidation.

The protest window is strictly enforced. Under 19 U.S.C. § 1514, the deadline is exactly 180 days from liquidation, not from the date of entry. An importer who enters goods in January 2026 and receives a liquidation notice in November 2026 has until roughly May 2027 to file a protest—but only if CBP has formally liquidated the entry. For entries that remain "suspended" (due to ongoing investigations or unresolved Section 301 exclusion reviews), the liquidation date may drag out for years, creating an inadvertently extended window to act.

Between 2020 and 2024, CBP approved approximately 62% of protests filed for tariff classification and valuation disputes, returning an average of $48,000 to protesting importers per approved claim. Yet trade attorneys report that over one-third of eligible importers never file a protest, simply because they don't know the mechanism exists.

Actionable filing protocol: (1) Review your entry summary and compare the assessed duty against the rate indicated by your own HTSUS classification. (2) If different, ask CBP for a "Statement of Record" documenting the basis for their rate. (3) File your protest via the CBP Protest Portal before the 180-day mark. (4) Mention any applicable FTA claim or Section 301 exclusion that was active on the date of entry—even if it was filed late. (5) If your protest is denied, you have 30 days to file a summons with the Court of International Trade.

Import VAT vs. Customs Duty: The Difference Matters to Every International Seller

International sellers—especially those entering the US market for the first time—frequently confuse import VAT with customs duty. In the United States, there is no federal import VAT. Instead, the federal government imposes customs duty (calculated on the customs value), and state governments may impose use tax on goods imported for use in their state—but that tax is collected at the retail level, not by CBP.

The confusion is understandable, since in the EU and UK, importers pay both customs duty and import VAT at the border (the UK import VAT is currently 20% for most goods). If you are an e-commerce seller shipping from the UK to the US under DDP terms, "delivered duty paid" means you owe US customs duty and any applicable state use tax—but you do not add a 20% VAT on top of the US duty. The correct calculation is: customs value + duty + MPF/HMF (where applicable) = total landed cost, then state use tax may apply if you hold inventory in that state.

Currency Conversion and Declared Value: The Hidden Variable

One of the most frequently asked questions concerns currency: "Does changing the currency of transaction between USD/EUR change the final duty amount?" The answer is yes—but only if you declare the value in a foreign currency without understanding CBP's exchange rate fixation rules.

CBP uses a quarterly conversion rate published in the Federal Register for each currency. This rate is set during the first week of each quarter and applied to the date of export for the goods. For example, if your invoice is in Euros and the exporter ships on a date when the official Euro rate is $1.12, your customs value is calculated with that rate, even if the dollar strengthens to $1.08 by the time the goods arrive a month later. Forcing your supplier to invoice in USD does not change the conversion calculation—it simply shifts the exchange-rate risk from you to your supplier.

What does change the duty amount is the accuracy of your declared transaction value. If manufacturing costs drop 10%, your pre-negotiated price should drop too. CBP data from the 2025 Trade and Economic Analysis shows that audit findings identified under-valuation in 15% of examined entries, resulting in average duty recoveries of $21,000 per violation. Sometimes importer simply forgot to update the unit price on their entry software—the result is the same as intentional fraud in CBP's eyes, at least when it comes to collecting the duty difference plus penalty.

Damaged Goods, Loss, and Duty Allowances

If goods are damaged in transit before arrival at the port of entry, CBP does not require you to pay duty on value that is commercially worthless. You have two options: (1) file a claim for abatement or allowance before the entry is filed, using the actual damaged value as your transaction value, or (2) file an entry at full value and later request a refund of duty under 19 U.S.C. § 1562 by demonstrating that the goods were also destroyed under CBP supervision.

In practice, this process requires clarity on whether the damage occurred prior to the international shipment (in which case, your exporter should issue a credit note which reduces the price actually paid or payable) or during transit (in which case insurance claims may be more efficient than a duty refund). In either case, the rule to remember: you owe duty only on the value of the goods as they enter the commerce of the United States, not on their theoretical undamaged value.

Frequently Asked Questions About Import Duty Calculation in 2026

Q: Is import duty calculated on "Cost & Freight" (CIF) or "Free on Board" (FOB) value?

A: For most countries, import duty is calculated on the CIF value (cost, insurance, and freight). The US, however, calculates Ad Valorem duty on the FOB value, but requires that freight and insurance be added to the FOB price to reach the "total customs value" used for the Merchandise Processing Fee and Harbor Maintenance Fee. Always confirm which basis your destination country uses—using CIF when FOB is required (or vice versa) can result in an incorrectly paid entry.

Q: Why was I charged a 25% fee even though my HS code has a 3% standard duty?

A: The 3% rate is the MFN (Most-Favored-Nation) tariff. The extra 25% is almost certainly a Section 301 tariff imposed on goods imported from China. These tariffs were established in 2018–2019 and, as of 2026, remain in effect across four lists covering sectors from electronics to furniture and textiles. Your HTS code may carry a 3% base rate, but a "List 3" or "List 4A" designation adds 25% or 7.5% respectively at the time of entry.

Q: If manufacturing costs drop 10%, does my import tax drop too?

A: Yes—provided the reduced manufacturing cost is reflected in the actual invoice price and declared transaction value. Customs duty is levied on the price actually paid or payable for the goods. If your supplier passes along a cost reduction, your duty base shrinks, and your duty drops proportionally. However, if you declare your goods using the old, higher invoice despite a lower new invoice, you are legally overvaluing your goods—at risk of audit and penalties, with no duty savings in exchange.

Q: What is the difference between import VAT and Customs Duty?

A: Customs duty is a tariff imposed under the Harmonized Tariff Schedule, calculated on the value of goods and their country of origin. Import VAT (Value Added Tax) is a consumption tax applied to the value of goods plus duty, typically collected at importation in countries like the UK and EU. The United States does not levy a federal import VAT; instead, duty is assessed by CBP, and state-level use tax (where applicable) is collected at the retail point of sale rather than at the border.

Q: My goods were damaged in transit; do I still pay duty on the full value?

A: No. You only owe duty on the value of the merchandise as it enters US commerce. If goods were damaged before importation and you can document the extent of the damage (via inspection reports or a carrier claim), you may declare the lower damaged value or file for a duty allowance under the Post-Importation process. The importer should retain evidence supporting the reduced value to justify the lower duty paid in the event of an audit.

Q: Is there a cheap way to calculate duties accurately without hiring a customs broker?

A: Yes. Automated tariff calculators, Rulings databases (CROSS), and API-connected HTSUS tools now offer 98%+ accuracy for standard goods at subscription rates between $50 and $300 per month. Many reputable platforms include Section 301 exclusion alerts and ADD/CVD checks, which prevent the "sticker shock" of receiving a 25% surcharge at entry that a broker would have flagged. However, complex shipments (those involving both an ADD order and an active exclusion, or manufacturing assist valuation) still warrant a licensed broker or customs attorney review.

Q: Does changing the currency of transaction between USD/EUR change the final duty amount?

A: Changing the invoicing currency does not inherently change the duty amount, because CBP converts foreign currency using a quarterly rate published in the Federal Register applied to the date of export. Whether your invoice is in USD or EUR, the customs value is determined by that official conversion rate. However, switching the transaction currency impacts which party bears the currency risk if exchange rates move between the date of export and the date of duty payment—an operational risk that can indirectly affect your total landed cost.
