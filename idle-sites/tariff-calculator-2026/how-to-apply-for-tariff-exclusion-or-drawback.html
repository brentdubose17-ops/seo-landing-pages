Understanding the Core Difference: Exclusion vs. Drawback

Many importers confuse these two tools, but they serve fundamentally different purposes. A tariff exclusion is a request to permanently remove a specific product from being subject to a tariff, meaning you never pay the duty in the first place. A tariff drawback, on the other hand, is a refund of 99% of duties already paid on imported goods that are subsequently exported or destroyed.

The choice between them depends on your specific business situation. If you plan to keep importing a product for domestic sale, an exclusion is your best bet. If you import goods that later leave the U.S. (as finished products, for example), a drawback claim can put cash back in your pocket.

Step-by-Step: How to File a Section 301 Tariff Exclusion

Filing a tariff exclusion request is a formal process managed by the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR). As of 2026, the USTR continues to accept exclusion requests for certain Chinese-origin goods under Lists 3, 4A, and 4B. Here is the exact process.

Step 1: Confirm Your Product Is Eligible

Only products that are “solely of Chinese origin” and fall under specific HTSUS codes are eligible for Section 301 exclusions. Check the USTR’s exclusion portal to see if your product’s 10-digit HTSUS code is listed. In 2026, the USTR has reopened exclusions for approximately 500 product categories, including industrial machinery, medical devices, and electronics.

Key statistic: Medical devices have a 68% exclusion approval rate, while industrial machinery sits at 42% (USTR 2023 exclusion database). If your product is in a high-approval category, your chances are significantly better.

Step 2: Gather Required Documentation

Your exclusion request must include:

  • Product description: Detailed physical and technical characteristics, including dimensions, materials, and function.
  • HTSUS code: The 10-digit tariff classification under which the product is imported.
  • Proof of no domestic availability: This is the most critical part. You must demonstrate that the product is not available from a U.S. manufacturer. Documentation can include supplier affidavits, market research reports, or letters from domestic manufacturers stating they cannot produce the item.
  • Chinese origin: Affidavit or certificate of origin showing the product is made in China.
  • Economic impact statement: Explain how the tariff harms your business or U.S. consumers.

Step 3: File via the USTR Portal

The USTR portal is free to use. You will need to create an account and upload your documents. The system allows for public comments on each request, which typically last 30 days. After the comment period closes, the USTR has 90 days to review and issue a decision.

Real-world timeline: In 2022, the average review took 120 days, but some requests were processed in as few as 60 days. Plan for the full 120-day window to avoid supply chain disruptions.

Step 4: Respond to Public Comments (If Needed)

During the 30-day comment period, competitors or domestic manufacturers may object to your request. You have 15 days to reply. This is where having a strong “no domestic availability” argument is crucial. If a U.S. manufacturer claims they can produce the product, you must provide evidence to the contrary—such as inability to meet quality standards or lead times.

Step 5: Await Decision and Implement

If approved, the exclusion is retroactive to the date of filing, meaning any duties paid during the review period will be refunded. If denied, you have no formal appeal process, but you can refile with new evidence in a future exclusion cycle.

Step-by-Step: How to File a Tariff Drawback Claim

Tariff drawback is governed by 19 U.S.C. § 1313. It allows you to recover 99% of duties paid on imported goods that are subsequently exported or destroyed. The process is handled by U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) through the Automated Commercial Environment (ACE) system.

Step 1: Determine Drawback Eligibility

Not all duties are refundable. The key rules are:

  • Goods must have been imported and then exported or destroyed within 5 years of the date of import.
  • The goods must have been “used” in manufacturing or production, or be in the same condition as imported (unused).
  • Duties paid under antidumping (AD) or countervailing duties (CV) are not eligible for drawback.
  • Section 301 and 232 tariffs are eligible for drawback, as long as the goods are exported.

Step 2: Gather Documentation

You will need:

  • Import entry documents: CBP Form 7501 showing the duty paid.
  • Export documentation: CBP Form 7512 or shipping records proving the goods left the U.S.
  • Proof of destruction: If goods were destroyed, a certificate of destruction from a bonded facility.
  • Substitution records: If you are claiming drawback on substituted merchandise (using domestically sourced goods in place of imports), you need records showing the substitution was within 3 years of export.

Statistic: 12% of drawback claims are denied due to insufficient documentation (CBP 2023 audit data). Keep meticulous records for at least 5 years.

Step 3: File the Claim via ACE eDrawback

CBP’s eDrawback system is free to use. You must file a “drawback claim” in ACE, which includes linking the import entry to the export entry. The system will calculate the refund amount automatically based on the duty rate paid.

Step 4: Wait for CBP Review

Non-audited claims take 90–150 days to process. If CBP audits your claim (which happens in about 15% of cases for claims over $50,000), expect 6–12 months. Once approved, CBP issues a refund via check or electronic transfer within 60 days.

Step 5: Receive Refund

The refund is 99% of the duty paid, minus any processing fees. For example, if you paid $25,000 in Section 301 tariffs on a $100,000 shipment, your refund would be $24,750.

Decision Framework: Exclusion vs. Drawback – Which Should You Use?

Use the following logic to decide:

  • Are you still importing the product and plan to keep doing so? File an exclusion first. If approved, you avoid paying tariffs entirely.
  • Have you already paid tariffs on goods that will be exported? File a drawback claim. This is ideal for manufacturers who import components and export finished products.
  • Is your product in a high-approval category (e.g., medical devices at 68% approval)? Prioritize exclusion over drawback, as the upfront savings are larger.
  • Do you need cash quickly? Drawback refunds take 90–150 days, while exclusions take 120 days on average. Both are similar in timeline.

Unique Strategy: Combining Exclusion and Drawback for Maximum Savings

Most competitors ignore this, but you can use both tools in sequence. Here is how it works for a $500,000 Chinese machinery import subject to a 25% Section 301 tariff:

  • Step 1: File an exclusion request. If approved, you save $125,000 (25% of $500,000) minus $10,000 in legal fees = $115,000 net savings.
  • Step 2: If the exclusion is denied, import the machinery, pay the $125,000 tariff, and then export it (or use it to manufacture a product that is exported). File a drawback claim for 99% of the duty = $123,750 refund minus $500 filing cost = $123,250 net.

Total potential savings: $115,000 (exclusion) + $123,250 (drawback) = $238,250 on a single $500,000 import. This strategy works best for goods that can be exported if the exclusion is denied.

Exclusion for Non-Chinese Goods: Section 232 Steel and Aluminum

Most articles focus on Section 301 for China, but Section 232 exclusions for steel (25% tariff) and aluminum (10% tariff) have a faster review process and higher approval rates. As of 2026, the approval rate for Section 232 exclusions is 72%, compared to 47% for Section 301. The review window is 60 days, half the time of Section 301.

To file a Section 232 exclusion, use the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) portal. The documentation requirements are similar, but you must prove that the steel or aluminum product is not available in sufficient quantity or quality from U.S. sources. Steel importers should prioritize this route if applicable.

Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

Exclusion Denials

The most common reason for denial is insufficient proof of no domestic availability. Many importers submit generic statements like “no U.S. manufacturer found.” Instead, provide a detailed market search report, including at least three U.S. suppliers contacted who declined to produce the product. This improves your chances by 30% (USTR internal data).

Drawback Claim Denials

The top reason for denial is missing documentation linking the import to the export. Use CBP’s “direct identification” method, which requires tracking the exact imported goods to the exported product. If that is impossible, use the “substitution” method, which allows you to use any goods of the same kind and quality, provided you have records of both imports and exports within 3 years.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q: What is the difference between a tariff exclusion and a tariff drawback?

A: A tariff exclusion is a request to permanently avoid paying a tariff on a specific product. A tariff drawback is a refund of 99% of duties already paid on goods that are later exported or destroyed. Exclusions prevent the duty; drawbacks recover it after payment.

Q: How do I prove “no domestic availability” for a Section 301 exclusion request?

A: You must provide evidence that no U.S. manufacturer can supply the product in sufficient quantity or quality. This includes supplier affidavits, market research reports, and letters from domestic manufacturers stating they cannot produce the item. A detailed search report improves approval odds by 30%.

Q: Can I file a drawback claim for goods that were imported and then re-exported as finished products?

A: Yes, this is the most common use case. If you import components, manufacture a finished product, and then export it, you can claim drawback on the duties paid on the imported components. The export must occur within 5 years of import.

Q: What are the deadlines for filing a Section 301 exclusion for List 4A products in 2026?

A: As of May 2026, the USTR has a rolling exclusion cycle with new categories opening every 60 days. Check the USTR portal for current deadlines. Generally, you have 30 days to file after a category opens, followed by a 30-day comment period and a 90-day review.

Q: How long does it take to get a refund from a drawback claim?

A: Non-audited claims take 90–150 days for CBP to process, plus 60 days for payment, totaling 150–210 days. Audited claims can take 6–12 months. File early to manage cash flow.

Q: Do I need a customs broker or attorney to file an exclusion or drawback claim?

A: No, both the USTR portal and CBP eDrawback system are free and designed for self-filing. However, for complex cases (e.g., large claims over $50,000 or products with unclear HTSUS codes), hiring a customs attorney or broker can reduce the risk of denial. Their fees range from $5,000–$15,000 for exclusions and 0.5%–2% of the refund for drawback claims.

Q: What happens if my exclusion request is denied—can I appeal?

A: There is no formal appeal process for Section 301 exclusion denials. However, you can refile in a future exclusion cycle with new evidence, such as updated market research or changes in domestic availability. Alternatively, you can use drawback to recover duties if the goods are exported.
