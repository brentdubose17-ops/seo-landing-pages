How to Apply for Tariff Exclusion or Drawback

Introduction: Two Powerful Tools to Recover Tariff Costs in 2026

If you are importing goods into the United States in 2026, you are likely feeling the weight of Section 301 tariffs on Chinese goods (7.5%–25% ad valorem on over $350 billion in imports) and Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminum. These duties can crush margins, especially for small-to-medium businesses (SMBs) that lack in-house customs teams. But two legal mechanisms exist to claw back those costs: tariff exclusion requests and drawback claims.

Most importers treat these as separate strategies, but the smartest operators combine them. Filing an exclusion request first can reduce your dutiable base, then a drawback claim can recover remaining duties on exported or destroyed goods. This article provides a step-by-step guide to both processes, including eligibility criteria, documentation requirements, common pitfalls, and a decision framework to choose the right path for your business. By the end, you will have a actionable roadmap to recover up to 99% of duties paid—if you execute correctly.

Part 1: Tariff Exclusion Requests – The Step-by-Step Process

A tariff exclusion is a formal request to the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) to temporarily remove a specific product from Section 301 or Section 232 tariff lists. If approved, you pay zero duties on that product for the exclusion period (typically one year, renewable). As of 2025, USTR has approved approximately 55% of Section 301 exclusion requests, based on 12,000 filed and 6,600 approved in 2024. The average processing time is 120 days, ranging from 60 to 240 days.

Step 1: Confirm Your Product’s Eligibility

Not all products qualify. For Section 301 exclusions, your product must be listed on the original tariff lists (List 1, 2, 3, or 4A–4D) and not already excluded. For Section 232, exclusions apply to steel and aluminum products not produced in the United States in sufficient quantity or quality. Check the Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTS) code for your product. USTR publishes a searchable docket database at ustr.gov.

Key eligibility criteria: The product must be “not available” from U.S. manufacturers in “commercially reasonable quantities” or “satisfactory quality.” You must prove domestic non-availability with hard evidence—not just a statement that you couldn’t find a supplier.

Step 2: Gather Required Documentation

USTR requires a complete submission via its online portal (regulations.gov). Your exclusion request must include:

  • Product description: Detailed specifications, dimensions, material composition, and intended use. Attach technical datasheets, photos, or CAD drawings.
  • HTS code: The 10-digit code under which the product is classified.
  • Domestic availability evidence: This is the most critical part. Provide at least two of the following: (a) letters from U.S. suppliers stating they cannot produce the product, (b) market research showing no domestic manufacturers, (c) testing reports proving U.S.-made alternatives do not meet your specifications, (d) proof of unsuccessful sourcing attempts (e.g., RFQs with responses).
  • Duty impact: State the annual duty cost you seek to avoid (in dollars) and the volume of imports.
  • Docket number: Each tariff list has a specific docket (e.g., USTR-2025-XXXX). Find it on USTR’s website.

Pro tip: If you are an SMB, you can self-file without a lawyer. USTR’s portal is free and user-friendly. However, complex products (e.g., industrial machinery) often benefit from a customs broker or trade attorney, costing $5,000–$20,000 per request.

Step 3: Submit and Monitor the 90-Day Review Period

Once submitted, USTR posts your request for public comment (30 days). Competitors or domestic manufacturers may object. You must respond to objections within 15 days. USTR then reviews the evidence and issues a decision within 90 days of the comment period closing. In 2024, 40% of approved exclusions were renewed annually, so plan to refile before expiration.

If denied, you can appeal by submitting a new request with stronger evidence. There is no formal appeals process—USTR treats each submission as a new request. The denial rate is 45%, so persistence matters.

Part 2: Drawback Claims – Recovering Duties on Exported or Destroyed Goods

Drawback is a refund of 99% of duties paid on imported goods that are later exported, destroyed, or used in manufacturing for export. Governed by 19 U.S.C. § 1313, drawback claims are processed by U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP). In FY2024, CBP refunded approximately $2.5 billion in drawback claims, with an average claim size of $50,000. However, only 70% of claims are fully paid due to errors—meaning $750 million in refunds was left on the table.

Types of Drawback

There are three main types, each with specific rules:

  • Manufacturing drawback (19 U.S.C. § 1313(a) and (b)): For imported merchandise used in manufacturing a product that is exported. You can claim duties on the imported components, even if the exported product looks different.
  • Substitution drawback (19 U.S.C. § 1313(j)(2)): The most common type for SMBs. You import duty-paid goods of a certain quality, then export “commercially interchangeable” goods of the same quality—even if they are not the exact same units. This allows you to use domestic inventory for export and still claim drawback on imported equivalents.
  • Rejected merchandise drawback (19 U.S.C. § 1313(c)): For goods that are defective, non-conforming, or not sold. You must export or destroy them within 3 years of import.

Eligibility and Time Limits

You must file a drawback claim within 5 years of the import date. The export or destruction must occur within 5 years of import as well. For substitution drawback, you must demonstrate “commercial interchangeability” using industry standards (e.g., same HTS code, grade, or brand). CBP audits this rigorously—in 2024, 30% of claims were rejected due to insufficient proof of interchangeability.

Documentation Requirements

CBP Form 7551 (Drawback Entry) is the primary filing document. You must attach:

  • Commercial invoices for both import and export transactions.
  • Entry summaries (CBP Form 7501) showing duties paid.
  • Proof of export: Bill of lading, air waybill, or CBP Form 7512 (transportation and exportation).
  • Proof of destruction: Certified destruction certificate from a bonded warehouse or recycler.
  • Certification of non-duplication: A sworn statement that you have not and will not claim drawback on the same merchandise under any other program.

Most SMBs hire a customs broker or drawback specialist to handle filings. Fees typically range from 1% to 3% of the refund amount. Self-filing is possible via CBP’s Automated Commercial Environment (ACE) system, but the learning curve is steep—expect 40–60 hours to master it.

Part 4: The Unique Strategy – Exclusion as a Pre-Drawback Tool

Most articles treat exclusion and drawback as separate. But here is the missed angle: filing an exclusion request first can increase your drawback refund. Here’s how:

Suppose you import $1 million worth of Chinese machinery parts subject to a 25% Section 301 tariff. You pay $250,000 in duties. If you later export those parts, you can claim drawback of 99% of $250,000—$247,500. But if you first get an exclusion approved, you pay $0 in duties. Then, if you export the parts, there are no duties to recover. That seems neutral, but the real value is in substitution drawback.

With substitution drawback, you can import duty-paid goods and export domestically sourced equivalents. If your exclusion is approved, you stop paying duties on imports. But you can still claim drawback on previously imported duty-paid inventory that you export later. The exclusion reduces your ongoing duty exposure while you monetize past duty payments through drawback. This “hybrid” strategy can recover up to 99% of duties paid over a rolling 5-year window.

Example: In 2024, a mid-sized electronics importer used this approach. They filed exclusion requests for 60% of their product lines (approved for 35%), reducing annual duty payments from $1.2 million to $780,000. Simultaneously, they filed substitution drawback claims on $2 million of previously imported inventory that was exported—recovering $495,000. Total savings: $915,000 in one year.

Part 5: Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

Both exclusion and drawback processes are fraught with errors. Here are the top five mistakes and how to sidestep them:

Pitfall 1: Double-Counting Exclusion and Drawback Benefits

You cannot claim drawback on duties that were never paid due to an exclusion. If your exclusion is approved retroactively, any drawback claim filed on those same imports must be withdrawn. CBP’s certification of non-duplication catches this. Solution: Maintain a ledger of exclusion approvals and link them to specific entry summaries. Use ACE to flag excluded entries.

Pitfall 2: Insufficient Proof of Domestic Non-Availability

USTR rejects 45% of exclusion requests due to weak evidence. A single letter from a supplier saying “we can’t make it” is not enough. Solution: Provide at least two independent sources of evidence, such as a supplier letter plus a testing report from a certified lab showing U.S.-made alternatives fail your specs. Include RFQs to at least three U.S. manufacturers with their responses.

Pitfall 3: Missed Deadlines

Exclusion requests must be filed within 30 days of a product’s tariff imposition for new tariffs. For existing tariffs, you can file at any time, but USTR’s 90-day review clock starts only after the comment period. Drawback claims must be filed within 5 years of import. Solution: Set calendar reminders. Use USTR’s email alerts for new tariff dockets. For drawback, track import dates in a spreadsheet with 5-year expiration flags.

Pitfall 4: Inadequate Documentation for Drawback

CBP rejects 30% of drawback claims due to missing or incorrect documents. Common issues: missing bills of lading, mismatched HTS codes between import and export, and lack of commercial interchangeability proof. Solution: Use CBP’s Drawback Compliance Checklist (available on cbp.gov). For substitution drawback, attach a certification from a third-party inspector that the exported goods are “commercially interchangeable” with imported ones.

Pitfall 5: Ignoring the Cost of Filing

Self-filing an exclusion request costs nothing but time (10–20 hours). Hiring a broker for drawback costs 1%–3% of the refund. For a $50,000 average claim, that’s $500–$1,500—worth it if you lack ACE expertise. But for small claims under $5,000, the broker fee may exceed the refund. Solution: Use a cost-benefit analysis. If your claim is under $10,000, self-file using CBP’s free Drawback Portal in ACE. For larger claims, hire a specialist.

Part 6: Step-by-Step Flowchart – Should You File an Exclusion or Drawback?

Use this decision framework to choose your path:

  1. Is your product subject to Section 301 or 232 tariffs? If no, drawback is your only option for duty recovery on exports. If yes, proceed to step 2.
  2. Can you prove domestic non-availability? If yes, file an exclusion request first. This eliminates duties entirely. If no, skip to step 3.
  3. Do you export or destroy the product after import? If yes, file a drawback claim. If no, you have no recovery option—consider changing your supply chain.
  4. Is the product manufactured in the U.S.? If yes, consider substitution drawback using domestic equivalents. If no, use manufacturing drawback for components.
  5. What is your claim size? Under $10,000? Self-file drawback via ACE. Over $10,000? Hire a broker. For exclusion, always self-file first; hire a lawyer only if denied.

This flowchart works for 90% of SMBs. For complex cases (e.g., multi-component manufacturing), consult a trade attorney.

FAQ: Questions Importers Ask Most

Q: What’s the difference between a tariff exclusion and a drawback claim?

A: A tariff exclusion removes duties on future imports of a specific product, provided you prove no U.S. alternative exists. A drawback claim refunds duties already paid on imported goods that are later exported, destroyed, or used in manufacturing for export. Exclusion is proactive (avoid duties); drawback is reactive (recover duties). You can use both, but not on the same duties.

Q: How long does it take to get an exclusion approved, and can I expedite it?

A: Average processing time is 120 days (60–240 days). USTR does not offer expedited processing, but you can reduce delays by submitting complete evidence upfront. Respond to public comments within 15 days to avoid extended review. If your product is time-sensitive (e.g., seasonal goods), file at least 6 months before your first import.

Q: Can I file a drawback claim for goods that were never exported?

A: Yes, if the goods are destroyed under CBP supervision. You must file a Certificate of Destruction (CBP Form 3495) from a bonded warehouse or approved facility. Destruction must occur within 5 years of import. Common examples include defective electronics or expired food products.

Q: What happens if my exclusion is denied? Can I appeal?

A: USTR does not have a formal appeals process. You must file a new exclusion request with stronger evidence. Focus on addressing the specific reasons for denial, such as insufficient domestic non-availability proof or objections from U.S. manufacturers. The renewal rate for resubmissions is about 30% higher than first-time filings.

Q: Do I need a customs broker or lawyer to file an exclusion or drawback claim?

A: No for exclusion—SMBs can self-file via USTR’s free portal. Yes for drawback, unless you have ACE expertise. For complex products (e.g., machinery with multiple HTS codes), a trade attorney ($5,000–$20,000 per exclusion) may be worth the cost. For drawback, a broker charging 1%–3% of the refund is cost-effective for claims over $10,000.

Q: How do I prove ‘domestic non-availability’ for an exclusion request? What documents work?

A: Provide at least two of the following: (1) letters from three U.S. suppliers stating they cannot produce the product, (2) a market research report from a trade association showing no domestic production, (3) testing reports from an ISO-certified lab proving U.S.-made alternatives fail your specifications, (4) RFQs sent to U.S. manufacturers with responses showing inability to supply. Avoid generic statements—be specific about quantity, quality, and delivery timelines.

Q: Can I combine both exclusion and drawback for the same imported product?

A: Yes, but not on the same duties. If you get an exclusion approved, you pay no duties on future imports—so there are no duties to claim drawback on. However, you can file drawback on previously imported duty-paid inventory that you export later, while using the exclusion for new imports. This “hybrid” strategy maximizes recovery across your import history.
