How to Calculate Import Duties from China: The 2026 Importer’s Playbook

Importing goods from China in 2026 requires a fundamentally different calculation than it did in 2017. The average U.S. tariff on Chinese imports now sits at approximately 19.9%—up from just 3.1% before the 2018 trade war—and the effective rate on specific goods can exceed 100% once Section 301, Section 232, and anti-dumping duties are stacked. To calculate your true landed cost, you must identify the correct 10-digit HTSUS code, determine the Transaction Value (price paid + freight + insurance), layer on all applicable trade war tariffs, and account for fees like the Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF) at 0.3464%. This guide walks you through every step of the calculation, including the Section 321 de minimis exemption, Incoterms implications, and rarely-discussed cost recovery tactics like Post-Summary Corrections that can reclaim overpayments within 180 days.

Why Import Duty Calculation Is Harder in 2026

The U.S. tariff landscape in 2026 is the most fragmented it has been in decades. Importers cannot simply look up a single duty rate and apply it to their invoice—they must navigate four overlapping tariff regimes: Most-Favored-Nation (MFN) rates, Section 301 tariffs on Chinese goods, Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminum, and potential anti-dumping/CVD orders that apply to specific products from specific Chinese manufacturers.

According to U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) data, roughly 60-70% of all U.S. import duties were historically collected on goods from China—but this figure is projected to drop below 50% by 2026 as supplier diversification accelerates. That matters because it signals that CBP is actively scrutinizing Chinese origin claims more aggressively than ever, making accurate classification and valuation critical.

Step 1: Nail Your HS Code and HTSUS Classification

Every duty calculation begins with a tariff classification. Chinese suppliers will frequently provide an HS code—often a 6-digit international code—but this is only the starting point. For U.S. imports, you must classify your goods under the 10-digit Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States (HTSUS), which adds country-specific statistical suffixes and duty rate qualifiers.

The 6-Digit vs. 10-Digit Problem

A Chinese supplier quoting HS code 9403.60 (wooden furniture) only tells you the international heading. The U.S. HTSUS breaks this down further: 9403.60.80 covers wooden furniture for non-bedroom use at a 0% MFN rate, yet it still faces a 25% Section 301 tariff. Misclassifying by even one subheading digit can change your duty rate by 10 percentage points or more—and CBP does not accept "my supplier told me" as a defense.

Q: Can I use the tariff code provided by my Chinese supplier, or do I need a U.S. one?

A: You must use a U.S. HTSUS code. Chinese suppliers typically provide a 6-digit international HS code, but the U.S. requires a full 10-digit HTSUS classification. The last four digits determine your specific duty rate, Section 301 applicability, and any anti-dumping or quota requirements. Relying on the supplier's code risks misclassification penalties, which CBP can assess at up to 20% of the duty underpaid.

How to Classify: The Rules of Interpretation

Classification follows the General Rules of Interpretation (GRI). Rule 1 states that classification is determined by the terms of the headings and any section or chapter notes. Rule 3(b) dictates that composite goods are classified by their "essential character." For example, an electric toothbrush with a replacement head is classified not as a brush but as an electromechanical appliance under 8509.80—a distinction that changes the duty from 6.7% to 0% plus a 7.5% Section 301 surcharge.

The single best move for a new importer is to request a binding ruling from CBP for a frequently-imported product. It costs between $177 and $182 per ruling request and provides ironclad protection against future reclassification disputes.

Step 2: Determine the "Transaction Value" — Not Just the Unit Cost

The second pillar of any duty calculation is valuation. U.S. customs law under 19 U.S.C. 1401a requires that duties be assessed on the "Transaction Value," which is defined as the price actually paid or payable for the merchandise, plus certain statutory additions. Critically, this includes packing costs, royalties, and any assists (like molds or tooling provided free to the supplier)—but it does not automatically include freight.

The FOB vs. CIF Value Trap

Here is the most common error we see in duty calculations: assuming the duty basis is the FOB factory price when the supplier quotes CIF. If you purchase goods on CIF (Cost, Insurance, and Freight) terms, the international freight and insurance are included in the price actually paid for the goods—and they are dutiable. If you purchase on FOB (Free on Board) terms, the freight is NOT part of the transaction value, and your duty basis is lower.

This distinction can reduce your dutiable value by 10-15% on heavy goods. For a container of ceramic tiles valued at $20,000 FOB with $3,000 in ocean freight, the FOB shipment carries an MFN duty of 8.5% on $20,000 ($1,700). The CIF shipment carries the same duty on $23,000 ($1,955)—a 15% difference that shaves directly into your margin.

The "Value-Add" Exclusion that most competitors miss: CBP allows importers to exclude international freight from the transaction value when the goods are sold on FOB terms and the freight is separately stated on the commercial invoice, even if you pay the freight yourself. This is the single easiest reduction in your landed cost, and it requires no special licenses—just correct Incoterm usage.

Step 3: Layer the Tariff Rates — MFN + Section 301 + Section 232

Once you have the correct HTSUS code and transaction value, the calculation multiplies the dutiable value by the applicable rate. But in 2026, "the applicable rate" is almost never a single number for Chinese goods.

Understanding MFN Rates

The MFN (Column 1 General) rate is the baseline tariff applied to all WTO members, including China. These rates range from 0% (for many electronics and raw materials) to 30% or higher for sensitive goods like apparel and footwear. The column 2 (non-MFN) rates, which apply to North Korea and Cuba, are dramatically higher and are not relevant to Chinese imports.

Section 301 Tariffs: The China Trade War Surcharge

Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 authorizes the USTR to impose duties on goods from countries engaging in unfair trade practices. As of 2026, Chinese goods face Section 301 tariffs in four distinct tranches, covering over $550 billion in annual imports:

  • List 1 (September 2018): 25% on $34 billion of industrial machinery, electronics, and medical devices
  • List 2 (August 2018): 25% on $16 billion of plastics, chemicals, and engines
  • List 3 (September 2018): 25% on $200 billion of consumer goods, furniture, and textiles
  • List 4A (September 2019): 7.5-15% on $120 billion of smartphones, laptops, toys, and apparel

The USTR is currently conducting a statutory four-year review of these tariffs, and by mid-2026, we expect finalized increases on strategic electronics and clean energy components—specifically a 5-10% hike on semiconductors, lithium-ion batteries, and certain rare earth magnets.

Let us look at a real-world example. A smartphone has a 0% MFN rate but faces a 7.5% Section 301 tariff (List 4A). A lithium-ion battery faces a 3.4% MFN rate plus a 25% Section 301 tariff—bringing the total effective rate to 28.4%. That is not a typo, and it is why every 2026 importer must check both columns before pricing.

Section 232: Steel and Aluminum Surcharges

Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 imposes a 25% ad valorem tariff on steel imports and a 10% tariff on aluminum imports, regardless of origin, unless excluded. But the 2026 wildcard is the proposed "Green Tariff" shift—CBP is piloting a carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM) that would add an environmental import fee of 2-4% on Chinese steel and aluminum goods above their existing Section 232 rates. Although the U.S. CBAM proposal is legally contested, several pilot programs already require importers to report embedded carbon emissions at entry.

Step 4: The Section 321 De Minimis Exemption ($800)

No discussion of 2026 import duty calculations is complete without addressing Section 321. The de minimis provision allows shipments valued at $800 or less entered into the United States to be imported duty-free and without formal entry. This exemption has become the backbone of cross-border e-commerce from China, with CBP reporting that de minimis shipments grew from 139 million in 2015 to over 1 billion in 2024.

Proposed 2026 Restrictions

The America Creating Opportunities for Manufacturing in the U.S. Act (ACOMI Act) proposes to exclude Chinese goods from Section 321 eligibility entirely. As of May 2026, the legislative text is in committee review, but CBP has already begun enforcing stricter documentation requirements for all de minimis shipments, including the collection of the ultimate consignee's identity to prevent consolidated shipments from being split into multiple sub-$800 entries—a practice known as "de minimis slicing."

If the ACOMI Act passes, every Chinese shipment under $800 will face the full MFN + Section 301 tariff stack that applies to formal entries. For an $800 shipment of consumer electronics at a 7.5% Section 301 rate, that is an additional $60 per package—a cost spike that will be passed directly to U.S. consumers.

Who Pays the Duty for Amazon FBA Shipments?

A frequent client question: if you import directly to an Amazon FBA fulfillment center, who pays the duty? The answer is unambiguous: you—the Importer of Record—are responsible for all duties, taxes, and fees at the border. Amazon does not act as your customs broker, does not advance duty payments, and its "FBA Delivery to Amazon" terms will not cover duties unless you ship DDP via an approved carrier partner.

If you ship DDP (Delivered Duty Paid), the supplier or freight forwarder may prepay the duty on your behalf, but they will invoice you back-with a markup. DDP does not mean you are exempt from duty—it simply changes who writes the check at the border.

Step 5: Factor MPF and Other Fixed Fees

Beyond ad valorem tariffs, CBP assesses the Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF) on all formal entries (value over $2,500). As of 2026, the MPF is 0.3464% of the entry value, with a minimum of $31.67 and a maximum of $614.35. Every importer must include this in their calculation or face a negative P&L surprise at the margin.

Breaking Down the Fees

  • MPF (Merchandise Processing Fee): 0.3464%, min $31.67, max $614.35 per entry
  • HMF (Harbor Maintenance Fee): 0.125% of the cargo value for ocean shipments arriving through U.S. ports—a flat tax that many novice calculators omit
  • Customs Broker Fee: Typically $75-$150 per entry if you use a licensed broker
  • Antidumping/CVD cash deposits: Applied as a per-unit or ad valorem deposit on goods subject to AD/CVD orders (e.g., wooden furniture from China at 4.24%, mattresses at 2.22%)

Step 6: The Post-Clearance "Check Engine Light" — Duty Drawback and PSCs

Most importers assume that once CBP liquidates an entry, the duty bill is final. In reality, U.S. customs law provides two powerful cost-recovery mechanisms that savvy 2026 importers exploit:

Post-Summary Corrections (PSC)

Under 19 CFR 103.31, an importer can file a Post-Summary Correction within 180 days of the date of entry to correct errors in classification, valuation, or duty rate. If you overpaid duty due to a clerical error or a misapplied rate, a PSC can reclaim the difference via a refund check from CBP. There is no downside cost—only the time to compile the evidence and file via the Automated Commercial Environment (ACE) system.

Duty Drawback

Duty drawback under 19 U.S.C. 1313 allows you to recover 99% of duties paid when you subsequently export the imported goods (or destroy them under CBP supervision). For goods that arrive, are warehoused, and are re-exported without entering U.S. commerce, the matching duty draw-back is the most direct path to cash recovery. Drawback claims can be processed within 60-90 days if filed electronically, and the recovery rate pencils out to roughly 1% of your duty paid as a net benefit after filing costs.

Country of Origin: The Vietnam Trap

A product made in China but shipped from Vietnam still incurs Chinese tariff rates. Country of Origin for customs purposes is determined by "substantial transformation"—the place where the product undergoes its last significant manufacturing process. Simply relabeling, packaging, or assembling in Vietnam does not change origin.

A 2024 CBP ruling (HQ H325456) confirmed that assembling a Chinese-manufactured bicycle frame with Vietnamese components in Vietnam does NOT confer Vietnamese origin because the frame's essential character remains Chinese. This "origin vs. shipment" trap has ensnared hundreds of importers attempting to dodge Section 301 tariffs by routing through third countries—and the penalties are severe: fraud penalties of up to three times the loss of revenue, plus potential criminal liability.

Calculating the Total: A Practical Worksheet

Here is a complete, step-by-step worksheet for 2026 that you can reproduce in any spreadsheet:

  1. Enter the FOB price per unit (the price the supplier charges at the Chinese port).
  2. Add freight and insurance if your Incoterm includes them (for CIF, CFR, or DDP shipments only).
  3. Multiply by the total quantity to get the invoice value.
  4. Add assists and packing costs (tooling, molds, or free packing materials).
  5. Apply HMF of 0.125% if arriving by ocean.
  6. Determine the total duty rate: MFN rate + Section 301 rate + Section 232 (if steel/aluminum) + AD/CVD (if applicable) + carbon adjustment fees.
  7. Multiply the dutiable value by the total rate to get duty liability.
  8. Add MPF (0.3464%, min $31.67, max $614.35).
  9. Add broker fees ($75-$150).
  10. Sum steps 3, 7, 8, and 9 to get your true landed cost.

Example: You import 2,000 LED light strips at $4.50 FOB each (invoice value $9,000) with $600 in ocean freight prepaid (not included in FOB). MFN duty is 0%, Section 301 adds 7.5%. HMF adds 0.125% of $9,000 ($11.25). MPF adds 0.3464% of $9,000 ($31.67 minimum). Your total duty is $675 + $11.25 + $31.67 = $717.92, or roughly 8% of the invoice value. A 2026 online calculator that fails to add Section 301 would quote you only $42.92.

The Green Tariff Shift: What Most Articles Miss

By May 2026, CBP has begun piloting its Environmental Import Fee program, initially targeting steel and aluminum from China. While the legal framework is still being contested in the Court of International Trade, the agency is already collecting data on embedded carbon for shipments over 1,000 metric tons. Expect an additional 2-4% environmental fee on Chinese steel products once the pilot concludes, which will layer onto the existing 25% Section 232 rate plus the MFN rate.

Importers of Chinese steel fasteners, structural steel, and aluminum extrusions should build this potential 4% contingency into their 2026-2027 pricing models now. Delaying forward contracts until the rule clears could leave you facing an unhedged duty spike of 3% or more on your entire annual steel import volume.

  2. Switch all supplier quotations to FOB terms and separately state international freight on your commercial invoice.

Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

Beyond the arithmetic, full compliance requires vigilance in three areas. First, the U.S. does not charge VAT on imports; a 20% VAT figure from your Chinese supplier is either an error or a service markup, not a legal U.S. government charge. Second, if your supplier quotes a price that bundles freight with the goods ("CFR Shanghai"), you must add that freight to the transaction value even if it is only implied. CBP guidance requires importers to allocate at least 25% of bundled freight costs to the dutiable value when the freight is not separately stated—a stance that adds surprising liability to DDP transactions.

Third, never file entries until you have confirmed whether the product falls under an anti-dumping duty order. The difference between an AD case (e.g., wooden cabinets from China at 4.24% or mattresses at 2.22%) and a non-AD product is the difference between a profitable import and a total loss.

FAQs on China Import Duty Calculation

Q: Does the China tariff apply to the product cost or does it include shipping?

A: It depends entirely on your Incoterm. If you purchase on CIF or DDP terms, the freight and insurance are part of the "price actually paid" and are dutiable. If you purchase on FOB or EXW terms and the shipping is separately stated on the commercial invoice, no international freight is added. Choosing FOB terms with separately stated freight can reduce your dutiable value by 10-15% on ocean shipments.

Q: What is the difference between Section 301 and anti-dumping duties (AD/CVD)?

A: Section 301 tariffs (7.5-100%) are imposed by the USTR as leverage against China's unfair trade practices like intellectual property theft and forced technology transfer. Anti-dumping and countervailing duties (AD/CVD) are remedial penalties imposed by the U.S. Department of Commerce when foreign goods are sold below fair market value or benefit from unfair government subsidies. The two can stack: a Chinese product can face both a 25% Section 301 tariff AND a separate AD duty expressed in dollars per unit.

Q: If I import directly to an Amazon FBA fulfillment center, who pays the duty?

A: You, as the importer of record, are solely responsible for all duties, MPF fees, and taxes. Amazon does not advance payments or act as a customs broker. If you ship under DDP terms, your freight forwarder may prepay the duty but will invoice you back with a 15-30% markup. The only way to avoid duty at the border is to qualify for the Section 321 de minimis exemption (value under $800 per shipment) or claim a valid exclusion before the shipment departs China.

Q: Does "DDP" (Delivered Duty Paid) from China mean I do not have to pay duty?

A: No. DDP simply transfers the duty payment obligation from the buyer to the seller (or the seller's agent) at the point of delivery. The duty is still paid—just not by you directly. You will pay for it indirectly through a higher unit price or a separate invoice from the freight forwarder. DDP is a convenience, not an exemption, and some shippers quote DDP without correctly calculating Section 301 tariffs, leaving you with a post-delivery duty bill from CBP.

Q: How do I get a refund if I overpaid at customs?

A: File a Post-Summary Correction (PSC) through ACE within 180 days of the original entry date. Attach a corrected entry summary with the proper HTSUS code or valuation and explain the error. CBP typically processes PSC refunds within 30-60 days and will send the refund directly to the payer. You can attempt to correct entries older than 180 days via a prior disclosure, which requires flagging the error to CBP yourself (and does not include a guaranteed refund).

Q: Does the manufacturer declare the correct HS code, or should I trust a broker?

A: Neither. Chinese manufacturers commonly classify products under the broadest international heading—often inflating the duty rate—because their export system ignores U.S. Section 301 additions. Your licensed customs broker files the entry you ultimately submit, so you are responsible for the accuracy of every field. The correct approach is to obtain a written classification opinion from your broker, then file a CBP ruling request for full legal protection on anything you import more than twice a year.

Bottom Line for 2026 Imports from China

The era of a single lookup table for China import duties is over. To remain profitable, you must master HS classification, monitor the Section 301 review outcome, choose FOB Incoterms to minimize dutiable value, and file PSCs to reclaim any overpayments. With an average effective duty rate of 19.9% on Chinese goods in 2026—and with CBP increasing targeted audits on Chinese origin and valuation—the cost of getting these calculations wrong compounds quickly.

Use a reputable duty calculator that accounts for Section 301, HMF, MPF, and AD/CVD contingencies. Cross-check your duty estimates with a licensed broker before placing your first large order, and re-run your calculations at least quarterly to account for tariff schedule changes.
