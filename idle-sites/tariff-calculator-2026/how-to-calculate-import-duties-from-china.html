How to Calculate Import Duties from China: The Complete 2026 Landed-Cost Playbook

Calculating import duties from China in 2026 requires stacking four separate cost layers: the base Most Favored Nation (MFN) tariff rate, any Section 301 punitive tariffs still in effect, the Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF), and freight-based charges like the Harbor Maintenance Fee (HMF). The average U.S. tariff on Chinese goods now stands at 19.9% versus 3.3% for most other countries, and the Section 301 moratorium means rates are actively in flux heading into mid-2026. This guide breaks down the exact HS code lookup process, the FOB-vs-CIF valuation trap, the "Middleman Trap" of routing through Hong Kong, and the recent shifts in the $800 de minimis threshold that affect direct-to-consumer shipments. By the end, you will be able to compute a true DDP landed cost with confidence—and avoid the classification errors that cost importers thousands in overpaid or underpaid duty.

The HS Code: Your Tariff Calculation's Ground Zero

You cannot calculate a single dollar of import duty without first identifying the correct 10-digit Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTS) code for your product. The U.S. uses the HTSUS (Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States), maintained by the U.S. International Trade Commission (USITC). Every imported good—from a lithium-ion battery to a stamped steel bracket—falls under exactly one 10-digit code that determines its base duty rate, its Section 301 exposure, and any anti-dumping or countervailing duties that may apply.

The first six digits of an HTS code are internationally standardized under the World Customs Organization's Harmonized System. The final four digits are country-specific breakout codes. If you export the same product to the EU, those last four digits will differ from your U.S. classification—this is a common source of confusion for first-time importers who try to reuse a European CN code for a U.S. entry.

Step-by-Step HS Code Identification

Start with the USITC HTS Search tool or the Customs Rulings Online Search System (CROSS) database. Do not guess based on product name alone; search by material composition, function, and end use. For example, a silicone phone case could be classified under Chapter 39 (plastics) or Chapter 42 (leather goods) depending on its primary material, and the duty difference can exceed 5%.

The most reliable method is a three-pass approach. First, search the HTS by keyword and review the top 20 results. Second, read the "Section Notes" and "Chapter Notes" for your candidate chapters—these contain the legal definitions that resolve ambiguous classifications. Third, cross-reference your shortlist against binding ruling letters on the CROSS database to see how U.S. Customs has ruled on substantially identical products.

Parts vs. Finished Goods Classification

One of the most consequential classification decisions is whether your product qualifies as a "part" of a larger article or as a finished good in its own right. This distinction frequently changes the duty rate by 5 to 15 percentage points. Rule 2(a) of the General Rules of Interpretation (GRI) states that a reference to an article covers incomplete or unfinished versions if they retain the essential character of the complete article—but parts that are shipped separately and require further fabrication often fall under different headings entirely.

Take a practical example from 2025 electronics imports: A completed LED driver circuit board intended for a television qualifies under HTS 8529.90 (parts of television receivers), which carries a 5% MFN duty plus Section 301 List 4A tariffs. But the same board imported as a standalone universal power supply could fall under HTS 8504.40 (static converters) at a different rate. When in doubt, consult a licensed customs broker before you ship. A broker's classification fee of $75–$150 is trivial compared to the cost of a post-entry correction, which includes penalties that can reach 20% of the undervalued duty.

MFN vs. Section 301 Tariffs: Decoding Your True Rate in 2026

Every product imported from China faces two overlapping tariff structures: the base MFN tariff that applies to most World Trade Organization member nations, and the additional Section 301 tariffs imposed under the Trump administration's trade actions. Your total duty rate is calculated as MFN rate plus any Section 301 percentage that applies—not a compounding multiplication but a simple additive stack. A product with a 3.9% MFN rate on List 3 pays a total of 28.9% (3.9% + 25%).

Base MFN Rates Explained

Most Favored Nation tariffs are the standard statutory rates that apply to imports from China, the EU, Japan, and most other countries with normal trade relations. These rates vary wildly by product category—from zero on many raw materials and pharmaceuticals to 30% or more on footwear and certain textiles. The average MFN rate applied to Chinese goods in 2025 was approximately 3.3% for non-301 products, which mirrors the general U.S. average.

The MFN rate is your baseline, but for Chinese imports it is almost never the rate you actually pay. The Section 301 surcharges effectively quadruple or even quintuple your effective tariff burden on affected goods.

Section 301 Lists and Their 2026 Status

The Section 301 tariffs, authorized under the Trade Act of 1974, were applied in four waves. List 1 (2018) covers $34 billion of goods at a 25% rate. List 2 (2018) adds $16 billion at 25%. List 3 (2019) targets $200 billion of goods at 25%. List 4A (2019) sweeps in roughly $120 billion of consumer goods—including many electronics, apparel, and household items—at an initial 15% rate, later reduced to 7.5% under the Phase One trade deal in early 2020.

Critical for May 2026: The Section 301 tariffs were set for a four-year statutory review in 2025. The USTR initiated that review in May 2024 and announced in September 2025 that tariffs would remain in place, but under a transitional moratorium that has kept actual collections in flux. As of the first quarter of 2026, List 3 (25%) and List 4A (7.5%) remain in force for non-excluded products, but the USTR continues to grant retroactive exclusions for specific product categories. You must check the current USTR exclusion status within 30 days of any shipment, because a newly granted exclusion can retroactively refund duties on entries not yet liquidated.

Exclusion Lists and How to Check Them

The USTR maintains 549 individual exclusions across Lists 3 and 4A as of February 2026. These exclusions carry their own 10-digit HTS codes and require that the product meet a specific physical description—a generic description is not sufficient. The exclusion process is product-specific: you cannot assume that because a similar product received an exclusion, yours qualifies.

To check your status, search the USTR Section 301 exclusion portal or the CROSS database using your exact manufacturing specifications. If your product appears on an exclusion list, file the exclusion code on your entry summary (CBP Form 7501) at the time of import. Retroactive filing is possible for entries still within the liquidation window (typically 314 days), but do not rely on this as a planning strategy.

The Complete Landed-Cost Formula: Beyond the Duty Itself

Calculating import duty is only half the financial picture. Your true "dutiable value" forms the base on which duty is assessed, and getting that base wrong is the single most common error this calculation guide exists to prevent.

FOB vs. CIF vs. DDP: Which Value Is Dutiable?

U.S. Customs assesses duty on the "transaction value" of your goods, which under 19 U.S.C. §1401a includes the price paid or payable plus certain enumerated additions. The critical rule: for ocean freight, duty is assessed on the CIF value (cost, insurance, freight) — meaning you pay duty on your freight cost and insurance premium too. For air freight, duty is assessed on the FOB value (free on board), and freight is excluded from the dutiable base—but the international freight must still be declared to CBP even if it is non-dutiable.

This creates a significant planning lever. If you ship a $10,000 order by ocean freight with a $1,500 freight charge and $200 of insurance, you pay duty on $11,700 (CIF). If you ship the same order by air with a $2,200 freight cost, you pay duty on $10,000 (FOB) because air freight is excluded from the dutiable value. The duty differential at a 25% total rate is $425 on ocean versus $2,500 on air—even though air freight nominally costs more, the duty savings can partially offset the freight premium for small, high-value shipments.

MPF and HMF: The Fees Everyone Forgets

The Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF) is a flat fee per entry, adjusted annually based on inflation and collected by U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP). For fiscal year 2026, the MPF ranges from $485 to $587 per entry for formal entries, with the exact figure depending on the ad valorem calculation cap of 0.3464% of the entered value. Informal entries (shipments valued under $2,500) incur a much lower fee of roughly $6–$9, but these are rare for commercial China imports.

The Harbor Maintenance Fee (HMF) applies exclusively to ocean cargo moving through U.S. ports and equals 0.125% of the value of the cargo. On a $50,000 container, that is $62.50—small individually but significant when aggregated across a year of shipments. Air freight shippers avoid HMF entirely, which is another reason air shipments of high-value, low-volume goods can be financially competitive despite higher per-kilo rates.

Rules of Origin and Valuation: What You're Actually Paying On

Determining whether your product is genuinely "Made in China" for tariff purposes requires more than checking the label on the factory floor. The country of origin is determined by "substantial transformation"—meaning the product must undergo a manufacturing process in China that changes its name, character, or use from its component parts. Simply assembling components in China from Korean or Japanese parts may or may not qualify, depending on the complexity of assembly.

This rule becomes critically important when you attempt to route products through third countries to avoid Section 301 tariffs. Customs has aggressively pursued "circumvention" investigations against transshipment through Vietnam, Malaysia, and Thailand. If CBP determines that your Vietnamese-assembled product is actually Chinese in origin due to insufficient transformation, you face retroactive duty assessments, penalties, and potential criminal referral. In 2025 alone, CBP issued over 40 circumvention determinations involving Chinese goods routed through Southeast Asia.

The Middleman Trap: Why Hong Kong Does Not Save You

A persistent myth among new importers is that invoicing through a Hong Kong subsidiary or trading company changes the country of origin. It does not. Country of origin is determined by where the goods are manufactured or substantially transformed, not where they are invoiced, shipped from, or paid for. A product manufactured in Shenzhen and invoiced from a Hong Kong shell company is still a Chinese product for tariff purposes, and CBP will assess Section 301 duties accordingly.

The same logic applies to "free trade agreement" routing. The USMCA (formerly NAFTA) provides zero-tariff treatment only for goods substantially transformed in a member country—Mexico or Canada. A product of Chinese origin that merely passes through Mexico on its way to the U.S. does not qualify for USMCA preferences, and claiming otherwise constitutes fraud under 19 U.S.C. §1592.

Transaction Value and Assist Issues

The "transaction value" method under GATT Article VII requires that you declare the price actually paid or payable for the goods, with specific statutory additions. These additions include commissions, packing costs, royalty or license fees, and—critically—the value of any "assists." An assist is any item you provide to the supplier free of charge or at reduced cost that is used in producing the imported goods. If you ship molds, dies, tooling, or engineering drawings to your Chinese factory, the value of those assists must be added to the transaction value of the imported goods.

Importers routinely understate or omit assist values, exposing themselves to undervaluation penalties that can reach 20% of the duty underpaid. The correct approach: maintain a detailed inventory of all tooling shipped to your supplier and add its depreciated value to each entry until fully amortized. Customs auditors examine assist valuation closely in post-entry reviews, and the documentation burden is entirely on the importer.

De Minimis (Section 321) and Duty Drawback in 2026

The $800 de minimis threshold under Section 321 has become the most controversial provision in U.S. customs law, and the rules have changed meaningfully entering 2026. Under this provision, shipments valued at $800 or less entering the U.S. are exempt from duty and formal entry requirements. The provision was originally designed for personal shipments and bona fide gifts, but e-commerce platforms exploited it at massive scale. By 2024, approximately $1.2 billion in goods was being shipped daily under the de minimis provision, overwhelming CBP's inspection capacity.

The $800 Threshold Shifts in 2026

Proposed legislation introduced in early 2025, which gained further traction through late 2025, would categorically exclude Section 301 goods from the de minimis exemption—meaning any Chinese product subject to List 3 or List 4A tariffs would immediately lose its $800 exemption even for low-value shipments. As of May 2026, the administrative rulemaking is still under review, but CBP has already increased sampling and inspection of de minimis shipments from China. The operational reality for 2026 is this: do not structure your shipping strategy around de minimis avoidance if your products are on Section 301 lists.

Undervaluing a shipment to fall below the $800 threshold is a federal offense under 19 U.S.C. §1481, and CBP has stepped up penalties for split shipments designed to evade formal entry. If CBP determines you deliberately undervalued, expect penalties of up to 20% of the dutiable value per violation, plus potential forfeiture of the goods.

Duty Drawback: Recovering Duty on Re-exported Goods

If you import finished goods into the U.S. and later re-export them, the duty drawback provision under 19 U.S.C. §1313 allows you to recover up to 99% of the duties paid on the imported merchandise, provided you file the proper claim within five years of the original importation. Manufacturing drawback and unused merchandise drawback are the two categories that apply to most China importers. The process requires meticulous record-keeping linking the import entry to the export documentation.

Drawback claims are increasingly digitized through the Automated Commercial Environment (ACE), and the average processing time for a complete claim in 2025 was approximately 90 days. Drawback is not free money—you must pay a processing fee—but for high-volume importers who re-export a portion of their inventory, drawback can recover tens of thousands of dollars annually.

Section 301 Decision Tree for 2026

Apply this rapid assessment to any product before you quote a landed cost. First, identify your correct HTS code. Second, determine if your code appears on the USTR's Section 301 List 3 or List 4A annexes. Third, check the current exclusion status. Fourth, apply the additive rate stack. Fifth, add MPF, HMF (if ocean), and your broker's fee. Sixth, verify whether any anti-dumping or countervailing duty orders apply to your specific product from China. The final figure is your true duty obligation.

If your product is on List 4A but has an active exclusion, your total duty may be just the MFN rate. If your product is on List 4A without an exclusion, add 7.5%. If your product is on List 3, add 25%. Products on List 1 or List 2 add 25%. Chinese solar panels, steel fasteners, and certain chemical products carry additional anti-dumping duties that can exceed 200%—these ship under separate orders entirely and are a category unto themselves.

Risk Matrix: When to Use an FTZ or Bonded Warehouse

Importers rarely consider the strategic option of a Foreign Trade Zone (FTZ) or bonded warehouse to defer duty payments. Under FTZ rules, you can import goods into a U.S. zone without paying duty until the goods enter U.S. commerce. If the goods are re-exported from the zone, no duty is ever paid. This creates a powerful cash-flow tool for importers who hold inventory in anticipation of export orders.

2026 Compliance Warning: Post-Importation Price Adjustments

One of the least-known valuation rules concerns post-importation price adjustments. Under 19 C.F.R. §152.103, if you pay a supplemental amount to your supplier after the goods have been entered—for example, a retroactive price increase or a quality-related settlement—you are required to file a post-importation increase to the declared value. Many China importers routinely ignore this obligation, particularly when paying end-of-year bonuses or rebates to suppliers. Customs considers these payments part of the transaction value and will assess additional duty plus interest if discovered during audit.

The reverse situation—post-importation decreases—generally requires the goods to have been entered within the liquidation window, and the decrease must be documented in the original sale contract. Do not attempt to reduce your declared value after the fact based on informal supplier credits. The documentation burden favors Customs substantially in these disputes.

Practical Steps to Calculate Your Duty Right Now

Begin by assembling your commercial invoice, packing list, and product specification sheets. Identify your 10-digit HTS code using the USITC search tool, then verify your Section 301 list status via the USTR annexes. Check the current exclusion list for your product's code. Calculate your total additive rate, multiply by your dutiable value (CIF for ocean, FOB for air), then add MPF and HMF as applicable. Finally, add your customs broker's clearance fee ($75–$150 per entry typically) and any inspection or examination charges.

Use a dedicated tariff calculator that updates its Section 301 logic in real time, since the moratorium and exclusion process is fluid through 2026. Always build a contingency buffer of 3–5% on top of your calculated landed cost to absorb rate adjustments or unexpected valuation increases. Under-budgeting duty by even 1% on a $500,000 annual import volume means $5,000 of unplanned cash outflow—and under-provisioning by 10% is a common cause of margin wipeouts for first-time importers.

Q: How do I calculate import duty from China to the USA?

A: Identify the correct 10-digit HTS code, find the MFN rate, add any applicable Section 301 rate (25% for List 3, 7.5% for List 4A as of early 2026), multiply by the dutiable value (CIF for ocean freight, FOB for air freight), then add MPF ($485–$587 per entry) and HMF (0.125% for ocean cargo). A $10,000 ocean shipment of a product with a 25% total stack on a 3.9% base MFN rate would incur approximately $2,925 in duty plus fees.

Q: What is the difference between FOB and CIF when calculating duty?

A: FOB (Free on Board) excludes freight and insurance from the dutiable value, while CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight) includes them. For ocean shipments, U.S. Customs assesses duty on the CIF value. For air shipments, duty is assessed on the FOB value—air freight charges are declared but not dutiable. This creates a real cost difference: a $1,500 ocean freight charge on a $10,000 order adds $375 in duty at a 25% rate, whereas the same freight by air adds nothing to the duty base.

Q: Do I have to pay customs fees if I ship via DHL or FedEx?

A: Yes. Express carriers like DHL and FedEx are required to file formal entries for commercial shipments valued over $2,500, and they typically charge an advanced clearance fee of $25–$85 per shipment on top of the actual duty and MPF owed. For shipments under $2,500, informal entry applies with a reduced MPF of approximately $6–$9, but the duty rate itself remains unchanged. Express carriers also pay the duty on your behalf and bill you afterward, usually with a small advancement surcharge.

Q: How can I avoid paying import duty from China?

A: There is no legal way to completely avoid duty on Chinese goods that are genuinely manufactured in China, but you can reduce it. Check for active Section 301 exclusions that can drop the rate from 25% to the base MFN level. Consider restructured product specifications that fall under a lower-rated HTS code—provided the classification is legally accurate. Use an FTZ if you plan to re-export the goods. What you cannot do is undervalue the shipment, misdeclare the country of origin, or route through a third country without substantial transformation—all of which carry penalties that dwarf any duty you are trying to save.

Q: What happens if I undervalue my shipment to avoid the $800 de minimis threshold?

A: Deliberately undervaluing a commercial shipment to fall under $800 is a violation of 19 U.S.C. §1481 and can result in penalties of 20% of the true dutiable value, forfeiture of the goods, and referral to the U.S. Department of Justice for repeated violations. CBP has significantly increased de minimis inspections for shipments from China since 2025, and split-shipment patterns are a red flag for automated targeting systems. The expected administrative change that would exclude Section 301 goods from de minimis entirely makes this strategy both illegal and increasingly unworkable.

Q: Is the 25% tariff on Chinese goods still in effect in 2026?

A: Yes, but with nuance. The Section 301 tariffs on List 3 remain at 25% and List 4A at 7.5% for products without active exclusions. The USTR completed its statutory four-year review in September 2025 and elected to keep the tariffs in force under a moratorium framework, meaning rates are subject to change with limited notice. Importers should check the USTR exclusion portal within 30 days of any shipment, as new exclusions are granted retroactively and can produce refunds on entries not yet liquidated.

The Bottom Line for 2026 Importers

Calculating import duties from China in 2026 is a multi-step process that punishes shortcuts. Master the HS code identification, verify your Section 301 status and exclusions, compute your dutiable value correctly under FOB vs. CIF rules, and stay current on the changing de minimis landscape. The single most valuable habit you can build is a monthly audit of your tariff calculation inputs—because the Section 301 moratorium means rates can shift at any time, and the importer of record carries full liability for every misstep.

For every shipment you plan, build your landed cost with a 3–5% contingency buffer, maintain complete documentation of assists and post-importation payments, and work with a licensed customs broker who monitors the exclusion lists actively. The cost of getting this wrong is measured not in a single shipment's duty discrepancy, but in compounding penalties, audit exposure, and supply chain hold-ups that destroy margins built on inaccurate calculations.
