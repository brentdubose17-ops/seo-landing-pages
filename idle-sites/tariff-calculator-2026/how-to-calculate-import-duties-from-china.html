How to Calculate Import Duties from China in 2026

Calculating import duty on Chinese goods in 2026 means stacking five or more separate charges on the same entered value: base Column 1 General (MFN) duty, Section 301 tariffs, Section 232 tariffs where applicable, IEEPA tariffs, AD/CVD cash deposits, plus merchandise processing and harbor maintenance fees. For a typical consumer product like a $10,000 television under HTS 8528.72.64, that stack produces total duty and fees of $5,937.14 — a 59.4% effective rate — calculated on the FOB transaction value, not the landed CIF value. The critical structural point most importers get wrong: these layers are additive, not compound. Section 301, Section 232, IEEPA, and AD/CVD all apply to the same declared customs value, never to a post-duty base. Get the HTS code wrong, declare the wrong Incoterms value, or miss an AD/CVD case, and the error compounds through customs liquidation, penalty exposure, and five years of audit lookback.

Rate volatility warning: tariff rates on Chinese goods change by executive order, USTR Federal Register notice, and CBP CSMS message — sometimes within days. Every rate below must be verified against the current 2026 HTSUS, the Federal Register, and CBP's CSMS bulletins before you rely on it for a filing or a pricing decision.

The China Duty Stack: Why 2026 Math Is Different

Before 2018, calculating duty on a Chinese import was a one-line exercise: look up the HTS code, read the Column 1 General rate, multiply by value. For a huge share of consumer goods that rate was zero.

That world is gone. US goods imports from China totaled $438.9 billion in 2024, and by May 2025 the Peterson Institute for International Economics estimated the average US tariff on Chinese goods at 52.5%, versus roughly 19.3% before 2025. The change is not a single tariff — it is a layered stack governed by different statutes, different agencies, and different effective dates.

High-value entries pay a smaller effective MPF rate. The FY2025 MPF cap of $634.62 means a $10,000 shipment pays 0.3464% ($34.64), but a $1,000,000 shipment pays only 0.063% — roughly one-fifth the rate. This matters when you model landed cost across SKU price tiers.

Step 1: Classify the Product — the 10-Digit HTS Code

Every calculation starts with the Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States code. US Customs and Border Protection requires classification at the 10-digit statistical level for entry. Classification is legally binding on the importer of record — a broker's suggestion is not a defense.

Classification decision tree

  1. Determine the essential character of the product. What is it, in ordinary commercial terms?
  2. Find the 2-digit chapter in the HTSUS (e.g., Chapter 85 = electrical machinery).
  3. Narrow to the 4-digit heading using the heading text and the Section/Chapter Notes.
  4. Apply the General Rules of Interpretation (GRI 1–6) in order. GRI 3(b) — essential character — resolves most multi-material goods.
  5. Select the 6-digit subheading, then the 8-digit US rate line, then the 10-digit statistical suffix that matches the actual product specification.
  6. Confirm with CBP via a binding ruling request (e-rulings) if the classification is ambiguous or the value at stake is material.

China origin and substantial transformation

Duty rates attach to the country of origin, not the country of shipment. Goods assembled in Vietnam from Chinese components are generally Vietnamese-origin if the components underwent substantial transformation — a new name, character, or use. Simple assembly, packaging, or minor finishing does not qualify.

Avoiding Chinese tariffs through a nominal third-country stop is the single most common compliance failure. CBP's EAPA (Enforce and Protect Act) investigations and False Claims Act suits have produced eight-figure penalties against importers who transshipped without genuine transformation. If your supply chain has any China content, document the transformation analysis before you file.

Step 2: Determine Dutiable Value — the US Uses FOB, Not CIF

This is where European and Asian importers consistently overpay in their models. The United States appraises imported merchandise under transaction value (19 U.S.C. § 1401a), which is essentially an FOB-based concept: the price actually paid or payable for the merchandise, plus specified additions, but excluding international freight and insurance if separately identified.

Incoterm What the buyer pays Freight & insurance in US customs value? Practical impact
FOB / FCA Goods + inland to port of export No (if separately stated) Cleanest valuation; lowest duty base
CFR / CIF Goods + international freight (+ insurance) No — can be deducted if separately identified on the invoice Requires an itemized invoice to strip freight
DAP Goods + freight to US destination Yes, freight must be deducted with evidence Importer must obtain carrier invoices to reduce duty
DDP Everything, including duty Yes — duty paid by seller is not deducted from value Highest duty base; risk of under-declaration by seller

Additions and deductions that change the number

  • Assists: tooling, molds, dies, and engineering provided free or at reduced cost to the Chinese supplier must be added to value. A $40,000 mold amortized over 20,000 units adds $2.00 per unit to entered value.
  • Royalties and license fees: dutiable if related to the imported merchandise and paid as a condition of sale.
  • First-sale vs. last-sale: if there is a genuine bona fide sale between the manufacturer and a middleman before the US sale, you may appraise at the first-sale price. On a 30% margin structure this can cut entered value by 20%+, which cascades through every layer of the duty stack.
  • Currency conversion: use the CBP-published exchange rate for the quarter in which the entry is filed.

Step 3: Apply Each Layer of the Stack

Base MFN duty

The US average applied MFN rate is roughly 2.2%, but averages hide the spread. Many electronics and industrial inputs enter at 0%. Apparel runs 11%–32%. Footwear ranges 6%–48%, with some athletic styles at 37.5% plus per-pair specific rates. Always read the actual rate line — some HTS codes use specific duties (cents per kilogram or per unit) that do not scale with price.

Section 301

Section 301 tariffs apply by HTS subheading, not by product description. If your code is on a list, the rate applies to the full entered value.

301 list Rate Status Notes
List 1 25% Active Effective July 6, 2018; ~$34B of goods
List 2 25% Active Effective Aug. 23, 2018; ~$16B
List 3 25% Active Effective Sept. 24, 2018; ~$200B; exclusions expired for most lines
List 4A 7.5% Active Reduced from 15% on Feb. 14, 2020
List 4B 15% (suspended) Suspended indefinitely Never took effect
2024 increases 25%–100% Active, phased 2024–2026 EVs 100%; solar cells 50%; semiconductors 50%; syringes/needles 100%; EV batteries 25%

Exclusions are the highest-leverage item on this table. USTR exclusion grants are product-specific, time-limited, and retroactive only when the notice says so. A single valid exclusion on a high-volume SKU can save more than any sourcing negotiation.

Section 232 — including derivatives

Steel and aluminum tariffs moved to 25% on March 12, 2025 and then to 50% on June 4, 2025. Critically, the 232 program extends to derivative articles: finished goods containing steel or aluminum — furniture, appliances, machinery housings, fasteners — can be assessed on the metal content value even when the HTS code is not a primary metal code.

If your Chinese product has meaningful steel or aluminum content, model 232 exposure separately. Many importers discover this only when CBP issues a CF-28 request for material content documentation.

IEEPA tariffs

The IEEPA fentanyl tariffs added 10% effective February 4, 2025 and another 10% effective March 4, 2025, for a total of 20% on all China-origin goods. The reciprocal IEEPA tariffs announced April 2, 2025 escalated from 34% to 84% to 125% within nine days, then were reduced to 10% for a 90-day period beginning May 14, 2025.

IEEPA tariff authority has been the subject of active federal litigation. Importers should preserve entry documentation, monitor Federal Register notices and CBP CSMS messages, and file protests where a rate change creates a refund opportunity. Refunds on unliquidated entries are not automatic.

AD/CVD — the hidden killer

Antidumping and countervailing duty orders routinely dwarf every other layer. Chinese aluminum extrusions carried a final dumping rate of 376.85% in 2024. Solar cells, steel products, tires, furniture, and countless other categories carry China-wide rates from 50% to over 400%.

Step 4: Fees, Entry Type, and the De Minimis Change

The $800 de minimis exemption (19 U.S.C. § 1321) was eliminated for China and Hong Kong-origin shipments effective May 2, 2025. In FY2024, more than 1.3 billion de minimis packages entered the United States, the majority from China. Every one of those shipments now requires an entry, an HTS code, and a duty calculation.

For postal shipments from China, CBP applied a transitional rule from May 2 to June 1, 2025: duty equal to 30% of value or $25 per item, whichever was greater, rising to $50 per item after June 1, 2025. Verify the current postal regime before quoting a per-item cost.

Step 5: Worked Example — $10,000 Television from China

Assume a $10,000 FOB Shenzhen shipment of televisions classified under HTS 8528.72.64, shipped by ocean freight, no AD/CVD order in scope.

Component Rate Base Amount
Base MFN duty 3.9% $10,000 $390.00
Section 301 (List 3) 25% $10,000 $2,500.00
IEEPA fentanyl 20% $10,000 $2,000.00
IEEPA reciprocal (90-day reduced rate) 10% $10,000 $1,000.00
Subtotal duty 58.9% — $5,890.00
MPF 0.3464% $10,000 $34.64
HMF (ocean) 0.125% $10,000 $12.50
Total duty + fees 59.37% — $5,937.14

Note what is not in the base: the $1,200 in international ocean freight and cargo insurance, which is excluded because the invoice is written FOB and the freight is separately identified. Under a CIF-based calculator, that freight would have added roughly $707 in avoidable duty at a 58.9% stack rate.

Add brokerage (typically $75–$150 per entry), port fees, and drayage, and the all-in landed cost lands near $16,200 on a $10,000 invoice — before any AD/CVD exposure.

Step 6: The Tariff Calculator 2026 Workflow

  1. Enter the 10-digit HTS code. The calculator pulls the base MFN rate and flags whether the subheading appears on Sections 301, 232, or an AD/CVD order.
  2. Enter the declared value and the Incoterm. Selecting FOB or FCA strips freight and insurance automatically; selecting CIF or DAP prompts you to itemize deductions.
  3. Confirm country of origin. China and Hong Kong trigger the full IEEPA stack and the de minimis prohibition.
  4. Select transport mode. Ocean adds HMF at 0.125%; air, truck, and rail do not.
  5. Add freight, insurance, brokerage, and port fees in the landed-cost block.
  6. Read two outputs: (a) total landed cost per unit, and (b) the cash deposit you must fund at entry. These differ — cash deposit includes AD/CVD at the deposit rate, which may be later reconciled at liquidation.
  7. Log the calculation date. Because rates change by executive order and Federal Register notice, a saved calculation without a timestamp is worthless.

Legitimate Ways to Lower the Bill

  • First-sale valuation. Document a genuine sale between manufacturer and middleman. On a 25% margin, this can reduce entered value by roughly 20%, cutting every percentage-point layer proportionally.
  • Duty drawback. Recover up to 99% of duties paid on imported goods that are subsequently exported (or destroyed under CBP supervision, or used in the manufacture of exported goods). With a 59% effective rate, drawback is often the largest single recovery available.
  • Foreign Trade Zone (FTZ) admission. Defer duty until the goods leave the zone, or elect the "privileged foreign status" rules to pay duty on the finished product rate if lower. FTZs also defer AD/CVD until withdrawal.
  • Section 301 exclusions. Monitor USTR Federal Register notices and CSMS for open exclusion windows.
  • Scope rulings for AD/CVD. If your product may fall outside a case scope, request a ruling before entry. Post-entry scope challenges are expensive and rarely successful.
  • Tariff engineering. A legitimate design change — altering a component so the classification lands in a lower-rate subheading — is legal if it changes the product's essential character and is not a sham. Document the commercial rationale, not just the tariff motive.

Common Mistakes That Cost Real Money

  • Compounding instead of adding. Applying 25% to a base that already includes 3.9% overstates duty. Every layer applies to entered value.
  • Using CIF value. Overpaying duty on international freight for years without realizing it.
  • Ignoring derivative 232 exposure. Assuming 50% steel tariffs only hit steel HTS codes.
  • Treating de minimis as still available. It is not, for China-origin goods.
  • Missing an AD/CVD order at entry. CBP assesses the deposit retroactively plus interest, and can pursue penalties for negligence.
  • Relying on a static rate table. Rates changed multiple times in a nine-day window in April 2025.

Q: How do I calculate total import duty on a product from China in 2026?

A: Add every applicable layer on the same entered value: base Column 1 General duty + Section 301 + Section 232 (if applicable) + IEEPA tariffs + AD/CVD cash deposits, then add MPF (0.3464%, min $32.71, max $634.62 in FY2025) and HMF (0.125% for ocean cargo). For a $10,000 FOB television under HTS 8528.72.64 with 3.9% base + 25% Section 301 + 30% IEEPA, that totals $5,937.14 — a 59.4% effective rate. The layers are additive, never compounded.

Q: What is the correct HTSUS code for my Chinese product?

A: You must classify at the 10-digit statistical level using the HTSUS General Rules of Interpretation, starting from the product's essential character and working down through chapter, heading, subheading, and statistical suffix. Classification is legally the importer's responsibility, not the broker's. For ambiguous or high-value items, request a binding ruling from CBP via the e-rulings system — it is free and gives you legal certainty for the life of the ruling.

Q: Does the $800 de minimis exemption still apply to China shipments?

A: No. The Section 321 de minimis exemption was eliminated for China and Hong Kong-origin merchandise effective May 2, 2025. Every shipment now requires an entry, an HTS classification, and full duty calculation — even a $20 order. For postal shipments, CBP applied a transitional rate of 30% of value or $25 per item from May 2 to June 1, 2025, then $50 per item afterward. Verify the current postal rule before quoting per-item landed cost.

Q: Are Section 301 tariffs added on top of base duty and IEEPA tariffs?

A: Yes. Section 301, Section 232, IEEPA, and AD/CVD are separate legal authorities, and each is assessed on the entered value independently. They stack additively on the same base — none of them applies to a post-duty amount. This additive structure is precisely why a product with a 3.9% base rate can carry a 58.9% or higher effective rate in 2026. As of September 15, 2026 that list is no longer complete: IEEPA tariff authority was held unlawful on February 20, 2026 (Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump, No. 24-1287). A fifth mechanism is pending in Congress: H.R. 5334 would add up to 500 percent duties on Russian goods and up to 100 percent secondary duties, but it is not law as of September 15, 2026. See who can impose each of these duties.

Q: Is duty calculated on FOB or CIF value for US imports?

A: The United States appraises under transaction value, which is FOB-based. International freight and insurance are excluded from customs value if separately identified on the commercial invoice. If your supplier quotes CIF, DAP, or DDP, request an itemized invoice showing freight and insurance separately so you can deduct them. This alone can save 5%–60% of the freight amount in duty, depending on your stack rate.

Q: What fees besides duty do I pay, and how do I calculate AD/CVD exposure?

A: Beyond duty you pay MPF (0.3464% of entered value, capped at $634.62 in FY2025), HMF (0.125% for ocean cargo only), brokerage ($75–$150 per entry), and port/drayage charges. AD/CVD exposure requires a case-scope analysis: search Commerce's AD/CVD case list by HTS code and product description, confirm whether your exact product falls within the published scope, identify the manufacturer for producer-specific rates, and post the cash deposit at entry. China-wide rates frequently exceed 100% — aluminum extrusions ran 376.85% in 2024 — so a missed AD/CVD order can turn a profitable import into a catastrophic one.

Q: Can first-sale or duty drawback lower my China import duty?

A: Both can, and materially. First-sale valuation, if you can document a genuine bona fide sale between the manufacturer and a middleman, can reduce entered value by 15%–25% and therefore every percentage-point layer of the duty stack. Duty drawback recovers up to 99% of duties paid on goods that are later exported, destroyed under CBP supervision, or used in manufacturing exported goods — with a 59% effective duty rate, that is the largest recovery lever available to most importers. Both require rigorous documentation and should be set up before you import, not after.

Bottom Line

The mechanics of calculating Chinese import duty in 2026 are straightforward: classify at 10 digits, declare FOB transaction value, and add each statutory layer to the same base. The difficulty is rate volatility and AD/CVD exposure. Run every SKU through a calculator that timestamps its rates, verify against the current HTSUS and CBP CSMS, and confirm AD/CVD scope before you commit to a purchase order — because the difference between a modeled 58.9% and an actual 376.85% is the difference between a viable business and a customs penalty case.
