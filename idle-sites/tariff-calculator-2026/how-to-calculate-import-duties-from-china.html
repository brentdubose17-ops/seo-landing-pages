How to Calculate Import Duties from China in 2026: A Complete Guide

Importing goods from China remains one of the most cost-effective ways to source products, but navigating U.S. customs duties in 2026 requires precision. With updated tariff codes, fluctuating trade policies, and the expiration of certain de minimis exemptions, importers must calculate duties accurately to avoid unexpected costs and customs delays. This guide walks you through the exact steps to calculate import duties from China, using real-world examples and current data.

Understanding the Key Components of Import Duty Calculation

Import duties from China are not a single flat fee. They are composed of several layers, each calculated differently. The three primary components are: the MFN (Most Favored Nation) duty rate, the Section 301 tariff (if applicable), and the Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF). As of May 2026, the baseline MFN rate for most Chinese goods ranges from 0% to 25%, depending on the Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTS) code. However, Section 301 tariffs—originally imposed under the Trump administration and largely maintained through 2026—add an additional 7.5% to 25% on thousands of product categories from China.

To calculate your total duty, you first need the customs value of the goods. This is typically the transaction price paid for the goods, plus shipping, insurance, and any commissions, but not including U.S. inland freight. The U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) uses the FOB (Free on Board) or CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight) value, but most importers use the CIF value as it includes freight to the U.S. port.

Step-by-Step: How to Calculate Import Duties from China

Follow these five steps to determine your total landed cost. We'll use a concrete example: importing 500 units of electric scooters from a Chinese supplier, with a unit price of $80, total freight of $1,200, and insurance of $300.

Step 1: Determine the Correct HTS Code

Every product has a unique 10-digit HTS code. For electric scooters, the likely code is 8711.60.0050 (electric motorcycles and cycles). You can search the official HTS database or use our Tariff Calculator 2026 tool to find the code. Getting the code wrong can lead to overpayment or penalties. For example, misclassifying a scooter as a toy (9503.00.0060) could result in a lower duty rate but risk a customs audit and back-duties.

Step 2: Calculate the Customs Value (CIF)

Total CIF value = (Unit price × Quantity) + Freight + Insurance. For our example: ($80 × 500) + $1,200 + $300 = $40,000 + $1,500 = $41,500 CIF. This is the value CBP uses to calculate duties.

Step 3: Apply the MFN Duty Rate

Look up the base MFN rate for your HTS code. For electric scooters (8711.60.0050), the MFN rate in 2026 is 2.4% (unchanged from 2025). So, MFN duty = $41,500 × 0.024 = $996.

Step 4: Add the Section 301 Tariff (if applicable)

Check if your product is on the Section 301 list. Most electric vehicles and parts from China are subject to an additional 25% tariff (List 3 and List 4A). For our scooters, the Section 301 rate is 25%. So, additional duty = $41,500 × 0.25 = $10,375. Note that some products, like certain electronics or medical supplies, may be excluded or have lower rates. Always verify the current exclusions—many expired in 2025, but new product-specific exclusions were issued in early 2026.

Step 5: Calculate the Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF)

The MPF is 0.3464% of the customs value, with a minimum of $27.23 and a maximum of $528.33 (as of May 2026). For our $41,500 shipment: $41,500 × 0.003464 = $143.76. Since this is below the max, the MPF is $143.76.

Total estimated duties and fees: $996 (MFN) + $10,375 (Section 301) + $143.76 (MPF) = $11,514.76. That's over 27% of the CIF value—a significant cost to factor into your pricing.

Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

Many importers underestimate duties because they forget the Section 301 tariffs or miscalculate the customs value. Here are the most common mistakes:

  • Using the wrong valuation method: Always use CIF, not FOB. If you use FOB, your duty base will be too low, and CBP may adjust it upon entry, causing delays.
  • Ignoring anti-dumping duties: Certain products from China, like steel fasteners, aluminum foil, and wooden furniture, have additional anti-dumping duties ranging from 10% to over 200%. Check the ITA's anti-dumping database before shipping.
  • Assuming all goods qualify for de minimis: As of 2026, the de minimis threshold ($800) remains for personal shipments, but commercial imports are increasingly scrutinized. If you split a large order into multiple small packages to avoid duties, you risk seizure and fines.
  • Not factoring in state-level taxes: Some states require use tax on imported goods. For example, California applies a 7.25% use tax on the CIF value if the goods are consumed in-state.

How to Reduce Your Import Duties Legally

While you cannot avoid all duties, you can lower your tax burden through legal strategies:

  • First Sale Rule: If you buy from a Chinese intermediary, you may be able to use the price paid to the original manufacturer (often lower) as the customs value. This requires proper documentation and a letter of explanation.
  • Tariff engineering: Modify your product slightly to fall under a different HTS code with a lower duty rate. For example, assembling a product in a different way may reclassify it as a part rather than a finished good.
  • Free Trade Agreements (FTAs): While China is not part of any U.S. FTA, you can import components from China, assemble them in a country like Mexico or Vietnam that has an FTA with the U.S., and then export to the U.S. with lower duties.
  • Section 301 exclusion requests: The USTR occasionally opens windows for product-specific exclusions. As of May 2026, no broad exclusion process is active, but monitoring trade announcements is wise.
