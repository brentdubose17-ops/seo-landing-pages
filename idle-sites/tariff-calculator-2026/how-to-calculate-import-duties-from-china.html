How to Calculate Import Duties from China in 2026 (Complete Guide)

To calculate import duties from China, you multiply the customs value of your shipment by the sum of every applicable duty layer — MFN, Section 301, IEEPA, Section 232, and AD/CVD — then add non-duty fees like MPF and HMF. As of 2025, the average effective US tariff on Chinese goods was roughly 19.3% before the 20% IEEPA China tariff took effect, pushing the baseline to approximately 39% for the typical dutiable import. A single flat "China tariff" number is always wrong: real duty is a stacked calculation where the total ad valorem rate on a $10,000 lithium-ion battery shipment (3.4% MFN + 25% Section 301 + 20% IEEPA) reaches 48.4%, plus about $47 in MPF and HMF fees. And since May 2, 2025, the $800 de minimis exemption has been suspended for China and Hong Kong, meaning every parcel is dutiable.

This guide walks through the exact order of operations US Customs and Border Protection (CBP) uses, the numbers you need in 2026, and the legal levers that can cut your landed cost by 20–50%.

Why a Single Tariff Rate Calculator Is Always Wrong

Most free online calculators ask for a product value and a country, apply one rate, and return a number. That approach breaks the moment the product is Chinese. US duty on Chinese goods is layered — MFN plus Section 301 plus IEEPA plus possibly Section 232 plus possibly AD/CVD — and each layer has its own eligibility rules, HTS-level scope, and exclusion lists.

Worse, the layers are not additive in a simple way. AD/CVD is assessed case-by-case by product and by exporter. Section 232 attaches to the metal content, not the finished good. Section 301 exclusions expire and get reinstated. IEEPA rates have changed four times since February 2025 and remain subject to litigation.

The practical implication: any estimate that does not show you the individual layers — and the assumptions behind them — should not be trusted for pricing, landed-cost modeling, or margin decisions.

Step 1: Establish Your Customs Value (Transaction Value)

Everything downstream is a percentage of customs value, so this is the highest-leverage number in the calculation. Under 19 CFR 152.103, US customs value is generally transaction value — the price actually paid or payable for the goods when sold for export to the United States.

Crucially, US customs value is normally an FOB value. International freight and insurance are excluded from the duty base when they are separately itemized on the commercial invoice. This is a meaningful difference from the EU, which uses CIF. On a $10,000 shipment with $1,200 of ocean freight and insurance, the duty base in the US is $10,000 — not $11,200. At a 48% combined rate, that's roughly $576 of avoided duty purely from correct invoice structuring.

What must be added to transaction value

Certain items are dutiable additions even if billed separately:

  • Assists: tooling, molds, dies, design work, or materials you provide to the Chinese factory free of charge.
  • Royalties and license fees related to the imported goods, where payment is a condition of sale.
  • Selling commissions paid to your agent (buying commissions paid to your own agent are not dutiable).
  • Packing costs — both physical packing and the labor to pack.
  • Proceeds of any subsequent resale that accrue to the seller.

What you sell the goods for in the US does not determine duty. Your wholesale or retail price is irrelevant to CBP. Duty is based on what you paid (or should have paid) your supplier for the goods at the point of export.

Shipping terms change who pays — and sometimes the duty base

If you are the buyer, FOB or CIF with a US entity as importer of record is almost always superior. DDP arrangements strip you of the ability to file for duty drawback, claim Section 301 exclusions, or use first-sale valuation.

Step 2: Classify with a 10-Digit HTSUS Code

Classification drives everything. A US importer must use the 10-digit Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States (HTSUS) statistical suffix, not the 6-digit international HS code.

Two products that look identical to a consumer can land in different chapters with wildly different duty. A Bluetooth speaker with a rechargeable battery might be 8518.22.00 (MFN free) or 8517.62.00 (MFN free), while the standalone lithium-ion cell inside it is 8507.60.00 at 3.4% MFN plus Section 301. Getting the code right is worth real money; getting it wrong triggers penalties.

Penalties for misclassification

Under 19 USC 1592, CBP can assess penalties for negligent or fraudulent misclassification. Negligence penalties are typically the lesser of the domestic value of the merchandise or two times the lawful duty loss. Fraudulent violations carry the domestic value of the goods. CBP also has six years from the date of entry to conduct a post-summary audit, and it can go back further in fraud cases.

Practical protection: request a binding ruling from CBP (eCFR Part 177) before importing a high-value or ambiguous product. Rulings are free, published, and binding on CBP for identical merchandise.

Step 3: Layer the Duties in the Correct Order

This is where most calculators fail. The layers stack additively on the same customs value — each one is a separate percentage of the same base, not compounding.

The five layers

  1. MFN (Column 1 General): The baseline rate. China is not eligible for any US free trade agreement preference, so Column 1 General applies. Ranges from 0% (toys, furniture, many electronics) to 32% (some apparel).
  2. Section 301: List 1–3 at 25%, List 4A at 7.5%, covering more than $350 billion in Chinese imports. The 2024 statutory increases raised EVs to 100%, solar cells to 50%, and semiconductors to 50%. Exclusions are product-specific, time-limited, and must be claimed with the correct HTS and exclusion number at entry.
  3. IEEPA China tariffs: 20% as of March 2025 (10% imposed in February plus 10% in March). The May 2025 Geneva truce added a 10% reciprocal baseline. These are executive-branch actions under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act and have been challenged in the Court of International Trade — verify the current rate and legal status before every entry in 2026.
  4. Section 232: National security tariffs on steel and aluminum, raised to 50% in June 2025, plus derivative products. Applies to the metal content of the good, not the whole value.
  5. AD/CVD: Antidumping and countervailing duty orders, case-specific and often enormous. Chinese solar cells have faced rates above 250%; steel nails above 100%; aluminum foil between 30% and 100%. AD/CVD requires a cash deposit at entry at the preliminary rate.

Step 4: Add Non-Duty Fees — They Move Your Effective Rate 1–5%

Duty is not the only money CBP collects or that your broker charges. On smaller shipments, fees can materially change your effective cost.

On a $2,000 entry, MPF hits the $32.71 floor, HMF is absent on air freight, broker fees run ~$150, and ISF ~$40. That's $222 on a $2,000 shipment — an additional 11% of value before a single dollar of ad valorem duty. Fee drag is inversely proportional to shipment size, which is why consolidating freight is one of the fastest ways to improve margin.

Worked Example: Total Duty on a $10,000 Shipment from China

Assume: 1,000 lithium-ion battery packs, FOB Shenzhen, $10,000 customs value, ocean freight and insurance separately itemized, imported by a US LLC with a continuous bond, no first sale, no exclusions.

  1. Customs value: $10,000 (freight/insurance excluded)
  2. MFN: 3.4% × $10,000 = $340
  3. Section 301 List 3: 25% × $10,000 = $2,500
  4. IEEPA: 20% × $10,000 = $2,000
  5. Total duty: $4,840 (48.4%)
  6. MPF: 0.3464% × $10,000 = $34.64
  7. HMF: 0.125% × $10,000 = $12.50
  8. Brokerage + ISF: ~$185
  9. Total landed cost before freight: $15,072.14

Effective rate on goods value: 50.7% once all fees are included. If you had assumed a single 25% Section 301 rate, you would have understated the true cost by roughly 100% — enough to erase an entire margin on a consumer product.

The $800 De Minimis Loophole Is Closed for China

The Section 321 de minimis exemption — historically $800 per shipment per day per importer — was suspended for goods of Chinese and Hong Kong origin effective May 2, 2025. Every parcel from China and Hong Kong, regardless of value, is now dutiable and requires formal or informal entry with full data.

For international postal shipments, CBP imposed an alternative duty structure: 30% ad valorem or a flat per-item fee of $25 (raised to $50 per item from June 2025). The flat-fee option is deeply punitive for low-value items — a $5 phone case shipped by post can carry a $50 duty.

Legally Reducing Your Duty: Five Levers

Strategy Typical savings Requirements Risk / effort
First sale valuation 20–50% reduction in duty base Bona fide two-tier sale (manufacturer → middleman → US buyer), arm's-length pricing, full documentation Medium — CBP scrutiny; needs records
Foreign Trade Zone (FTZ) 10–30% via inverted tariff; duty deferral CBP FTZ application, activation, inventory controls Medium–high — setup cost, compliance
Duty drawback Up to 99% refund on exported or destroyed goods Export within 5 years, matching records, TFTEA claims Medium — formal filing
Section 301 exclusion Full removal of the 301 layer (25% or 7.5%) Product must match a live exclusion, correct HTS and exclusion number at entry Low — but availability is intermittent
Section 232 drawback / product exclusion Varies; full exclusion on approved products BIS exclusion granted or drawback on exported downstream goods High — competitive process

Duty drawback is the most underused tool in the market. Under the Trade Facilitation and Trade Enforcement Act (TFTEA), importers can recover up to 99% of duties paid on goods later exported or destroyed, and the lookback window is five years. If you import from China and export finished goods anywhere in the world, you likely have an unclaimed refund sitting on your books.

Who Is Responsible for Paying Import Duties?

The importer of record (IOR) is legally liable for all duty, taxes, and fees — not the Chinese supplier, not the freight forwarder, and not the customs broker. The IOR is typically the US entity that owns the goods at the time of entry and is on the entry summary (CBP Form 7501).

DDP arrangements create a common trap. When a Chinese supplier sells DDP and uses its own US entity as IOR, that supplier controls the entry — and the buyer loses access to drawback, exclusions, and first sale. Worse, if the supplier under-declares value, CBP pursues the IOR on record. Structuring your company as the IOR is usually worth the added responsibility.

Common Mistakes That Inflate Effective Duty

  • Bundling freight into the invoice price. This silently increases your duty base on every entry.
  • Using the 6-digit HS code instead of the 10-digit HTSUS. Guarantees classification errors and missed exclusions.
  • Ignoring the MPF cap. Large shipments stop accruing MPF at $634.62 — a reason to consolidate rather than split entries.
  • Assuming de minimis still applies to China. It has been suspended since May 2, 2025.
  • Missing Section 301 exclusion numbers at the time of entry. Exclusions cannot always be claimed retroactively.
  • Treating "China" as one origin. Substantial transformation in a third country can shift origin, but CBP scrutinizes transshipment aggressively — and penalties for false origin claims are severe.
  • Not modeling the 145% scenario. If your business survives only at 20% IEEPA, you have concentration risk.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Do I pay import duties on shipping and insurance from China?

A: Generally no. US customs value is transaction value (FOB), and international freight and insurance are excluded when separately itemized on the commercial invoice. If the supplier bundles freight into a single all-in price, CBP may treat the full amount as the dutiable value. Always require freight and insurance to be shown as distinct line items.

Q: How do I calculate the total duty on a $10,000 shipment from China?

A: Add every applicable ad valorem layer and multiply by the customs value. For a $10,000 lithium-ion battery shipment: 3.4% MFN + 25% Section 301 + 20% IEEPA = 48.4%, or $4,840 in duty. Then add MPF at 0.3464% ($34.64) and HMF at 0.125% ($12.50) for ocean freight. Total government charges: $4,887.14, plus roughly $185 in brokerage and ISF.

Q: Is the $800 de minimis exemption still available for China?

A: No. The Section 321 de minimis exemption was suspended for Chinese and Hong Kong origin goods effective May 2, 2025. Every parcel is now dutiable, and postal shipments face either 30% ad valorem or a flat $25 per item (raised to $50 per item from June 2025).

Q: Are tariffs based on what I pay the supplier or what I sell the product for?

A: What you pay the supplier. US customs value is transaction value — the price actually paid or payable for the goods sold for export to the United States. Your US wholesale or retail price is irrelevant to CBP, except that assists, royalties, and selling commissions must be added to the dutiable base.

Q: Do Section 301, IEEPA, and MFN tariffs stack?

A: Yes. They stack additively on the same customs value. A 3.4% MFN product subject to 25% Section 301 and 20% IEEPA faces a 48.4% combined rate. Section 232 and AD/CVD, where applicable, stack on top of that. This is why a flat-rate calculator understates duty on Chinese goods by 30–100% or more.

Q: Can I legally reduce import duties from China?

A: Yes, through five main levers: first sale valuation (20–50% reduction in duty base), Foreign Trade Zones (10–30% savings via inverted tariff), duty drawback (up to 99% refund on exported goods within a 5-year window), Section 301 exclusions, and Section 232 product exclusions. Each requires documentation and carries audit risk if mismanaged.

Q: What HTS code do I use, and what happens if I misclassify?

A: You must use the 10-digit HTSUS statistical suffix, not the 6-digit international HS code. Misclassification penalties under 19 USC 1592 range from the value of the merchandise up to two times the lawful duty loss for negligence, and up to the full domestic value for fraud. CBP can audit up to six years back. Request a binding ruling under 19 CFR Part 177 for ambiguous products.

The Bottom Line for 2026

Calculating import duties from China is a five-layer exercise, not a single-rate lookup. Get your 10-digit HTSUS code right, establish customs value correctly (FOB, freight excluded), stack MFN + Section 301 + IEEPA + Section 232 + AD/CVD, add MPF and HMF, and model at least three IEEPA scenarios. Assume no de minimis relief for China and Hong Kong.

The importers who win in 2026 are the ones who treat duty as an engineering problem — using first sale, FTZs, and drawback to legally compress a 48% effective rate into the low 30s. The ones who lose are still using a free calculator that returns a single number.
