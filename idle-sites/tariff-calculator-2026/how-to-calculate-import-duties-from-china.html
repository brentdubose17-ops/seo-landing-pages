How to Calculate Import Duties from China: The 2026 Importer's Playbook

To calculate import duties from China in 2026, you multiply your FOB dutiable value by the sum of every applicable ad valorem rate — Column 1 General, Section 301, Section 232, IEEPA, and AD/CVD — then add MPF at 0.3464% and HMF at 0.125% for ocean freight. A $10,000 FOB shipment of Chinese goods subject to a 3.4% General rate, 25% Section 301, and 20% IEEPA owes $4,840 in duty plus $34.64 MPF and $12.50 HMF — $4,887.14 all-in, an effective rate of 48.87%. The $800 Section 321 de minimis exemption was eliminated for China and Hong Kong on May 2, 2025, which means even a $10 shipment now requires a formal or informal entry. The bottom line: duty calculation is no longer "HTS code × rate." It is a six-layer stack on a correctly constructed value base, and getting the order wrong is the single most common — and most expensive — mistake importers make.

The Six-Layer Duty Stack: Order Matters

Most free tariff calculators stop at "look up your HTS code, multiply by the rate." That answer is wrong for China almost every time. Chinese-origin goods are typically subject to multiple overlapping duty authorities, and each one is assessed as an ad valorem percentage of the same dutiable value base.

Here is the stack, in the order CBP applies it:

Layer Authority Typical Rate (China) Dutiable Base Example Product
1. Column 1 General (MFN) HTSUS General Notes 0% – 20%+ FOB transaction value Steel cookware: 3.4%
2. Section 301 Trade Act of 1974 §301 7.5% or 25%, up to 100% FOB transaction value EVs: 100%
3. Section 232 Trade Expansion Act §232 25% – 50% FOB transaction value Steel/aluminum: 50%
4. IEEPA Int'l Emergency Economic Powers Act ~20% combined (verify CSMS) FOB transaction value All Chinese-origin goods
5. AD/CVD 19 U.S.C. §1671 / §1673 Often 100% – 500%+ FOB transaction value (case-specific) Aluminum extrusions: 86.01% AD
6. Fees 19 CFR / USER fee statute MPF 0.3464%; HMF 0.125% FOB value; HMF on ocean only Any commercial entry

Layer 1: The Column 1 General Rate

Every product has a 10-digit HTSUS statistical suffix — there are roughly 17,000 of them in the current schedule. The first eight digits determine your duty rate; the final two are statistical reporting only. The Column 1 General rate is the baseline, and for many consumer goods it is surprisingly low (0% to 5%).

China is not a Column 2 country. It gets Column 1 General rates — which is exactly why the stacked rates below matter so much. The punitive layers are added on top of MFN, not in place of it.

Layer 2: Section 301 — $350 Billion and Counting

Section 301 remains the largest single driver of China tariff cost. Coverage breaks down as follows:

  • List 1: $34 billion at 25%
  • List 2: $16 billion at 25%
  • List 3: $200 billion at 25%
  • List 4A: $120 billion at 7.5%

That is over $350 billion in Chinese imports subject to additional duty. The May 2024 statutory review raised rates on strategic sectors, and those increases took effect across 2024–2026:

Product Category Section 301 Rate
Electric vehicles 100%
Solar cells and modules 50%
Semiconductors 50%
Steel and aluminum products 25%
EV lithium-ion batteries 25%
Critical minerals 25%
Syringes and needles 100%
Medical gloves (surgical/nitrile) 50%

Critically, Section 301 is assessed on the FOB transaction value — not on CIF. This is the single most important structural fact in duty calculation for Chinese goods.

Layer 3: Section 232 — Steel, Aluminum, and Now Derivatives

Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminum were raised to 50% as of June 2025, up from the original 25%. This matters because 232 stacks with Section 301 and IEEPA. A steel bracket from China can carry 25% (Column 1 General) + 25% (301) + 50% (232) + 20% (IEEPA) = 120% before fees. Derivative products — downstream articles made of steel or aluminum — are also captured, based on the declared metal content value.

Layer 4: IEEPA China Tariffs — Verify Before You Ship

IEEPA tariffs are imposed under emergency economic powers and have swung wildly in recent years. Following the late-2025 truce posture, the combined IEEPA China layer stood at roughly 20% total (a 10% fentanyl-related tranche plus a 10% reciprocal tranche), down from a peak of 145% (20% + 125%) earlier in 2025.

Because IEEPA rates change by executive action and can move within days, never quote a rate from a blog post — including this one. Pull the current rate from a CSMS message at CBP.gov before every purchase order. Freeze the rate in your landed-cost model on the date of entry, not the date of the quote.

Layer 5: AD/CVD — The Silent Killer

Antidumping and countervailing duty orders are where importers get destroyed. These are not ad valorem rates published in the HTSUS; they are case-specific and depend on your manufacturer's assigned rate.

Examples in force on Chinese goods include:

  • Aluminum extrusions: 86.01% AD + 33.28% CVD
  • Certain solar cells: combined rates exceeding 250%
  • Numerous Chinese manufacturers carry "all-others" or adverse-facts-available rates above 100%

If your product falls under an AD/CVD order and you are not entered into CBP's ACE AD/CVD module correctly, you will receive a bill — and a penalty case — years later. Always run your HTS code against the ITC's AD/CVD order database and the Commerce Department's scope rulings before your first shipment.

Layer 6: Fees — MPF and HMF

Two government user fees ride on top of every commercial entry:

  • Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF): 0.3464% of dutiable value. For FY2025 the floor is $32.71 and the ceiling is $634.62.
  • Harbor Maintenance Fee (HMF): 0.125% of value, ocean freight only. Air and truck shipments are exempt.

Note the direction of the stack: MPF is calculated on the dutiable value, not on the duty amount. So on a $10,000 entry, MPF is $34.64 regardless of whether your total duty is $400 or $4,800.

Step 1: Build the Correct Dutiable Value (This Is FOB)

The United States uses a FOB (Free On Board) transaction value system under 19 CFR Part 152, not CIF. This means international freight and insurance are excluded from the duty base — if they are separately identified on the commercial invoice.

That "if separately identified" clause has real money attached. Keep reading.

What Gets Added to FOB Value

Dutiable value is not just the unit price times quantity. You must add:

  • Assists: tooling, molds, dies, engineering, design work, and materials you provide to the Chinese factory free of charge
  • Royalties and license fees related to the imported goods
  • Packing costs — both containers and internal packing
  • Selling commissions paid to your agent
  • Proceeds of any subsequent resale accruing to the seller

Buying commissions (paid to your own sourcing agent) are not included. The distinction between buying and selling commissions is one of the most commonly missed items in a CBP audit.

Incoterms and the Dutiable Value Base

Incoterm What's Included in Invoice Price Dutiable Additions / Deductions
FOB Goods + inland freight to port + export clearance Ideal base. No deduction needed.
CIF FOB + international freight + insurance Deduct freight and insurance if separately stated on invoice
CFR/CPT FOB + international freight Deduct the international freight amount
DDP Everything, including duty Must back out freight, insurance, duty, and domestic delivery to derive FOB
DAP Goods + freight to named place Deduct international freight to derive FOB

Actionable tip: Instruct every Chinese supplier to state "FOB [Port] USD X / Ocean Freight USD Y / Insurance USD Z" as three separate line items on the commercial invoice. If freight is bundled into a single CIF number, CBP presumptively treats the whole amount as dutiable value. At a 48.4% stacked rate, failing to separate $800 of freight costs you roughly $387 in unnecessary duty — every single shipment.

Step 2: Worked Example — $10,000 FOB from China

Here is the full arithmetic for a shipment of Chinese-made consumer goods with no AD/CVD exposure:

Component Rate / Amount Calculation Duty
FOB dutiable value — — $10,000.00
Column 1 General 3.4% $10,000 × 0.034 $340.00
Section 301 25% $10,000 × 0.25 $2,500.00
Section 232 N/A — $0.00
IEEPA 20% $10,000 × 0.20 $2,000.00
AD/CVD N/A — $0.00
Subtotal duty 48.4% — $4,840.00
MPF 0.3464% $10,000 × 0.003464 $34.64
HMF (ocean) 0.125% $10,000 × 0.00125 $12.50
Total government cost — — $4,887.14

Effective government cost: 48.87% of FOB value. On top of that you will pay your customs broker ($75–$150 per entry), a continuous customs bond (roughly 0.35%–0.5% of total duty, minimum ~$400–$600 annually), and the freight itself.

CIF vs. FOB: The $484 Question

Now run the same shipment as a bundled CIF transaction at $11,000 with $800 freight and $200 insurance embedded in the price:

Scenario Dutiable Value Rate Duty
CIF — freight/insurance not broken out $11,000 48.4% $5,324.00
FOB — freight/insurance separately invoiced $10,000 48.4% $4,840.00
Savings from proper invoicing — — $484.00

That is a pure margin difference created by a single sentence in your purchase order. Multiply it across 50 shipments a year and it is $24,200.

Step 3: De Minimis Is Gone for China

The $800 Section 321 de minimis exemption was eliminated for China and Hong Kong on May 2, 2025. There is no longer a low-value free pass for Chinese-origin goods.

For non-postal commercial shipments, every China entry — regardless of value — now requires a formal or informal entry, with full duty and MPF. For postal shipments sent via the international mail system, CBP applies a simplified flat charge:

  • Initially: 30% of value or $25 per item, whichever is greater
  • From June 1, 2025: $50 per item

Why most online calculators are wrong: a large number of free tariff tools still apply the $800 de minimis threshold as a default assumption. If you plug in a $500 DTC order and the calculator says "no duty owed," it has not been updated. Ignore it. On a $500 China shipment you now owe duty at the full stacked rate, plus a minimum $32.71 MPF.

Step 4: Build Your Landed Cost Worksheet

Duty is not your landed cost. Here is the audit-ready formula:

Landed Cost = FOB + International Freight + Insurance + Duty + MPF + HMF + Broker Fee + Bond Allocation + Inland Delivery + Ancillary Fees

An example on the $10,000 FOB shipment above:

  • FOB goods: $10,000
  • Ocean freight: $800
  • Marine insurance: $200
  • Duty: $4,840
  • MPF: $34.64
  • HMF: $12.50
  • Customs broker: $125
  • Bond allocation: $25
  • Inland drayage/delivery: $450
  • Total landed: $16,487.14
  • Landed cost as % of FOB: 164.9%

That is the number you need for pricing decisions. If your product cannot survive a 165% landing factor, no sourcing efficiency will save it — you need a tariff-mitigation strategy or a different country of origin.

Legal Ways to Reduce the Duty

1. First Sale Valuation

If you buy through a middleman, you may be able to declare the price paid by that middleman to the manufacturer rather than the price you paid the middleman. Typical value reduction: 20% to 50%. First sale requires bona fide sales, arm's-length negotiation, and documentation CBP will accept. It is one of the highest-ROI valuation strategies available, because it reduces the base that every stacked layer multiplies against.

2. Duty Drawback

If you import Chinese goods and subsequently export them — or export products made from them — you can recover up to 99% of duties paid, including Section 301 and IEEPA amounts. Drawback claims must be filed within 5 years of import. Many importers leave this entirely on the table.

3. Section 301 Exclusions

Exclusions are granted by product and by HTS subheading with defined effective windows. Existing exclusions apply retroactively to the original effective date if claimed correctly. Check the USTR exclusion portal and CBP CSMS before every entry — a valid exclusion can wipe out 25% of your cost instantly.

4. Foreign Trade Zones (FTZ)

Admitting goods into an FTZ defers duty until they leave the zone. If you re-export, no duty is ever owed. If you manufacture in-zone, you can elect the finished-product rate — which is often lower than the component rates.

5. Tariff Engineering

Legally changing the form or composition of a product so it classifies under a lower-duty HTS provision. This is legitimate when the modification is real and commercially motivated. It becomes fraud when the product is unchanged and only the classification is fabricated.

Penalties: The Cost of Getting It Wrong

Under 19 U.S.C. §1592, CBP penalty exposure scales with culpability:

  • Negligence: up to 2× the unpaid duties
  • Gross negligence: up to 4× the unpaid duties
  • Fraud: up to the domestic value of the merchandise

On a $10,000 shipment with $4,840 in duty, a gross negligence finding exposes you to $19,360 — plus the underlying duty, plus interest, plus your legal fees. Given that most misclassifications are avoidable with a $150 broker review, the math for doing it properly is overwhelming.

The Tariff Calculator 2026 Workflow

Here is the sequence to run on every new China product, in order:

  1. Classify: Get the 10-digit HTSUS code. Confirm with a CBP CROSS ruling if ambiguous.
  2. Establish origin: Confirm the goods are wholly obtained or substantially transformed in China. Document it.
  3. Check AD/CVD: Search the ITC order database and Commerce scope rulings. If covered, stop and get legal review.
  4. Check 232: Is it steel, aluminum, or a listed derivative?
  5. Check 301: Which list? Which rate? Any active exclusion?
  6. Apply IEEPA: Pull the current CSMS rate. Do not assume.
  7. Build FOB value: Add assists, royalties, packing, selling commissions. Deduct separately-stated freight and insurance.
  8. Sum the ad valorem layers and multiply by FOB value.
  9. Add MPF (0.3464%, floor $32.71, cap $634.62) and HMF (0.125%, ocean only).
  10. Add broker, bond, and inland costs to get true landed cost.

Then — critically — re-run steps 6 and 5 before every single entry. IEEPA and 301 rates have changed multiple times within single quarters.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Do I pay import duty on shipping and insurance from China?

A: No. The United States assesses duty on FOB transaction value, not CIF. International freight and insurance are excluded from the dutiable base — but only if they are separately identified on the commercial invoice or other documentation. If freight is bundled into a single delivered price, CBP treats the entire amount as dutiable value. On a shipment with a 48.4% stacked rate, failing to separate $1,000 of freight costs you $484 in unnecessary duty.

Q: What is the total tariff rate on Chinese goods in 2026?

A: There is no single number. The effective rate is the sum of Column 1 General + Section 301 + Section 232 + IEEPA + any AD/CVD order rate, all applied to the same FOB value. For a typical consumer good with no 232 or AD/CVD exposure, that lands around 48% (3.4% + 25% + 20%). For steel articles it exceeds 120%. For Chinese solar cells with AD/CVD it can exceed 300%. Always verify current IEEPA and 301 rates via CBP CSMS before entry.

Q: Is the $800 de minimis exemption still available for China?

A: No. The Section 321 de minimis exemption was eliminated for China and Hong Kong on May 2, 2025. Every commercial shipment from China now requires a formal or informal entry and payment of applicable duty and MPF — regardless of value. Postal shipments are subject to a simplified charge of 30% of value or $25 per item initially, rising to $50 per item from June 1, 2025. Any calculator still showing "no duty under $800" for Chinese goods is out of date.

Q: How do I calculate duty on a $10,000 FOB shipment from China?

A: Multiply the FOB value by each applicable ad valorem rate and sum them, then add fees. Using a 3.4% General rate, 25% Section 301, and 20% IEEPA: $10,000 × 48.4% = $4,840 duty. Add MPF of $34.64 (0.3464%) and HMF of $12.50 (0.125%, ocean only). Total government cost: $4,887.14, or 48.87% of FOB. Then add broker fees, bond allocation, and inland delivery to reach true landed cost — roughly $16,487 in this example.

Q: What is the difference between Section 301, IEEPA, and AD/CVD?

A: Section 301 is a retaliatory tariff authorized by the Trade Act of 1974 targeting unfair trade practices; it applies to over $350 billion in Chinese imports at 7.5%–100%. IEEPA tariffs rest on emergency economic powers granted to the President and currently add roughly 20% to all Chinese-origin goods, though the rate has swung from 0% to 145% and must be verified via CSMS. AD/CVD are not tariffs at all — they are case-specific duties imposed after formal Commerce Department investigations into dumping and subsidization, and they routinely exceed 100%.

Q: Can I avoid China tariffs by routing through Vietnam or Mexico?

A: Only if the goods undergo genuine substantial transformation in that country — meaning they become a new and different article with a distinct name, character, or use. Simple assembly, repackaging, or minor finishing does not change country of origin. Transshipping Chinese goods through a third country without transformation is origin fraud, and CBP enforces it aggressively through penalties under 19 U.S.C. §1592, which allow up to the domestic value of the merchandise for fraudulent claims.

Q: What fees are added beyond the duty rate?

A: Beyond the stacked duty, every commercial entry pays the Merchandise Processing Fee at 0.3464% of dutiable value (FY2025 minimum $32.71, maximum $634.62) and, for ocean freight, the Harbor Maintenance Fee at 0.125%. You will also pay your customs broker ($75–$150 per entry), a continuous customs bond (roughly 0.35%–0.5% of duty, ~$400–$600 per year minimum), and any freight forwarder and drayage charges.

Q: How do I find my HTS code?

A: Start with the USITC Harmonized Tariff Schedule search at hts.usitc.gov — there are roughly 17,000 10-digit statistical suffixes. The first eight digits set your duty rate. For anything ambiguous or high-value, search the CBP CROSS database for a binding ruling issued on identical merchandise, or file your own ruling request. A $150 broker review up front is far cheaper than a §1592 penalty later.

The Bottom Line

Calculating import duties from China in 2026 requires you to stop thinking about a single rate and start thinking about a structured stack applied to a carefully constructed value base. The formula is: (FOB + assists + royalties + packing + selling commissions) × (Column 1 General + Section 301 + Section 232 + IEEPA + AD/CVD) + MPF + HMF + broker + bond.

Three operational habits separate importers who survive this environment from those who get hit with six-figure penalty notices:

  1. Separate freight and insurance on every invoice. It is free money worth $400+ per shipment at current rates.
  2. Pull CSMS before every entry. IEEPA and 301 rates are moving targets. Static rate tables are liabilities.
  3. Screen for AD/CVD and 232 before you place the PO, not after. These layers can multiply your cost by three and are almost impossible to unwind at the port.
