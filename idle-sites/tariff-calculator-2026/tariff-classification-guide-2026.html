HS Code vs. HTS Code vs. TARIC Code: Which One Do You Actually Need?

The confusion between HS, HTS, and TARIC codes is the most common starting point for classification errors. These are not interchangeable terms, and using the wrong level of specificity invalidates your entry.

The Harmonized System (HS): The Global Foundation

The HS Convention, administered by the World Customs Organization, establishes a standardized nomenclature of approximately 5,300 six-digit codes covering all traded goods. These codes are legally binding in all 200+ contracting parties. The 6-digit level is where global comparability ends — and where national divergence begins.

US HTS: The 10-Digit National Expression

The United States extends every 6-digit HS code into 8-digit and 10-digit statistical suffixes, creating more than 100,000 distinct national codes. The 8-digit level determines the duty rate; the 10-digit level adds statistical tracking. When you file a US entry, you must report the full 10-digit HTS code — not the 6-digit HS base.

EU TARIC and UK Global Tariff: Different Suffixes, Different Rates

The European Union's TARIC adds 8-digit and 10-digit codes plus additional TARIC measures (anti-dumping, suspensions, surveillance). The UK operates its own UK Global Tariff with distinct 8-digit and 10-digit suffixes. Critically, the same product can carry the same 6-digit HS base but diverge completely at the national level — with different duty rates, quota treatments, and licensing requirements.

Practical Guidance

For US importers, you need the 10-digit HTS code at time of entry. For EU importers, the 10-digit TARIC code plus any additional TARIC-related measure codes. For UK importers, the 10-digit UK Global Tariff code. Using a 6-digit HS code on a customs declaration is almost never sufficient — and frequently results in rejection or penalty exposure.

The GRI Framework: A Deterministic Path to the Correct Code

Classification is not intuition — it is a structured legal analysis governed by the six General Rules of Interpretation (GRIs). Applied sequentially, they produce a defensible, repeatable answer. Here is the step-by-step logic chain every importer should follow.

GRI 1: Start with the Text

Classification is determined first by the terms of the headings and any relative Section or Chapter notes. This is the foundation — not a suggestion. If the product's description matches a heading unambiguously, classification ends there. For example, a ceramic coffee mug falls squarely within heading 6912 based on its terms and the Chapter 69 legal notes.

GRI 2: Incomplete, Unfinished, and Mixed Goods

GRI 2(a) extends coverage to incomplete or unfinished articles that have the essential character of the complete article. GRI 2(b) addresses mixtures and combinations of materials — ruling that goods classifiable under multiple headings by composition follow the rules of GRI 3.

GRI 3: When Multiple Headings Apply

This is where most classification disputes arise. GRI 3 applies when a product is prima facie classifiable under two or more headings. It proceeds in hierarchical order:

  • GRI 3(a): The most specific description prevails over a more general one. However, if two headings each refer to only part of the materials or components, they are considered equally specific.
  • GRI 3(b): Mixtures, composite goods, and goods put up in sets are classified by the component that gives them their essential character. This is the "principal function" test applied to multi-function technology products.
  • GRI 3(c): When GRIs 3(a) and 3(b) fail, classification falls to the heading that appears last in numerical order.

GRI 4: The Fallback

If no heading covers the goods, classify them under the heading for goods to which they are most akin. GRI 4 requires substantive justification and is rarely used in practice.

GRI 5: Containers and Packing

Camera cases, musical instrument cases, and similar containers specially shaped to hold specific articles are classified with those articles when they are of a kind normally sold therewith. Standard packing materials follow the goods they contain.

GRI 6: The National Level

GRI 6 extends the preceding rules to classification at the subheading level within each heading. Once the 4-digit heading is fixed, the same logic determines the 6-digit subheading and, nationally, the 8-digit and 10-digit splits. Many importers successfully determine the heading but fail at the subheading — where duty rate differences of several percentage points frequently live.

Multi-Function Technology Products: Applying the Principal Function Test

Artificial intelligence hardware, IoT devices, EV components, and modular consumer electronics have created a classification crisis. A smartwatch with heart-rate monitoring touches Chapter 85 (electronics), Chapter 90 (medical/measuring), and Chapter 91 (clocks). A robot vacuum combines Chapter 85 (electric motors), Chapter 84 (mechanical appliances), and Chapter 90 (optical sensors).

The determinative test under GRI 3(b) is essential character — which component or function defines the product's purpose. CBP and the WCO have issued numerous rulings refining this analysis, and the decisions are not always intuitive.

Case Study: The Smart Scale Divergence

A "smart scale" that measures body weight and transmits data to a smartphone app could classify under Chapter 84 (weighing machinery), Chapter 85 (with wireless transmission), or Chapter 90 (medical monitoring). The classification chosen determines whether the duty rate is 3.9%, 4.6%, or 0%. One mid-sized importer in this exact situation recovered $412,000 in annual duty overpayments after a reclassification audit moved three product lines from Chapter 85 to Chapter 90 based on the principal medical function — a determination supported by prior NY ruling letters.

The lesson is that "essential character" requires analyzing marketing claims, primary intended use, and the engineering reality of what makes the device function. A scale designed for consumer weight tracking with a Bluetooth convenience feature is not the same as a Class II medical device scale intended for clinical use. Documentation matters — and designers who consult customs before finalizing specifications can save their companies substantial duty exposure.

Design-Time Classification Consultation

Engineers rarely consider customs implications during product development, yet a 2-gram change in material composition or the addition of a Bluetooth module can shift a product from a duty-free heading to a 6.5% rate. Companies that embed classification review into their product development gates consistently achieve lower effective duty rates because they can still modify specifications at minimal cost. Legal Note Traps: The #1 Cause of Misclassification

Section and Chapter legal notes override heading text and are frequently the source of classification surprises. Approximately 80% of binding ruling requests returned as "insufficient information" fail because importers did not address applicable legal notes.

Chapter 84 vs. Chapter 85: Mechanical vs. Electrical

Note 3 to Section XVI provides the framework: machines and apparatus answering to a purpose described in both Chapters 84 and 85 are classified according to their principal function. But the trap is that many products appear to have multiple principal functions. Note 3 instructs that when no single principal function can be determined, and GRI 3(a) does not resolve it, classification defaults to the last numerical heading — which favors Chapter 85.

Chapter 90: The Optics and Medical Overlap

Chapter 90 covers optical, photographic, measuring, checking, precision, and medical instruments. Note 1(f) excludes from Chapter 90 any articles that are more specifically covered elsewhere. This exclusionary note causes immense confusion — a device with both optical measurement and electronic data processing functions may fall into Chapter 84 or 85 depending on which function is deemed principal.

Practical Audit Tip

Before finalizing any classification, read all applicable legal notes in the relevant Section and Chapter in full. Flag any note containing "excludes," "includes," or "does not cover" — these are the language that overrides your intuitive judgment. Most misclassifications trace directly to ignored legal notes, not to incorrect application of heading text.

Jurisdiction Comparison: Same Product, Different Codes Across Markets

Global supply chains mean the same product often clears customs in multiple jurisdictions. The 6-digit HS base is harmonized, but national suffixes, interpretations, and duty rates diverge substantially. The table below illustrates how one product category — ceramic tableware — breaks down across the three largest Western markets.

Jurisdiction Code Structure Duty Rate Key Notes
Global HS (6-digit) 6912.00 N/A (base) Ceramic tableware, kitchenware, other household articles
US HTS (10-digit) 6912.00.4810 9.8% (general) / 0% (duty-free under certain FTAs) US splits by porcelain vs. non-porcelain and by end-use
EU TARIC (10-digit) 6912 00 23 7.0% EU additional codes for fine-grained pottery and earthenware
UK Global Tariff (10-digit) 6912 00 29 7.0% UK diverges from EU for certain ceramic categories

This example demonstrates why importers operating in multiple markets need jurisdiction-specific classification. The same physical product carries a 9.8% US rate and a 7.0% EU rate — and the US/UK subheading splits require analysis of characteristics (porcelain vs. earthenware) that are irrelevant at the 6-digit level. Relying on a single global code is a compliance failure waiting to happen.

AD/CVD orders and Section 301 tariffs add another overlay. The same HTS code can carry dramatically different effective duty rates depending on country of origin. A Chinese-origin product under one HTS code may face a 25% Section 301 tariff in addition to the base duty, while the identical product from Vietnam enters at the base rate. Classification determines the base rate, but origin determines the outer layers — and both must be analyzed together for accurate total landed cost.

The Cost of Misclassification: Penalties, Interest, and Lost Duty Savings

Misclassification penalties under 19 U.S.C. 1592 follow a severity ladder based on culpability:

  • Negligence: Up to $10,000 per violation, plus unpaid duties and interest
  • Gross negligence: Up to the value of the merchandise
  • Fraud: Up to $25,000–$100,000+ per violation, with potential criminal referral

These figures, combined with the global estimate of $70+ billion in annual customs error costs, explain why proactive classification audits consistently produce strong ROI. The typical savings from reclassification done correctly range from 0.5% to 2% of duty per line — which, for a mid-sized importer with $50 million in annual imports, translates to $250,000–$1,000,000 in annual savings.

The Voluntary Prior Disclosure Advantage

CBP's prior disclosure mechanism allows importers to voluntarily disclose errors before detection, substantially mitigating penalties — often to zero monetary penalty plus interest on unpaid duties. The 2026 window is uniquely favorable because the upcoming 2027 code changes provide a natural audit trigger. Importers who review their classifications now, identify errors, and file prior disclosures before the new codes take effect minimize their exposure and position themselves cleanly for the transition.

Binding Rulings: When to Use Them and What They Cost You

CBP issues binding ruling letters (NY and HQ rulings) that provide prospective certainty for specific products. The average turnaround for complex goods was approximately 18 months in 2025, down from 24 months in 2023. That timeline is unacceptable for many product launches — but the alternative, self-classification without a ruling, carries ongoing risk.

Importers can rely on existing rulings issued to other companies if the descriptions match their products exactly. However, subtle differences in materials, components, or function can invalidate reliance. The practical approach is to search CBP's ruling database for products matching yours, compare specifications meticulously, and only then decide whether to file a new ruling request.

Approximately 80% of ruling requests are initially rejected as "insufficient information." The most common deficiency is failure to provide detailed product descriptions including material composition, function, and intended use. Submitting complete specifications upfront — ideally with technical drawings or photographs — dramatically shortens the process.

AD/CVD and Section 301 Overlay: The 2026 Reality

Classification alone does not determine your effective duty rate. Country of origin triggers additional layers of protectionist measures that can dwarf the base duty. The interaction between classification and origin is the most misunderstood area of trade compliance.

Scenario HTS Code Base Duty Additional Tariffs Effective Rate
Chinese-origin steel fastener 7318.15.20 8.5% Section 301 (25%) + AD/CVD (up to 200%+) Varies by AD/CVD order
Vietnamese-origin steel fastener 7318.15.20 8.5% None (absent separate AD/CVD finding) 8.5%
Mexican-origin steel fastener 7318.15.20 8.5% USMCA preferential rate 0% (with certificate of origin)

The same HTS code applied to the identical product yields duty-free treatment under USMCA for Mexican origin, an 8.5% base rate for Vietnamese origin, and 33.5%+ for Chinese origin once Section 301 applies. Classification errors compound these origin-based complications because AD/CVD orders are code-specific — a misclassified product can evade a duty order it should trigger or, worse, trigger one it should not.

Rules of origin add yet another layer. A product that meets the tariff shift rule under USMCA may fail the RVC (regional value content) test, making it ineligible for preferential treatment despite correct classification. The interplay between classification and origin rules requires separate, deliberate analysis — most guides ignore this entirely, but it is central to accurate duty calculation in 2026.

Misclassification Risk Audit Checklist for 2026

Run this checklist against your current product classifications to identify likely errors before CBP or a prior disclosure deadline finds them first.

  1. Codes ending in "00": If your US HTS code ends in "00" at the 8-digit level, verify no further statistical split exists at the 10-digit level. Many importers stop at 8 digits when 10 are required.
  2. Multi-function goods: Any product with both logic/processing functions and power/motive functions is at risk. Apply GRI 3(b) rigorously and document the principal function analysis.
  3. Cosmetic vs. medical claims: Products with therapeutic claims may trigger Chapter 90 classification and FDA jurisdiction. Marketing language matters — "moisturizing" vs. "treating eczema" changes both code and agency oversight.
  4. Material composition changes: A 2% change in polymer composition or metal alloy can shift classifications across headings. Audit specifications against your last classification annually.
  5. Component vs. finished good: Parts of machines classify under the same heading as the complete machine (GRI 2(a)) but only if they have the essential character. Verify your parts actually qualify.
  6. Software and digital elements: Physical media with embedded software classifies by the carrier medium, not the digital content. AI-enabled devices classify by physical structure and principal function, not by the AI algorithms running on them.
  7. Sets and kits: Retail kits containing multiple components must satisfy the GRI 3(b) set definition — components put up together for a specific activity. Missing the "specific activity" element defeats set classification.
  8. Preferential origin eligibility: Your classification may make you eligible for a free trade agreement rate you are not currently claiming. Verify FTA eligibility for each top-shipping HTS line.
  9. Section 301 scope: For Chinese-origin goods, verify your classification against the current Section 301 exclusion lists and the product descriptions in each HTS subheading covered by the tariffs.
  10. Binding ruling consistency: If you rely on a CBP ruling, confirm the ruling has not been modified or revoked through the annual ruling review process.

How to Conduct a Self-Audit (and When to Hire a Customs Attorney)

A self-audit begins by extracting your last 12 months of entry data — your customs broker can provide a summary of all HTS codes used. Rank them by duty paid and by total shipment value. Triaging to your top 20 codes by revenue covers the vast majority of your duty exposure.

For each top code, gather product specifications, technical datasheets, marketing materials, and manufacturing documentation. Re-run the GRI analysis from scratch without looking at your existing classification. Where your fresh analysis diverges from your filed code, investigate further. This "clean sheet" approach frequently reveals classifications inherited from brokers years ago that were never tested against current legal notes.

If the discrepancy suggests a material duty differential or potential penalty exposure, consult a licensed customs attorney before filing corrections. Prior disclosures must be drafted carefully to avoid inadvertently admitting liability beyond the disclosed scope. Most customs attorneys will offer a complementary consultation to evaluate your exposure — the cost of that call is trivial compared to the $10,000+ per-violation penalties you may otherwise face.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is the difference between HS code, HTS code, and TARIC code — and which one do I actually need?

A: The HS code is the global 6-digit Harmonized System code used by all WCO member countries. The HTS code is the US national extension to 8 and 10 digits — the 10-digit version is required for US customs entries. The TARIC code is the EU's equivalent extension with additional 10-digit measure codes. If you import into the US, you need the 10-digit HTS code; into the EU, the 10-digit TARIC code; into the UK, the 10-digit UK Global Tariff code. A 6-digit HS code alone is insufficient for any customs declaration.

Q: How do I classify a product with multiple functions, like a smartwatch with heart-rate monitoring?

A: Apply GRI 3(b), the essential character test. Analyze which component or function defines the product's primary purpose. CBP rulings on similar products are instructive — a smartwatch marketed primarily as a health-monitoring device with notification features may classify differently than one marketed as a general-purpose wearable. Document your principal function analysis with marketing materials, engineering specifications, and intended-use evidence. When in doubt, review existing NY and HQ rulings for comparable products, or file a binding ruling request with detailed specifications.

Q: What happens if I used the wrong tariff classification code — can I correct it retroactively?

A: Yes, through the Post-Summary Correction (PSC) process, which allows correction of entry data within 180 days of the entry date, or through a prior disclosure if the error involves duty underpayment. Voluntary prior disclosures before CBP initiates an investigation substantially mitigate penalties — often to zero monetary penalty plus interest on duties owed. After CBP discovers the error independently, penalties escalate from $10,000 per violation for negligence to $25,000–$100,000+ for fraud. The 2026 window before 2027 HS amendments take effect is strategically valuable for filing corrections.

Q: When does CBP issue a binding ruling, and can I rely on someone else's ruling?

A: CBP issues binding ruling letters (NY and HQ rulings) that set the classification for the specific product described in the ruling request. You can rely on rulings issued to other companies if your product matches the description in the ruling exactly — but subtle specification differences can invalidate reliance. The average turnaround for complex ruling requests is approximately 18 months in 2025–2026. For faster certainty, search CBP's online ruling database, compare your product specifications meticulously against existing rulings, and only file a new request when no applicable ruling exists.

Q: Do free trade agreements affect my classification choices — does my code change my FTA eligibility?

A: Absolutely. Classification determines both the base duty rate and which tariff shift rules apply for FTA eligibility. The USMCA, EU-Japan EPA, and RCEP each use classification-based rules of origin — your product must shift from a non-originating tariff subheading to an originating one to qualify. The same product classified under two different subheadings may qualify under one FTA but not another. Additionally, FTA preferential rates apply to your specific 8-digit HTS line. Verify FTA eligibility for every top-shipping code, and ensure your certificate of origin accurately reflects the same classification you use on entries.

Q: How do I classify software, digital goods, or AI-enabled devices that have no physical "principal function"?

A: Physical goods with embedded software classify according to their physical carrier and principal physical function — software on a USB drive classifies by the storage medium, not the code it contains. AI-enabled hardware classifies by the physical structure and principal intended use. Pure digital goods with no physical carrier are generally outside the scope of customs tariffs entirely. However, devices marketed with AI capabilities can trigger complicated "essential character" analysis under GRI 3(b) — a robot vacuum with AI navigation still classifies primarily as a vacuum cleaner, while an AI-powered medical diagnostic device may fall under Chapter 90 based on its medical function.
