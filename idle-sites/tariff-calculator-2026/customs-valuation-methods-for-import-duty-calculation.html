What Is Customs Valuation — and Why It Is Not the Same as Classification

Customs valuation is the process of assigning a monetary value to imported merchandise for the purpose of calculating duties and taxes. Classification (the HS code) tells you what rate applies; valuation tells you on what dollar amount that rate is applied. Both are required, and both are audited independently.

Consider a cotton T-shirt classified under HS 6109.10. The US MFN duty rate is 16.5%. If you declare a customs value of $10,600, the base duty is $1,749. If you mistakenly declare $9,000, the duty drops to $1,485 — an underpayment that CBP can treat as negligence or fraud, not an innocent math error. Valuation errors are the single most common cause of customs penalties because they are easy to spot statistically and hard to defend retroactively.

In short: classification picks the multiplier. Valuation sets the multiplicand. This article is entirely about the latter, with the 2026 twists that make it a moving target.

The Six WTO Valuation Methods (and the Order You Must Apply Them)

The WTO Agreement on Customs Valuation, incorporated into US law at 19 CFR §152 and EU law at UCC Articles 70–74, establishes a strict hierarchy of six valuation methods. You must attempt Method 1 first. The customs authority may reject it only for defined reasons, and you cannot skip ahead to a more convenient method to lower your duty.

According to WTO Secretariat data, approximately 90–95% of global import transactions are valued under Method 1. The remaining 5–10% — related-party transactions that fail the arm's-length test, consignment shipments, leased goods, and distress sales — fall to Methods 2 through 6.

Method 1: Transaction Value

Transaction Value is the price actually paid or payable for the goods when sold for export to the importing country, plus statutory additions (royalties, buyer-supplied materials, packing, commissions, and seller proceeds) and minus statutory exclusions (post-importation transport, import taxes). The next section covers this in full detail — it is where 90% of duty errors actually happen.

Methods 2–6: The Fallback Ladder

Method 2 (Identical Goods) uses the transaction value of identical goods produced in the same country, exported at the same or substantially the same time, and sold at the same commercial level and quantity. Method 3 (Similar Goods) relaxes that standard to goods that are commercially interchangeable and have like characteristics. Method 4 (Deductive Value) starts with the unit resale price of the imported goods in the importing country and subtracts commissions, duties, taxes, and post-importation transportation costs. Method 5 (Computed Value) builds up from the producer's cost of materials, fabrication, profit, and general expenses. Method 6 (Fallback) permits "reasonable means" consistent with the principles of the Agreement — a flexible last resort, not a license to invent numbers.
