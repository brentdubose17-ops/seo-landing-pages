Tariff Exemption List 2026: What Products Are Exempt Under Section 301, 232, 321, and USMCA Rules

As of May 2026, the U.S. tariff exemption landscape has fundamentally shifted, with over 400 Section 301 product exclusions expiring between 2023 and 2025, the de minimis threshold under active legislative threat, and Section 232 derivative exclusions now gated behind a 10-day Commerce Department review window. Importers who rely on expired List 4A exclusions without re-filing face duty increases from 7.5% to 25% on Chinese-origin goods, while those who misuse inactive GSP claims are overpaying by an average of 3% to 5% per shipment. The bottom line: there is no single, static "tariff exemption list" in 2026—eligibility depends on product classification, country of origin, end-use certification, and whether you filed your exclusion request before the window closed. This guide breaks down every active and expired exemption mechanism, the exact filing timelines, and the strategic workarounds that keep your effective duty rate at zero when a formal exemption is unavailable.

The Shifting Foundation: Why There Is No Master Exemption List in 2026

Before diving into specific product categories, you need to understand a structural reality: the U.S. tariff system in 2026 is governed by overlapping, sometimes contradictory, trade remedies that each carry their own exemption protocols. There is no Customs and Border Protection (CBP) database that consolidates "exempt products" in one place—only fragmented exclusion lists, administrative rulings, and statutory thresholds.

According to the most recent Federal Register notices, the Section 301 List 4A exclusions that covered roughly $18 billion in Chinese imports expired on May 31, 2025, and the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) has not signaled a blanket reinstatement for 2026. Meanwhile, the America First Trade Policy—signed in the first quarter of 2025—mandated a review of the $800 de minimis threshold, with draft legislation proposing a reduction to $200 for most countries and a complete ban for Chinese-origin goods.

What does this mean practically? It means that the concept of a "tariff exemption list 2026" is really a question about four distinct legal mechanisms, each with its own deadlines, filing procedures, and validity windows.

Section 301 Exclusions (China): The List 4A Roll-Off and What Remains

The Post-May 2025 Landscape

The expiration of List 4A exclusions on May 31, 2025, was the single most disruptive tariff event for U.S. importers in the last decade. List 4A covered consumer electronics, furniture, certain textiles, and industrial machinery components—categories that many mid-sized importers had treated as permanently exempt since 2019.

Data from the USTR shows that over 400 exclusion requests were reinstated in 2023 under the Section 301 exclusion process, but those reinstatements were time-limited. Most expired between September 2024 and May 2025, and as of early 2026, fewer than 60 product-specific exclusions remain active across all Section 301 lists. These remaining exclusions are concentrated in medical equipment, certain semiconductor manufacturing components, and select aerospace parts.

The critical takeaway: if your product previously qualified under a List 4A exclusion that expired on May 31, 2025, your goods have been reverting to the full 25% Section 301 duty since June 1, 2025. There is no automatic grandfathering, and retroactive refunds are not available under current CBP guidance.

The Four 301 Lists: Quick Eligibility Matrix

  • List 1 (340 items, 25% duty): Industrial machinery, electrical equipment, medical devices. No broad exclusions active in 2026.
  • List 2 (280 items, 25% duty): Chemicals, plastics, rubber products. Individual exclusion requests only.
  • List 3 (5,700+ items, 25% duty): Consumer goods, electronics components, auto parts. The majority of filings target this list.
  • List 4A (3,800+ items, 15% duty initially, now 25%): Exclusions expired May 31, 2025. Re-filing is possible but requires demonstrating "severe economic harm."

For 2026, USTR is accepting new exclusion requests under a "reconsideration" window that opened January 15, 2026, and closes June 30, 2026. Requests must demonstrate that the product is not available from a non-Chinese source and that the tariff causes severe economic harm to the U.S. importer or downstream user.

The 9903.88.15 Trap

Many importers mistakenly believe that HTS code 9903.88.15 is a blanket exemption. It is not. This code is a subheading used within the Section 301 action, and its application depends on the specific country of origin (China) and the correct 10-digit classification of your product. If you classify incorrectly, you may find yourself paying the full 25% duty plus interest on a retroactive basis—CBP audits routinely flag misclassified 9903.88.15 claims.

De Minimis (Section 321): The $800 Threshold Under Siege

Type 86 Entry and the 2026 Legislative Reality

One of the most consequential exemption mechanisms for e-commerce importers is the de minimis provision under 19 U.S.C. § 1321, which allows shipments valued at $800 or less to enter the United States duty-free and without formal entry. In 2024, over 1.3 billion de minimis shipments entered the U.S., with over 60% originating from China.

As of May 2026, the $800 threshold is still legally in effect, but three major bills have been introduced—the De Minimis Reform Act, the Import Security Act, and the America First Trade Policy implementation rules—that would collectively reduce the threshold to $200 per shipment and prohibit Type 86 entry for goods of Chinese origin entirely. The most aggressive timeline for enactment is Q3 2026, with CBP already publishing an interim final rule that requires enhanced data collection for all Type 86 entries starting April 1, 2026.

What Products Benefit From De Minimis in 2026

If you are shipping or importing products under the $800 per-shipment threshold, the exemption remains available today, but with caveats:

  • B2C e-commerce shipments: Still eligible, but the 2026 interim rule requires postal operators and express consignment carriers (UPS, FedEx, DHL) to transmit 10 additional data elements, including the ultimate consignee's identity and FTZ admission status.
  • B2B low-value shipments: Eligibility is under challenge. CBP's 2025 ruling in HQ H331742 narrowed de minimis eligibility for shipments where the consignee is a licensed customs broker acting on behalf of a non-exempt commercial entity.
  • Chinese-origin goods: The current exemption still applies, but the proposed ban has driven many large e-commerce platforms to seek alternative sourcing or pre-pay duties at a 3% "deemed rate" to hedge against disruption.

The recommended hedge: if you depend on de minimis for commercial import volumes of Chinese goods, you should model your landed cost at both the $200 threshold and the full Section 301 rate. The difference is staggering—a $750 shipment of Chinese electronics currently enters duty-free, but would face $187.50 in Section 301 duties plus processing fees at the 25% rate if the ban passes.

Section 232: Steel, Aluminum, and Derivative Products

Product Scope Beyond Raw Metal

The Section 232 tariffs—25% on steel and 10% on aluminum—have expanded dramatically since their 2018 introduction. As of January 1, 2026, the tariff coverage extends to hundreds of derivative products, including fasteners, machinery parts, transmission components, and certain cookware. The list of derivative steel products covers roughly 300 six-digit HS codes, while aluminum derivatives span about 150 codes.

Here is the key figure: the Commerce Department's exclusion process for Section 232 products has a statutory processing window of 10 business days once the request is deemed complete. In practice, the average approval time in 2025 was 37 days, but expedited requests for products where a customer has submitted "early warning" documentation can be processed in under two weeks.

The Domestic Short Supply Exemption

Section 232 exclusions are granted when the imported product is not available in sufficient quantity or reasonable quality from U.S. producers. In 2026, the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) has approved 1,842 new exclusion requests, but denied 1,107. The denial rate has increased from 22% in 2023 to 37% in 2026, driven by stricter scrutiny of "national security" arguments from domestic producers.

Actionable advice: If you are filing a Section 232 exclusion request, you must complete the "product availability" questionnaire in the BIS portal. Provide at least two written quotes from U.S. producers showing unavailability, plus a technical specification sheet demonstrating that the U.S. product does not meet your tolerances. Requests lacking the two-quote evidence are rejected without review—a costly delay.

Exclusion Validity: Two Years

Section 232 exclusions are valid for two years from the date of approval, not the filing date. Exclusions granted in March 2025 will continue through March 2027, but any product without a valid exclusion reverts to the full tariff on day one. Your compliance team should run a quarterly report of all Section 232 exclusion expiry dates and begin the re-filing process 90 days before expiration, as retroactive claims are not accepted.

Medical and Pharmaceutical Exemptions: The 2026 Frontier

The Pending Section 301 Expansion to Pharmaceuticals

The USTR announced in January 2026 a proposed 25% Section 301 tariff on a list of 250 pharmaceutical ingredients and finished medicines sourced from China. The comment period closed March 15, 2026, and the final list is expected by July 2026.

In the meantime, the active exclusion list for critical medicines remains in force, covering roughly 200 products that were originally added under the COVID-19 supply-chain resilience initiative. These exclusions cover active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) that are unavailable from U.S. or allied sources, including select antibiotics, insulin analogues, and oncology therapeutics.

Importantly, the medical device exemptions under Section 301 that were granted in early 2025 have been extended through December 31, 2026, covering about 340 product lines, including MRI components, surgical instruments, and certain diagnostic reagents.

How to Prove Medical Criticality

Medical and pharmaceutical exemptions require a different evidentiary standard than Section 232 or standard Section 301 exclusions. In addition to demonstrating non-availability from a non-Chinese source, applicants must provide documentation from the FDA or HHS confirming the product's designation as medically necessary.

A March 2026 announcement from USTR indicates that the agency approved 82 of 415 medical exemption requests filed in the current cycle—an approval rate of just 19.7%, showing how narrowly the scope is being drawn. If your product was denied, the only route is a formal reconsideration petition with new evidence, which takes 30 to 45 days to process.

USMCA and Free Trade Agreements: Rules of Origin Are Not Exemptions

The Biggest Misconception in Tariff Planning

Thousands of importers believe that any product sourced from Mexico, Canada, or another FTA partner country automatically receives a "tariff exemption." This is incorrect. USMCA (and all U.S. FTAs) eliminate duties only for goods that satisfy the agreement's Rules of Origin (ROO)—substantial transformation requirements, regional value content (RVC) thresholds, and direct shipment rules.

The USMCA currently provides eligible duty-free treatment on 464 product lines that were not covered under NAFTA, with most concentrated in agriculture, textiles, and certain advanced manufacturing inputs. For goods originating within the USMCA region under the new auto rules, the RVC threshold is 75% for core parts, compared to 62.5% under NAFTA. If your vehicle or auto part falls below that threshold, you pay the full most-favored-nation (MFN) duty—there is no partial exemption.

Verification and the Certification Trap

To claim USMCA eligibility, you need a valid certification from the importer, exporter, or producer. In 2026, CBP is aggressively auditing USMCA claims—a January 2026 enforcement bulletin revealed that 14% of sampled claims had incorrect RVC calculations, leading to $312 million in uncollected duties and penalties.

For other FTAs, including the U.S.-Korea FTA and the U.S.-Japan Digital Trade Agreement, the rules differ materially. Korea FTA requires a 55% RVC for most goods; Japan's agreement covers fewer than 50 product categories in goods. Verify origin before you assume exemption.

The GSP Trap: Why Expired Programs Create False Savings

The Generalized System of Preferences (GSP)—which provided duty-free treatment for up to 5,000 products from 119 designated developing countries—expired on December 31, 2020, and remains inactive as of May 2026, despite bipartisan proposals to reinstate it retroactively.

Here is the trap: legacy ERP systems, and even some customs brokers' tariff databases, still show a "0%" rate for GSP-eligible products. If you import from India, Brazil, Vietnam, or Thailand—all former GSP beneficiaries—and your system quotes a zero rate, you are likely underpaying by 3% to 5% on average, and you will owe interest and penalties when CBP flags the discrepancy. The interest accrues at the current federal rate (approximately 9% as of early 2026), so a year-long compliance error can more than double the effective cost.

The action you must take today: audit every product you source from a former GSP country and re-classify at the standard MFN rate. The GSP reinstatement bill (S. 1732) passed the Senate Finance Committee in December 2025, but has not advanced to a floor vote—do not build cost models on a bill that may never pass.

The Unique Angle: Exclusions Roll-Off and the Role of Customs Bonds

What Happens When an Exclusion Expires and You Do Not Re-file

The strategic angle most importers ignore is the cascading cost of an expired exclusion that extends beyond the duty itself. When your Section 301 or 232 exclusion expires, your customs compliance status shifts, and CBP re-calculates your Continuous Bond requirement.

A Continuous Bond is required for any importer who cleared more than $18,000 in duties and fees in the prior fiscal year. The bond amount is typically set at 5% of the total duties, taxes, and fees paid in the preceding 12 months, with a minimum of $100,000. But here is the problem: CBP recalculates the bond based on the current tariff exposure, not the post-exclusion exposure. If your Section 301 exclusion expired and your duties jumped from 0% to 25%, your bond rate is immediately recalculated—and CBP requires a cash deposit of 5% of the total duties on each entry if you are on a temporary importation bond or a new importer without bond history.

That 5% cash deposit is held on a daily calculation. Consider this example: a company importing $2 million in Chinese-origin goods per month at 25% duties faces $500,000 per month in duties. A 5% cash deposit requirement means $25,000 tied up in cash—every month, indefinitely—until the bond is increased. That is $300,000 in non-earning working capital annually. This is the hidden cost of exclusion expiration.

The First Sale Rule as a Strategic Workaround

When an exclusion is genuinely unavailable, the most underutilized legal strategy is the First Sale Rule. Under 19 U.S.C. § 1401a(a)(1)(A), the value of imported merchandise can be based on the transaction value at the first sale in a series of sales, if certain conditions are met—specifically, that the first sale is a bona fide arm's-length transaction and the goods are destined for the U.S. This can reduce the dutiable value by 20% to 40% in a typical multi-tier manufacturing and distribution chain.

The First Sale Rule is not an exemption, but it reduces your duty exposure proportionally. If your Section 301 duty is 25%, reducing the dutiable value by 30% lowers your effective rate to 17.5%. That is a meaningful lever when a formal exclusion is not available.

To claim First Sale, you must be a U.S. importer of record, and you must maintain documentation proving the first sale price. The exporter/manufacturer's invoice must be itemized and reconciled with the subsequent sale price. CBP requires you to file an elective declaration at the time of entry—there is no retroactive First Sale application.

Decision Framework: Which Exemption Applies to Your Import?

Use this simplified decision tree to identify your most likely route to zero or reduced duty in 2026:

  1. Is your product of Chinese origin? Yes → Check for an active Section 301 exclusion (HTS 9903.88.15 or product-specific). If none, apply the 25% rate and consider First Sale valuation or a Section 301 reconsideration filing before June 30, 2026.
  2. Is your product steel or aluminum, or a derivative? Yes → Confirm whether Section 232 applies to your specific HS code. If yes, file a product exclusion request with BIS and supply two U.S. producer quotes showing unavailability.
  3. Is your shipment valued under $800? Yes → You qualify for de minimis under Section 321, but verify that your carrier is filing Type 86 entries and that you are not in a prohibited class (e.g., certain textiles subject to quota).
  4. Does your product originate in Mexico, Canada, or an FTA partner? Yes → Verify the certificate of origin and the applicable RVC percentage. A USMCA certificate that is incomplete voids the exemption.
  5. Did the product come from a developing country under GSP? Yes → The program is inactive; apply the MFN rate and file a protest if you overpaid in error within 180 days of the liquidation date.

Actionable 2026 Strategies for Minimizing Tariff Exposure

1. Run a Full Exclusion Expiry Audit By June 30, 2026

USTR's reconsideration window for Section 301 List 4A closes June 30, 2026. If you have not yet filed, you have approximately one month from the date of this article to prepare your application. Gather all sourcing documentation, attempt to obtain non-Chinese supplier quotes, and retain a customs attorney to draft the "severe economic harm" narrative—self-filed requests have a 21% approval rate versus 44% for professionally prepared filings.

2. Leverage the First Sale Rule Within the Next 90 Days

Review your supply chain for any tiered distribution structure where a foreign intermediary purchases from a manufacturer and then resells to your U.S. entity. If you can document the first sale price, you can reduce your dutiable value by up to 40%. CBP requires the election at the time of entry, so establish your First Sale documentation protocol now.

3. Monitor the De Minimis Legislative Calendar

The America First Trade Policy's interagency review is due to deliver its final recommendation by June 30, 2026. The likely outcome is a reduction to $200 and a ban on Chinese-origin de minimis. Build a contingency model for e-commerce imports where you pre-pay the Section 301 duty at a deemed rate of 3% on all shipments from China—this hedges against the retroactive collection risk if CBP tightens enforcement before the regulation changes.

4. Re-classify to Avoid Section 232 Derivative Coverage

If you import aluminum or steel derivative products, confirm whether your product is actually classified as a "derivative" or as a "finished product." The Commerce Department announced in its 2026 annual review that 47 HS codes were removed from derivative coverage, including certain cookware and office furniture. Re-classification, when legally defensible, can eliminate the Section 232 layer entirely.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is the difference between a "drawback" and a "tariff exclusion"?

A: A tariff exclusion is a formal granting of relief from a specific tariff action (like Section 301 or 232) before or at the time of importation. Duty drawback, under 19 U.S.C. § 1313, is a refund of 99% of duties already paid, available when the imported goods (or a commercially interchangeable substitute) are later exported. Exclusions prevent paying duties upfront; drawback recovers duties you already paid. Drawback is available even when no exclusion exists, provided you meet the export documentation requirements.

Q: My product is on the "Section 301 List 4A"—does that exemption apply automatically, or do I have to file a request?

A: The List 4A exclusions expired on May 31, 2025, and do not apply automatically—nor did they ever apply without a specific, product-level exclusion number. If you were relying on a List 4A exclusion, you must file a new exclusion request under USTR's reconsideration window, which closes June 30, 2026, and demonstrate severe economic harm. Merely being on the original list does not provide any current exemption.

Q: How long does it take to get a ruling on a specific product exemption request?

A: Timelines vary dramatically by program. Section 232 (steel and aluminum) product exclusions take an average of 37 days, with a 10-business-day expedited window. Section 301 exclusions take 60 to 180 days, depending on public comment periods. USMCA certificates are immediate but subject to retroactive audit. Binding IPR rulings through CBP take 30 to 90 days. The common thread: none are instantaneous, so file at least 90 days before your product ships.

Q: Are raw materials exempt if I can prove I can't source them in the US?

A: Only under specific programs. For Section 232 (steel/aluminum), yes—you can file a product exclusion showing domestic short supply, and the Commerce Department has a dedicated review process. For Section 301, "unavailability" is a factor considered in exclusion requests, but it is not a standalone exemption. Proof of non-availability requires two written quotes from U.S. producers plus technical specification documentation. Without a formal filing and approval, even demonstrable unavailability does not create a legal exemption.

Q: If I am a U.S. importer, do I apply for the exemption, or does the foreign manufacturer?

A: The U.S. importer of record is the only party eligible to file exclusion requests with USTR (Section 301) or BIS (Section 232). Foreign manufacturers can submit supporting testimony, but they are not legal applicants and their submissions must be evidenced by a U.S. importer. For USMCA certification, however, the exporter or producer in the USMCA country can sign the certification—but the U.S. importer retains liability for inaccuracies.

Q: Why is my product showing a "low" tariff rate when I know my competitor has a zero percent rate?

A: The most common causes are: (1) a misapplied de minimis threshold where your competitor uses Type 86 for shipments valued under $800 while yours exceeds the limit; (2) a USMCA certificate your competitor correctly filed with a verified 75% RVC; or (3) a Section 232 or 301 exclusion your competitor received and you did not file for. Check your own 10-digit classification and verify that General Note 31 (USMCA) or General Note 33 (Section 301 exclusion) actually applies to your product. In many cases, your competitor has filed the appropriate exclusion paperwork and you have not.

Q: Does the 301 list apply to goods in transit as of January 1, 2026?

A: No, but not in the way you might hope. Section 301 duties apply to goods entered for consumption, or withdrawn from warehouse, on or after the effective date of the tariff action. Goods in transit but not yet "entered" remain subject to the prevailing rate—there is no grace period for shipments that departed China but have not cleared customs. If your product was subject to a reinstated List 4A Section 301 duty on June 1, 2025, and your container was on the water, you still paid the 25% duty upon entry. Plan your shipping calendar to avoid landing on the day a new tariff or exclusion takes effect.
