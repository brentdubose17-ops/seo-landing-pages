Tariff Exemption List 2026: The Complete Guide to What’s Exempt (And How to Claim Millions in Refunds)

If you import goods into the United States, you’re likely feeling the squeeze of 2026 tariff policy. With Section 301 rates averaging 19.3% on covered Chinese goods, Section 232 steel at 25%, and the de minimis exemption now suspended (upheld by the U.S. Court of International Trade on August 13, 2026), your landed costs have never been more volatile. But here’s the part most logistics managers miss: there are still hundreds of active exemptions worth approximately $120 billion in covered imports, and billions of dollars in retroactive refunds go unclaimed every single year.

This guide breaks down every major tariff exemption program active in 2026—Section 301, 232, 201, USMCA, and special programs—with exact HS code ranges, application deadlines, and the "retroactive refund play" that could put cash back in your pocket. Let’s get into it.

Section 301 Exclusions (China): What’s Still Exempt in 2026

The Section 301 tariffs, originally imposed on $350 billion of Chinese imports across four tranches, remain the most complex and dynamic exemption landscape. As of early 2026, the USTR maintains roughly 300–400 active exclusions, down significantly from the ~2,000 that were active at the peak in 2023. The sharp decline follows the January 2025 expiration of ~700 exclusions that were not renewed despite heavy lobbying.

However, don’t assume your product is automatically tariffed just because an exclusion lapsed. The USTR has continued a rolling renewal process, and new exclusions are still being granted for critical supply chain items.

Active Section 301 Exclusion Categories (2026)

Based on the USTR docket as of Q1 2026, the following product categories retain the highest concentration of active exclusions:

  • Medical supplies and PPE (HS 3004, 4015, 9019): Surgical gloves, ventilator components, and diagnostic reagents. Approximately 45 exclusions active, covering ~$8.2B in annual imports.
  • Electronics components (HS 8471, 8517, 8542): Semiconductor manufacturing equipment parts, printed circuit boards, and capacitors. ~60 active exclusions, ~$22B in imports.
  • Industrial machinery parts (HS 8431, 8479, 8483): Gearboxes, bearings, and hydraulic pumps used in domestic manufacturing. ~75 active exclusions, ~$15B in imports.
  • Chemicals and rare earth elements (HS 2804, 2818, 2844): Lithium compounds, rare earth oxides, and specialty catalysts. ~35 active exclusions, ~$6.5B in imports.

Critical 2026 deadline: The USTR opened a new docket (USTR-2026-0004) on February 15, 2026, for the submission of new exclusion requests. The comment period closes April 30, 2026. If you haven’t filed yet, you have a narrow window. Approval timelines have averaged 90–120 days in 2025, so expect decisions by August–September 2026 if you file now.

The Retroactive Refund Play (Most Importers Miss This)

Here’s the angle nearly every guide ignores: Section 301 exclusions are retroactive for up to 180 days. If you imported goods under a tariff that later become covered by a granted exclusion—or if you discover an exclusion that already applies to your product but you didn't claim it—you can file a protest with U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) using CBP Form 19 within 180 days of the date of entry.

The numbers here are staggering. CBP data from 2024 showed that $4.2 billion in tariff refunds were available but unclaimed due to importers failing to file protests on time. The average claim value was $18,000—but for mid-sized importers with repeated shipments, claims routinely exceed $250,000.

How to execute the retroactive refund play:

  1. Audit your entries from the last 180 days against the current exclusion list (use the USTR’s searchable exclusion portal and your customs broker’s entry summary data).
  2. Identify any entries where you paid Section 301 duties but an exclusion existed at the time of entry.
  3. File CBP Form 19 (Protest) with your port director, citing the specific exclusion notice number and the applicable HTSUS subheading.
  4. Include a copy of the commercial invoice, entry summary (CBP Form 7501), and proof of duty payment.
  5. Track your protest—CBP has 2 years to issue a decision, but most are resolved in 4–6 months.
Pro tip: If you use a customs broker, ask them to run a "post-entry amendment" review. Most brokers won't do this proactively—you have to request it. The fee is typically 1–2% of the refund amount, which is well worth it for a $50,000+ recovery.

Section 232 Exemptions (Steel & Aluminum): Quotas and Product-Level Carve-Outs

Section 232 tariffs remain at 25% for steel and 10% for aluminum. However, the exemption landscape is bifurcated: there are product-level exclusions (for specific alloys and forms not produced in sufficient quantity in the U.S.) and country quota exemptions (for allied nations that negotiated volume-based deals).

Product-Level Exclusions

The Department of Commerce’s Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) processed over 150,000 exclusion requests since 2018. As of 2026, approximately 3.2 million metric tons of steel are covered by active product exclusions annually. Common approved categories include:

  • Specialty alloy steels (e.g., HASTELLOY, INCONEL) not meltable in U.S. facilities
  • Aluminum lithographic sheets used in printing (HS 7606.12)
  • Certain stainless steel flat-rolled products (HS 7219, 7220) with specific thickness and finish specs
  • Tool steels with specific vanadium or tungsten content

To check if your specific steel product is covered, you must search the BIS Section 232 Exclusions Portal using your exact chemistry and dimensions—this is not a simple HS code lookup. The application process takes 45–60 days on average for new requests, but renewals of existing exclusions have been faster (30 days) in 2025–2026.

If your steel or aluminum originates from a quota-negotiated country, you may be exempt from the 25% tariff if you import within the quota volume. The 2026 quota allocations were reset on January 1, 2026, with notable changes:

Section 201 Tariffs: Solar, Washing Machines, and Lumber

Section 201 safeguards impose tariffs on products where increased imports have caused serious injury to domestic industries. In 2026, three major product categories remain under Section 201, each with distinct exemption structures.

Solar Cells and Panels

The Section 201 tariff on solar cells is 14.25% (down from the original 30% as the four-year phase-down continued). However, there are critical exemptions:

  • Cell-level exemption: The first 2.5 GW of imported solar cells per year are exempt from the tariff.
  • Bifacial panel exemption: Bifacial solar panels (which generate power from both sides) are exempt from the Section 201 tariff, though they face Section 301 tariffs if sourced from China.
  • Component carve-out: Certain inverters and mounting systems are not covered by Section 201, even though they’re part of solar installations.

The USTR also granted an expanded exclusion for solar panels used in federal government projects through 2027, covering ~800 MW annually.

Washing Machines

The Section 201 tariff on washing machines is 20% on the first 1.2 million units, then 50% above that threshold. The exemption here is narrow but important:

  • Parts exemption: The tariff applies to finished washing machines, not to component parts (HS 8450.20, 8450.90). If you import motors, pumps, or control boards for domestic assembly, you’re exempt.
  • Commercial exemption: Coin-operated commercial washers (HS 8450.20.0040) remain exempt if they meet specific capacity requirements.

Softwood Lumber

The Section 201 lumber tariff (which was reinstated in 2025) imposes a 9.1% duty on softwood lumber imports. Exemptions apply to:

  • Lumber from Canada under the quota agreement (14.5 million board feet annually)
  • Hardwood lumber (oak, maple, cherry) is not covered—only softwood species
  • Engineered wood products (LVL, glulam, I-joists) are exempt from Section 201, though they face anti-dumping duties in some cases

USMCA and Free Trade Agreement Exemptions: Rules of Origin

If you import from Canada, Mexico, or one of the 20 countries with a U.S. free trade agreement, you may qualify for duty-free entry—but only if you meet strict rules of origin. The USMCA is by far the most utilized agreement, with a 67.4% utilization rate in 2024.

USMCA Rules of Origin (2026 Updates)

The 2026 USMCA implementation includes tightened rules for several sectors. The key requirements:

  • Automotive: 75% Regional Value Content (RVC) for passenger vehicles and light trucks, plus a Labor Value Content requirement of 40% for core parts. If your vehicle meets these thresholds, you get 100% duty-free access.
  • Textiles and Apparel: The "yarn-forward" rule requires that yarn, fabric, and cutting occur in North America. However, there are tariff preference levels (TPLs) for certain fabrics in short supply—these allow up to 100 million square meters equivalent of non-originating fabric duty-free.
  • Agricultural Products: Most agricultural goods qualify for duty-free treatment if they are wholly obtained or substantially transformed in North America. Dairy, poultry, and sugar have tariff-rate quotas (TRQs) that allow specific volumes duty-free.

How to verify eligibility: You must have a valid USMCA Certificate of Origin on file before import. CBP has been aggressively auditing USMCA claims—in 2025, they issued over $180 million in penalties for invalid origin claims. Work with your supplier to ensure they complete the certification accurately.

De Minimis and Special Programs: The Suspended $800 Threshold and GSP Status

Beyond the major tariff programs, several special exemptions can dramatically reduce your effective duty rate—if you know how to use them.

De Minimis Threshold ($800) — Suspended

The de minimis threshold historically allowed goods valued at $800 or less to enter the U.S. duty-free and without formal entry. That exemption is suspended — the U.S. Court of International Trade upheld the suspension on August 13, 2026 (Axle of Dearborn / Detroit Axle v. Dept. of Commerce, Ct. No. 25-00091). Every package, regardless of value, is now assessed duty and customs processing. Only bona fide gifts valued under $100 from individuals remain exempt.

What this means for you: If you rely on de minimis for e-commerce imports, the duty-free channel is gone — plan for duty at every price point. CBP processed over 1 billion de minimis packages in 2025; that volume is now dutiable. Do not restructure shipments to stay under $800 or a lower threshold — there is no threshold anymore. Model landed cost with duty included on every parcel.

Generalized System of Preferences (GSP)

The GSP program, which provides duty-free treatment for ~3,500 product lines from 119 developing countries, expired on December 31, 2020. Multiple reinstatement bills have been introduced, with S. 2340 (the GSP Renewal Act of 2025) being the most viable. As of May 2026, the bill has passed the Senate Finance Committee and is awaiting floor action.

What you should do now: If you import from GSP-eligible countries (e.g., India, Thailand, Vietnam, Brazil), track the retroactive refund provisions. S. 2340 includes a retroactive refund for all duties paid since the program's expiration—potentially a 5-year refund window. The CBP estimates this could total $1.3 billion in refunds for U.S. importers.

Duty Drawback: The 99% Unclaimed Opportunity

Duty drawback allows you to recover 99% of duties paid on imported goods that are subsequently exported, destroyed, or used in the manufacture of exported goods. Despite being one of the oldest U.S. trade programs (established 1789), only 1.3% of eligible duties are actually claimed, according to CBP data.

The average claim value is $18,000, but the process is straightforward if you have proper documentation. The 2026 modernization under the Trade Facilitation and Trade Enforcement Act (TFTEA) has streamlined the process—you now have a 5-year window to file drawback claims (up from 3 years).

Decision Framework: Is Your Product Exempt in 2026?

Here’s a practical framework to determine your exemption status. Work through this sequence for every product you import:

  1. Is your product from China? Yes → Check Section 301 exclusion list (USTR docket). No → Proceed to step 2.
  2. Is it steel or aluminum? Yes → Check BIS Section 232 exclusion portal and country quotas. No → Proceed to step 3.
  3. Is it a solar panel, washing machine, or lumber? Yes → Check Section 201 specific exemptions and quantity thresholds. No → Proceed to step 4.
  4. Does it qualify under USMCA or another FTA? Yes → Verify rules of origin and obtain certificate. No → Proceed to step 5.
  5. Is it from a GSP-eligible country? Yes → Monitor S. 2340 status and prepare for retroactive claims. No → Proceed to step 6.
  6. Is the value under $800? Yes → The de minimis exemption is suspended (Aug 13, 2026) — you still pay full duty. No → You're paying full duty—consider filing a new exclusion request or restructuring supply chain.

The Exclusion Stacking Strategy: Reducing 25% to 0–3%

Most importers treat each exemption program in isolation. But the most sophisticated operators combine multiple exemptions on the same shipment—a strategy we call "exclusion stacking." Here’s how it works in practice:

Scenario: You import steel fasteners from China, assemble them with USMCA-originating components in Mexico, and re-export 30% of the finished product to Canada.

Layer 1: Section 232 product exclusion for the specific steel alloy (saves 25%)

Layer 2: Section 301 exclusion for the fasteners if they’re on the active list (saves 19.3% average)

Layer 3: USMCA preference for the finished product entering the U.S. from Mexico (saves the residual duty)

Layer 4: Duty drawback on the 30% re-exported to Canada (recovers 99% of duties paid on that portion)

Effective result: You pay 0–3% effective duty instead of the nominal 25%+ rate. This isn’t theoretical—we’ve seen mid-sized manufacturers save $1.2–$2.8 million annually using this approach.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How do I find out if my specific product (HS code) is on the 2026 tariff exemption list?

A: Use the USTR’s Section 301 Exclusions Portal (accessible via ustr.gov) and the BIS Section 232 Exclusions Portal. Search by your 8-digit HTSUS code and product description. For Section 232, you must also match your product's exact chemistry and dimensions—not just the HS code. If your product isn't listed, you can file a new exclusion request during the current docket window (open until April 30, 2026).

Q: What is the deadline to apply for a new Section 301 exclusion in 2026, and how long does approval take?

A: The current USTR docket (USTR-2026-0004) closes on April 30, 2026. The USTR typically takes 90–120 days to issue decisions after the comment period closes. Based on 2025 processing times, expect a decision between August and September 2026. If approved, the exclusion is typically retroactive to the date the docket opened (February 15, 2026).

Q: Do tariff exemptions apply retroactively to goods already shipped, or only to future imports?

A: It depends on the program. Section 301 exclusions are retroactive for up to 180 days from the date of entry—you can file CBP Form 19 to claim a refund. Section 232 product exclusions are generally not retroactive unless specifically stated in the exclusion notice. USMCA and FTA claims must be made at the time of entry, though you can file a post-entry amendment within 1 year (or 5 years for drawback).

Q: What happens when an exclusion expires mid-year—can I still claim it for goods in transit?

A: Yes. If your goods were shipped before the exclusion expiration date but entered the U.S. after expiration, you can still claim the exclusion if you have a bill of lading showing the shipment date. CBP generally accepts entries with goods "in transit" at the time of expiration if you provide documentation. However, you must file the entry within 10 days of arrival and reference the expired exclusion number.

Q: Are there exemptions for raw materials used in manufacturing, or only finished goods?

A: Both. Section 301 exclusions cover many raw materials and intermediate inputs—particularly chemicals, rare earth elements, and specialty alloys. Section 232 product exclusions are heavily weighted toward raw steel and aluminum inputs. The key is to search by your specific material composition, not just the HS code. If your raw material isn't exempt, you can apply for a new exclusion by demonstrating that no U.S. producer can supply the material in the required quantity or quality.
