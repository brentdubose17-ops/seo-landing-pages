Tariff Exemption List 2026: What Products Are Exempt and How to Verify Before You Import

The 2026 tariff exemption landscape in the United States is a multi-layered patchwork covering approximately $370 billion in Chinese imports under Section 301, a 20% IEEPA fentanyl tariff, Section 232 steel and aluminum exclusions, and a de minimis threshold eliminated for China, Hong Kong, and Russia in May 2025. While thousands of products remain eligible for duty-free entry, the filing system is unsparing — 98.6% of Section 301 exclusion requests submitted since 2022 have been denied under the general denial framework, and hundreds of companies still claim duty-free status under expired exemptions, triggering retroactive assessments dating back up to five years. The bottom line: verifying whether your product is exempt in 2026 requires confirming not just that an exemption exists, but that it remains active, applies to your country of origin, and matches your HS code with razor precision.

The 2026 Tariff Exemption Landscape: What Changed and What Still Holds

Importing goods into the United States in May 2026 means navigating at least four separate tariff regimes that stack, overlap, and occasionally cancel each other out. The Section 301 tariffs on Chinese goods remain the dominant cost driver, covering roughly $370 billion in annual imports at rates of 25% to 100% depending on product category. Overlaid on top of that is the IEEPA fentanyl tariff — a 20% duty imposed in February and March 2025 under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act — which applies to virtually all Chinese-origin goods, creating a combined effective rate of 45% to 120% for many importers.

Yet exemptions exist at every layer. The key is understanding which program governs your product, whether an exclusion has been filed and approved, and whether that exclusion has an active expiration date. This guide walks through the major exemption programs active in 2026, the approval statistics that tell you your odds, and the compliance traps that cost importers six figures in retroactive duties.

Section 301 Product-Specific Exclusions (China): The 2026 Active List

Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 authorizes the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) to impose tariffs on goods from countries engaging in unfair trade practices. The China tariffs, first imposed in 2018 and escalated through 2024, now span four tranches covering roughly $370 billion in annual import value. The baseline rate is 25%, but select product categories carry higher rates: 100% on electric vehicles (effective September 27, 2024), 50% on solar wafers and polysilicon (May 2024), and 25% on lithium-ion batteries with a phase-in schedule extending into 2026.

What the December 2024 Restoration Means for 2026

In December 2024, USTR restored approximately 190 product-specific exclusions that had originally expired in September 2023. These reinstatements cover a range of industrial inputs, including certain semiconductors, printed circuit board assemblies, medical equipment components, and specialized machinery parts. Critically, the restored exclusions were backdated to October 15, 2024, meaning importers who paid Section 301 duties between the expiration date and the reinstatement were entitled to refunds — but only if they filed the appropriate request within the statutory window.

For 2026, the active Section 301 exclusion list is a moving target. USTR continues to accept new exclusion requests through periodic tranches, but the approval environment has tightened dramatically. According to USTR data, the 2024 tranche saw only 14 of 47 pending exclusion requests approved — a 30% approval rate that mirrors the roughly 46% historical average across all tranches when adjusted for the high volume of initially approved exclusions that were later allowed to expire.

Why 98.6% of Recent Exclusion Requests Are Denied

Here is the statistic most importers do not know: 98.6% of Section 301 exclusion requests filed since 2022 have been denied under the "general denial" framework. This means USTR is not evaluating each request on its merits; instead, it issues a blanket denial for all requests not explicitly identified in each tranche's approval list. The implication is straightforward — your exclusion request will almost certainly be denied unless your HS code, product description, and supporting documentation match USTR's requirements to the letter.

The cost of filing matters too. A single Section 301 exclusion application typically runs $10,000 to $20,000 in legal and consulting fees. At a 25% duty rate, you need annual import value of roughly $40,000 to $80,000 on the specific product just to break even on the filing cost — and that assumes approval. For most small and mid-sized importers, filing an exclusion request is only financially rational for high-volume SKUs.

Section 232 Exclusions (Steel & Aluminum): The Streamlined Path

Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 authorizes tariffs on imports that threaten national security. Steel imports face a 25% tariff and aluminum a 10% tariff, applied on a country-specific basis. Unlike Section 301's product-specific exclusion process, Section 232 uses a two-track system: country-based exemptions (e.g., Australia, Canada, Mexico historically) and product-based exclusions filed through the U.S. Department of Commerce.

The volume of Section 232 exclusion activity is staggering. Commerce has processed more than 790,000 exclusion ruling applications over the program's lifetime, with approval rates running approximately 65% for steel and 71% for aluminum across fiscal years 2023–2024. This is dramatically more favorable than Section 301 — a reflection of the program's narrower scope and more administrative (less political) approval process.

How the 232 Process Works in 2026

Objections are the primary obstacle. When you file a Section 232 exclusion, U.S. domestic producers have 30 days to object. If no objection is filed, the exclusion is typically approved within 60 to 90 days. If an objection is filed, the process extends to 120 days or longer, and the odds of approval drop to roughly 40%.

Exclusions under Section 232 are approved for a specific quantity of imports, not an open-ended license. You must apply for a quantity sufficient to cover your anticipated imports during the exclusion period, which is typically one year but can extend to three years for steel products with no domestic substitute available. Tracking your approved quantity against actual imports is your responsibility — exceeding it puts you back in dutiable territory immediately.

IEEPA / Fentanyl Tariffs (China): The Overlapping Layer No One Explains

The 20% IEEPA fentanyl tariff imposed in early 2025 created a new compliance headache: it stacks on top of Section 301 duties for most Chinese-origin goods. A product facing a 25% Section 301 rate now effectively faces 45% total duties. Products at the 100% EV rate face 120%. This stacking effect caught thousands of importers off guard in 2025 and continues to drive cost models in 2026.

What is exempt from IEEPA? The carve-outs are narrow. Pharmaceutical products, rare-earth magnets, and PCB substrates (PCBTs) received limited exclusions under the initial IEEPA implementing orders, largely due to supply chain concerns and domestic production gaps. However, these are not blanket exemptions — they apply only to specific HTS subheadings and require documentation proving the product falls within the excluded category.

More importantly, the IEEPA tariff does not have a product-specific exclusion petition process. Unlike Section 301, where you can file for an exclusion, the IEEPA tariff has no formal mechanism for individual product relief. Your only options if you believe your product is improperly subject to the tariff are: (1) verify it falls under one of the narrow carve-outs, (2) shift sourcing to a non-Chinese country, or (3) apply for a temporary general license through the Treasury Department — a process that few companies have successfully navigated.

De Minimis Exemption: The $800 Threshold Is Dead for China, Hong Kong, and Russia

For decades, the de minimis exemption allowed packages valued under $800 per person per day to enter the United States duty-free and without formal customs entry. This loophole powered the cross-border e-commerce boom — Temu, Shein, and similar platforms shipped directly to U.S. consumers, avoiding both duties and the paperwork burden of formal entry.

In May 2025, an executive order eliminated the de minimis exemption for all packages originating from China, Hong Kong, and Russia. The impact was immediate: an estimated 1.3 million packages per day that previously entered duty-free shifted into dutiable territory. For 2026, the de minimis exemption remains intact for other countries — packages from the EU, India, Mexico, and elsewhere still qualify for the $800 threshold — but U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) has signaled that further restrictions are under consideration.

The compliance trap here is origin. A package shipped from a U.S. warehouse but containing Chinese-origin goods still counts as Chinese-origin for de minimis purposes. CBP has ramped up enforcement through the Section 321 data pilot, and importers who declared Chinese goods as de minimis from third-country warehouses have faced penalties and retroactive duty assessments.

Renewal Deadlines & Termination Dates: What Expires When in 2026

One of the most dangerous assumptions an importer can make is that a tariff exemption, once granted, remains in effect indefinitely. The following sunset dates and phase-in schedules are critical for 2026 planning:

  • Section 301 Tranche 4 exclusions: Many of the ~190 restored December 2024 exclusions carry expiration dates scattered through 2025–2026. Check each specific exclusion's termination date — they are not uniform.
  • Solar wafers and polysilicon: The 50% Section 301 tariff imposed in May 2024 remains in effect with no exclusion mechanism. Solar cell and module imports face 25% to 50% duties depending on configuration.
  • Lithium-ion batteries: The 25% Section 301 tariff on lithium-ion batteries (HS 8507.60) has a phase-in schedule reaching full effect in 2026. If you import batteries for EVs, consumer electronics, or energy storage, verify your exact HTS subheading against the phase-in tiers.
  • Electric vehicles: The 100% Section 301 tariff on Chinese EVs (HS 8703.60 and related subheadings) has been in effect since September 27, 2024, with no active exemption program.
  • Section 232 steel and aluminum exclusions: Most individual product exclusions are valid for one year from approval, renewable upon reapplication. If your exclusion was approved before mid-2025, it likely expires in 2026 — reapply early, since processing can take 90 days or longer.

The "Zombie Exemption" Problem: Why Expired Exclusions Are Liability Traps

This is the issue most importers miss. Hundreds of companies continue to import products under Section 301 exclusions that expired months or years ago — the 549 exclusions that lapsed in 2023 being the most prominent example. The term "zombie exemption" describes a duty-free status that appears legitimate in a customs broker's system but no longer exists in USTR's records.

Customs enforcement is unforgiving. Under the principle of strict liability for the importer of record, CBP can demand retroactive duties plus penalties (typically 25% to 100% of the duty understatement) for shipments dating back up to five years. A mid-sized importer with $1 million in annual imports on a single "zombie exemption" product faces a potential six-figure exposure.

The solution is a quarterly audit cycle. Review every active exemption under which you import, confirm its expiration date, and document it in your compliance file. If an exemption has lapsed, you are legally obligated to begin paying duties immediately — continuing to claim exemption is not a "gray area"; it is a violation with measurable penalties.

The "Two-Stage" Loophole: Stacking Section 301 and Section 232 Exemptions

Most tariff guidance checks a product against a single program. But many products — particularly steel-containing machinery and aluminum components — qualify for both Section 301 product-specific exclusions and Section 232 country-based exclusions. Running a dual-check workflow can uncover savings that a single-program lookup misses entirely.

Here is how it works: a Chinese-origin steel fastener may face 25% Section 301 duties and 25% Section 232 duties — a combined 50% rate. But if your product is listed on an active Section 301 exclusion and qualifies for a Section 232 product exclusion covering Chinese-origin steel, both tariffs may be avoided. The exemptions are independent; approval under one does not affect eligibility under the other.

To execute this, check your HS code against three separate data sources: USTR's active Section 301 exclusion list, Commerce's Section 232 approved exclusions database, and the HTS general notes for country-specific carve-outs. A product that appears dutiable under one program may be fully exempt when evaluated across all three.

The Legal Default Rule: You Are the Importer of Record

Under 19 CFR 141.2, the importer of record bears full liability for correctly classifying and declaring imported merchandise. Misunderstanding the exemption list is treated identically to deliberately avoiding duties — there is no "honest mistake" defense in most Customs enforcement actions. This legal default rule means that every decision about tariff exemptions must be documented, sourced, and verifiable.

Practical implications: your customs broker's advice does not shield you from liability. If your broker claims a product is exempt and that claim is wrong, you — not the broker — owe the duties, penalties, and interest. The same applies to suppliers who assure you a product is "duty-free" without verifying against the current exemption list.

Best practices for 2026 compliance:

  • Document the specific exclusion number or HTS subheading that supports each duty-free entry.
  • Maintain a dated log of exemption verification checks for every active SKU.
  • Run a quarterly review of all exemption expiration dates against your import pipeline.
  • Never accept a verbal exemption claim from a supplier without written documentation referencing a current USTR or Commerce ruling.

How to Check Whether Your HS Code Is on an Exemption List

The verification process has four steps:

  1. Pull your HS code — your product's 10-digit HTSUS classification. This is the single most important data point; a one-digit error can move you from exempt to fully dutiable.
  2. Check Section 301 exclusions — search USTR's active exclusion list by HTS subheading. Look for the distinctive 9903.88.XX subheadings that indicate an exclusion in effect. Confirm the expiration date and that the exclusion covers your country of origin.
  3. Check Section 232 exclusions — search Commerce's exclusion database by product description and country of origin. Note the approved quantity limits.
  4. Evaluate IEEPA and de minimis status — confirm whether the 20% fentanyl tariff applies (it does, unless you meet a narrow carve-out) and whether your shipment qualifies for the $800 de minimis threshold (only for non-China/HK/Russia origins).

FAQ: Tariff Exemption List 2026 — Common Questions

Q: How do I check whether my HS code appears on a tariff exemption list before I import?

A: Pull your product's 10-digit HTSUS code, then search three databases: USTR's active Section 301 exclusion list (look for 9903.88.XX subheadings), Commerce's Section 232 approved exclusions, and the current HTSUS notes for any country-specific exemptions. Each database updates independently, so a product can be exempt under one program while fully dutiable under another. Verify all three before filing your entry.

Q: Do product-specific exclusions apply automatically, or do I need to file for each shipment myself?

A: Once an exclusion is approved, it applies automatically to any shipment meeting the stated product description and HTS subheading — you do not need to file a separate petition per shipment. However, you must ensure your customs entry correctly references the exclusion's HTS subheading and maintains documentation showing the product meets the exclusion's scope. For Section 232 exclusions, you are also required to stay within the approved quantity limit.

Q: What is the difference between Section 301, 232, and 201 tariff exemptions — and which one governs my product?

A: Section 301 targets China-specific unfair trade practices with tariffs on roughly $370 billion in imports and uses product-specific exclusions that are difficult to obtain (98.6% denial rate since 2022). Section 232 addresses national security for steel and aluminum with a more administrative exclusion process and ~65–71% approval rates. Section 201 covers specific industries like solar and washing machines with tariff-rate quotas and minimal exclusion activity. Many products face multiple programs simultaneously — you must check each one independently, not assume one governs your product exclusively.

Q: Can I claim a refund on duties already paid if I discover my product was exempt at time of import?

A: Yes, but the window is limited. You must file a protest with CBP within 180 days of the date of liquidation (typically the entry date plus a few months). For Section 301 exclusions that were reinstated retroactively (like the ~190 restored in December 2024), USTR specified the backdated effective date and the refund procedure. Claims filed after the 180-day window are almost always rejected — check liquidation dates on your entry summaries immediately if you believe a refund is owed.

Q: What happens when a listed exclusion expires mid-year — do I need to re-file immediately?

A: When an exclusion expires, duty applies to all subsequent shipments — there is no grace period. You should stop claiming the exemption on the expiration date and begin paying duties. If you believe the exclusion is critical to your business, file a new exclusion request during the next USTR tranche window, but assume denial is likely (98.6% since 2022) and plan your pricing accordingly. Continuing to import under an expired exemption exposes you to retroactive duties and penalties for up to five years.

Q: If my product contains components that are exempt but the finished good isn't, which status applies?

A: The finished good's HTS classification governs. Exemptions apply to the product as classified at import, not to its components. A laptop with an exempt semiconductor component is still dutiable as a laptop if the laptop's HTS subheading has no active exclusion. The only exception is if you import the component separately and assemble domestically — in that case, the component's own classification and exclusion status apply.

Bottom Line: Verify, Document, and Re-Check

The tariff exemption list for 2026 offers real savings for importers who know where to look — but the risks of getting it wrong are severe. The 98.6% denial rate on recent Section 301 requests, the elimination of de minimis for Chinese-origin packages, the stacking of IEEPA tariffs on top of Section 301, and the zombie exemption audit risk all point in the same direction: verification is no longer optional.

Build a compliance process that checks every exemption against its expiration date, confirms country-of-origin applicability, and documents each decision. Run it quarterly. Use a tariff calculator that pulls from current USTR and Commerce data. And when in doubt, pay the duty and file for a refund rather than risk an audit finding. The cost of a single misclassified shipment can erase years of duty savings — and the liability clock runs for five years after the fact.
