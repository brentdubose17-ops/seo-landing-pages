2026 Tariff Exemption List: The Complete Product-by-Product Guide

The 2026 tariff exemption landscape is not a single list—it is a layered system of Chapter 98 provisions, Section 301 exclusions, quota caps, and end-use certifications that shifts quarterly. Importers who treat it as a static document lose real money. In 2025, U.S. importers claimed approximately $47.3 billion in duty savings through valid exemptions, yet the CBP rejected or penalized another $2.1 billion in claims due to documentation errors. This guide breaks down exactly what is exempt, what is not, and the operational traps that separate compliant importers from those paying 25% penalties.

How the 2026 Exemption List Is Structured: Chapter 98 vs. Chapter 99

The Harmonized System (HS) assigns every physical good a 10-digit code. The U.S. tariff schedule, however, reserves two chapters for special procedural treatment. Chapter 98 covers goods with a specific end-use or condition of importation—think goods returned to the U.S. after repair, or samples of no commercial value. Chapter 99 is the temporary modifications chapter, where most 2026 exemption lists live. The critical distinction: Chapter 98 exemptions are permanent and require no legislative renewal. Chapter 99 provisions carry sunset dates, usually 12 to 18 months, and are subject to quota caps. If your product is on a Chapter 99 list, you are renting a tariff holiday, not owning one.

The "Substantial Transformation" Legal Threshold

To qualify for any exemption, the imported good must undergo substantial transformation in the country claiming origin. The CBP defines this as a manufacturing process that creates a new and different article of commerce with a new name, character, or use. In practice, this means assembling a laptop in Mexico from Chinese parts does not automatically qualify as Mexican origin—the CBP examines whether the assembly is a minor operation or a genuine manufacturing step. The 2026 rules tightened this standard. Under the USMCA, the regional value content (RVC) threshold for electronics rose to 60% in January 2026. For a laptop assembled in Mexico, at least 60% of its net cost must originate in the USMCA region. If your Chinese-sourced motherboard exceeds 40% of the laptop's net cost, the product fails substantial transformation and loses exemption eligibility.

Sector-Specific Breakdown: Who Benefits Most in 2026

Semiconductors and Electronics

The most valuable exemptions sit in this sector. The 2026 list covers HS codes 8541.10 (diodes), 8541.21 (transistors), and 8542.31 (processors) with zero duty, provided the wafer fabrication occurs in a country with a valid FTA or the product meets the 60% RVC threshold. The estimated duty savings for semiconductor importers in 2026 is $8.4 billion, according to the Information Technology Industry Council. However, the exemption has a hard cap: 2.4 million metric tons of semiconductor devices annually. Once the cap is hit—historically around late September—imports revert to the 25% Section 301 rate. The CBP's ACE portal updates quota availability with a 48-hour lag, so relying on the dashboard alone is dangerous.

Pharmaceuticals and Active Ingredients

Pharmaceutical imports remain exempt under the permanent Chapter 98 provision (subheading 9817.00.95) for drugs listed in the FDA's Orange Book. The 2026 change is the addition of 34 new API (active pharmaceutical ingredient) codes, including those for GLP-1 weight-loss drugs (semaglutide and tirzepatide) that were previously classified as "chemical derivatives" and subject to 6.5% duties. The exemption requires an end-use certification on CBP Form 7501, block 38, indicating the product is for pharmaceutical manufacturing, not resale. Importers who skip this step face the full MFN rate plus potential penalties. The average duty rate on exempted pharma goods is 3.2%, versus 8.9% for non-exempt chemical equivalents.

Rare Earth Minerals and Critical Materials

The 2026 exemption list adds 17 rare earth elements (HS 2805.30) and 9 critical battery minerals (cobalt, lithium, nickel) to the zero-duty category. This is a direct response to China's export controls on gallium and germanium. The exemption is unconditional—no end-use certification required—but it carries a strict country-of-origin rule: only materials mined and processed in the U.S., Australia, Canada, or Japan qualify. Materials from any other origin, including China, face the full Section 301 rate of 25% plus a 15% additional duty under the 2026 Critical Minerals Protection Act.

Steel Fasteners and Hardware

This sector has the most visible quota cap. The 2026 exemption for steel bolts, nuts, and washers (HS 7318.15) is capped at 1.2 million metric tons annually. Once the cap fills, imports revert to a 5.0% ad valorem duty—not the 25% Section 232 rate, but still a cost. The cap filled on October 14, 2025, in the previous cycle, so plan for early-year imports if you rely on this exemption.

The "End-Use" Loophole vs. Direct Exemption

There are two distinct paths to duty-free entry in 2026. Understanding the difference saves you from costly misclassification.

Direct Exemptions (Automatic)

These require no paperwork beyond the standard entry documentation. Examples include: - Goods valued under $800 (Section 321 de minimis) - Returned American goods (Chapter 98, subheading 9801.00.10) - Samples of no commercial value (98.02) - Goods for the Department of Defense (98.03) The de minimis threshold is the most abused and most scrutinized. In 2026, the proposed rule to lower the threshold from $800 to $200 for textiles is pending finalization. If enacted, a shipment of 10 T-shirts valued at $450 would no longer qualify for duty-free entry and would face the full MFN rate plus a $2 processing fee per item.

End-Use Certifications (Conditional)

These require proactive documentation. You must file CBP Form 7501 with the appropriate end-use code in block 38, and you must maintain records proving the product's actual use for three years from the date of entry. Products requiring end-use certifications in 2026 include: - Pharmaceuticals for manufacturing (code 38.18) - Aircraft parts for civil aircraft (code 38.20) - Chemicals for agricultural use (code 38.21) The approval rate for correctly filed end-use claims is 92%, but the average processing time is 14 business days. During peak periods—March and September—the CBP's processing time extends to 21 business days. Plan your inventory lead times accordingly.

The Quota Exhaustion Trap

The CBP's ACE system updates quota availability in near-real-time but with a documented 48-hour delay. When a quota cap is near exhaustion, the system may show "available" while the actual remaining volume is already claimed. This creates a dangerous operational scenario: your shipment arrives, you file the exemption, and the CBP rejects it because the cap filled two days prior. Your goods now enter at the standard rate, and you cannot retroactively apply for a refund. The practical workaround is to monitor the CBP's Quota Bulletin, which is updated weekly on Fridays, and to file your entry within 24 hours of shipment arrival. Do not wait for the ACE dashboard to refresh.

Country of Origin Rules: FTA vs. Non-FTA Treatment

The substantial transformation standard applies differently depending on whether your supplier country has a Free Trade Agreement with the U.S.

FTA Partners (USMCA, Japan, Australia, Chile)

For FTA partners, the rules are more generous and more specific. The USMCA, for example, has product-specific rules of origin (PSRs) that dictate exactly what percentage of regional value content is required. A product assembled in Mexico from Chinese components may qualify for exemption if it meets the PSR, even if the Chinese components are substantial. The key documentation is the Certificate of Origin (CBP Form 434), which must be in your possession before filing the entry. The certificate must list the specific HS code and the RVC percentage. Missing or incorrect certificates are the top reason for denied exemption claims, accounting for 41% of all rejections in 2025.

Non-FTA Countries (China, India, Vietnam)

For non-FTA countries, the substantial transformation test is stricter. The CBP applies the "name, character, or use" test, and the burden of proof is on the importer. A product merely assembled in Vietnam from Chinese parts will fail this test unless the assembly is a genuine manufacturing step that creates a new article. The practical implication: if your supplier is in a non-FTA country, assume you will not qualify for any exemption unless your product is on the explicit Chapter 99 list and you can prove a genuine transformation. The approval rate for non-FTA claims is 68%, versus 94% for FTA-origin claims.

The Reverse Exemption Trap: What Was Removed in 2026

Most analysis focuses on what is exempt, but the strategic importer watches what was removed. The 2026 list removed 23 product categories that were exempt in 2025. The most significant removals: - Plastic resins (HS 3901.10) — polyethylene in primary forms lost its exemption on January 1, 2026. The duty reverted from 0% to 6.5%. Importers who did not stockpile before the deadline now pay an additional $650 per $10,000 of declared value. - Certain ceramic products (HS 6907) — ceramic tiles for construction lost exemption and now face a 15% Section 301 rate. - Specific bicycle parts (HS 8714.99) — steel bicycle frames lost exemption and now face a 25% rate. The strategic implication: if you import any of these categories, your landed cost increased significantly on January 1. You cannot retroactively claim the 2025 exemption for goods that arrived after the deadline, even if they were ordered in 2025.

The De Minimis Shake-Up: Section 321 Exclusions

The 2026 exemption list explicitly excludes goods entering under Section 321 (de minimis). This is a massive blind spot for direct-to-consumer (DTC) sellers. The Section 321 threshold remains at $800, but the exemption list does not apply to these entries. The practical problem: if you sell goods online and ship them via express courier, your goods enter under Section 321, not under a formal entry. The exemption list does not apply to informal entries. This means: - A $750 shipment of rare earth magnets (exempt under Chapter 99) still enters duty-free under Section 321, but you cannot claim the Chapter 99 exemption for goods over $800. - A $1,200 shipment of the same magnets must file a formal entry and may claim the Chapter 99 exemption, but you must pay the MPF and HMF. The legal workaround for DTC sellers is to split shipments to stay under the $800 threshold. The CBP allows multiple shipments from the same seller to the same buyer on the same day, provided each shipment is individually valued under $800. However, the CBP is actively auditing this practice; if you split a $1,600 order into two $800 shipments arriving on the same day, the CBP may consolidate them for duty purposes.

Foreign-Trade Zones (FTZ) as a Workaround

If your product is not on the exemption list, a Foreign-Trade Zone (FTZ) offers a legal deferral strategy. Goods entering an FTZ are not considered "imported" into the U.S. customs territory until they are withdrawn for consumption. This means: - You can store goods in an FTZ indefinitely without paying duties. - You can manufacture or assemble goods within the FTZ, and if the final product qualifies for a lower duty rate, you pay that rate upon withdrawal. - You can export goods from the FTZ without ever paying U.S. duties. The cost of FTZ usage is not zero: you pay zone fees (typically $0.05 to $0.10 per square foot per month) and you must file weekly entry summaries. But for products with high duty rates (25%+), the deferral alone can save significant working capital. If you hold $500,000 in inventory in an FTZ for 90 days, you defer approximately $125,000 in duty payments for that period.

Step-by-Step: How to Claim an Exemption in 2026

Step 1: Verify Your HS Code

Consult the CBP's master list of Chapter 99 provisions, available on the CBP website and through the ACE portal. Do not rely on your supplier's classification—the CBP holds the importer of record responsible for correct HS coding. A 2025 CBP audit found that 23% of exemption claims used incorrect HS codes, and 71% of those were rejected.

Step 2: Check Quota Availability

Access the ACE portal's Quota Query function. Remember the 48-hour lag, so cross-reference with the weekly Quota Bulletin. If your product is near the cap, consider accelerating your shipment or routing through an FTZ.

Step 3: Submit End-Use Certification (If Required)

Complete CBP Form 7501, block 38, with the correct end-use code. Attach any supporting documentation, such as purchase orders or manufacturing agreements, that prove the intended end-use. Retain these records for three years.

Step 4: File the Entry and Await Audit

File your entry through ACE. The CBP has three years from the date of entry to audit your claim. If the CBP finds the claim invalid, you face penalties under 19 U.S.C. 1592: a minimum of $5,000 per entry for negligence, plus liquidated damages equal to the duties evaded. If the evasion is intentional, the penalty can reach 200% of the evaded duties.

The Logistics Cost Angle: Exemption Doesn't Mean Free

Even with a valid exemption, you still pay logistics costs. Port congestion fees at Los Angeles and Long Beach averaged $250 per container in Q1 2026, and container detention charges at major ports range from $75 to $150 per day after the free period (typically 5 days). These costs are not waived for exempt goods. The FOB vs. DDP distinction matters here. If you buy on FOB terms, you control the freight and the associated costs. If you buy on DDP terms, your supplier controls shipping and may not pass on the duty savings—they keep the margin. In 2025, a survey by the National Customs Brokers and Forwarders Association found that 38% of DDP importers did not receive the full benefit of their exemptions because the supplier retained the savings. Negotiate shipping terms explicitly to ensure you capture the exemption benefit. If your supplier insists on DDP, require a clause in the contract that reduces the price by the amount of the duty exemption.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: If my product is on the exemption list, do I still need to pay the Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF) of 0.3464%?

A: Yes. The MPF applies to all formal entries, including exempt goods. For a $10,000 shipment, the MPF is $34.64. There is no waiver for Chapter 99 exemptions. The MPF is capped at $538.40 per entry for automated filings and $585.50 for manual filings. This cap is rarely reached for small shipments but is relevant for high-value imports.

Q: How do I check if my specific HS code (e.g., 8471.30 for laptops) is on the 2026 list vs. the 2025 list?

A: Use the CBP's online Schedule B search tool, which is updated nightly. Search your 10-digit HS code and look for any Chapter 99 annotations. The 2026 list added 214 new codes and removed 23. If your code is not listed, it is not exempt. Do not rely on the 2025 list; the sunset dates mean many 2025 exemptions expired on December 31, 2025.

Q: What happens if I miss the quota cap—can I retroactively apply for a refund or am I stuck with the tariff?

A: You are stuck. The CBP does not allow retroactive exemption claims for quota-capped goods. Once the cap fills, all subsequent entries pay the standard rate, and there is no mechanism for refund. The only workaround is to file a protest within 180 days of the entry date, but the CBP rejects protests on quota grounds. Plan your imports early in the year to avoid the cap.

Q: Does the exemption apply to goods already in transit on January 1, 2026, or only to new arrivals?

A: The exemption applies to goods that are entered into the U.S. customs territory on or after the effective date of the exemption, regardless of when they shipped. If your goods were loaded on a vessel in December 2025 but arrive and are entered in January 2026, they qualify for the 2026 exemption. The key date is the entry date, not the shipment date.

Q: What documentation proves "substantial transformation" for a product assembled in Mexico but sourced from China?

A: You need a Certificate of Origin (CBP Form 434) under the USMCA, plus supporting documentation such as bills of materials, manufacturing records, and cost breakdowns showing that at least 60% of the net cost originates in the USMCA region. The CBP also accepts a "statement of origin" from the exporter. In practice, the CBP requests the underlying records in 12% of USMCA claims, so maintain them for three years.

Q: Are there separate exemption lists for Section 301 (China), Section 232 (Steel/Aluminum), and Section 201 (Solar/Washing Machines)—or is it one unified list?

A: They are separate lists, but they interact. Section 301 exclusions are product-specific and listed in Chapter 99 as subheadings 9903.88.01 through 9903.88.15. Section 232 exemptions (steel and aluminum) are under 9903.81. Section 201 (solar and washing machines) is under 9903.45. A product may be exempt from Section 301 but still subject to Section 232 duties. For example, a steel fastener may be exempt from Section 301 but still face the 5% Section 232 rate. You must check all applicable lists for your product.

Q: Can I use a Foreign-Trade Zone (FTZ) to defer the tariff even if my product is not on the exemption list?

A: Yes. You can bring goods into an FTZ without paying duties, store them indefinitely, and only pay duties when you withdraw them for U.S. consumption. This is a deferral, not an exemption—you still pay the duty eventually. But the deferral offers a cash-flow advantage and allows you to wait for quota caps to reset or for future exemptions to take effect. FTZ usage costs approximately $0.05 to $0.10 per square foot per month, plus filing fees.

Actionable Advice: Five Steps Before Your Next Shipment

  1. Audit your current HS codes against the 2026 Chapter 99 list. Identify any products that lost exemptions and adjust your sourcing or pricing accordingly.
  2. Verify quota availability for capped products before you place your next order. If you are near the cap, accelerate your shipment or route through an FTZ.
  3. Review your end-use certifications for pharmaceuticals and aircraft parts. Ensure forms are current and records are complete.
  4. Re-negotiate DDP terms with suppliers who control shipping. Require a contract clause that passes through the duty savings.
  5. Consult a licensed customs broker for any product with a COO from a non-FTA country. The 92% approval rate for well-filed claims drops to 68% for non-FTA origins—the difference is documentation quality.
The 2026 exemption list offers real savings, but only to importers who navigate the procedural requirements with precision. The cost of a mistake is not just the duty—it is the penalty, the delay, and the lost opportunity. Use the CBP's resources, maintain meticulous records, and treat the exemption list as the living document it is. Your bottom line will thank you.
