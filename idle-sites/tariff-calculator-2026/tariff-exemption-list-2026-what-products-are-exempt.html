Tariff Exemption List 2026: What Products Are Actually Exempt From U.S. Tariffs

No product is exempt from all U.S. tariffs in 2026. What most "exemption lists" actually mean is that a specific product, from a specific country, classified under a specific HTS code, is exempt from one specific duty authority — usually the reciprocal tariff — while remaining fully exposed to Section 232, Section 301, antidumping, or other layers. The 2026 reciprocal tariff baseline of 10% applies to nearly all countries and all products not named in Annex II, of which the major exempt categories are Section 232 goods, copper, pharmaceuticals, semiconductors, lumber, critical minerals, and energy products. Meanwhile, the $800 de minimis safe harbor — which shielded 1.36 billion shipments in 2024 alone, per CBP — was eliminated for China on May 2, 2025 and globally on August 29, 2025. Importers who rely on a simple exempt/not-exempt list will badly misprice their landed costs in 2026.

The real work in 2026 is not finding an exemption list. It is calculating your effective duty rate after stacking — MFN + Section 301 + Section 232 + reciprocal − FTA benefits − valid exclusions. This guide breaks down every category, every authority, and every expiration date you need.

"Exempt From What?" — The Question Most Guides Never Answer

Search "tariff exemption list 2026" and you will find hundreds of articles listing product categories with a green checkmark next to them. Almost none tell you which tariff authority they are exempt from. That distinction is worth tens of thousands of dollars per container.

The Four Different Kinds of Exemption

There are four distinct mechanisms that importers casually lump together as "exemptions." They operate under completely different rules, different application processes, and different expiration dates.

  • Reciprocal tariff exemptions (Annex II). These carve specific products out of the 10% baseline and the higher country-specific rates imposed under the April 2025 reciprocal tariff framework. They do not remove Section 232 or Section 301 duties.
  • Section 232 / Section 301 exclusions. Narrow, product-specific, HTS-level carve-outs granted through a formal petition process. They expire (typically 1–2 years) and must be renewed or reapplied for.
  • FTA / duty-free treatment. Country-based programs (USMCA, KORUS, CAFTA-DR, AGOA) that reduce or eliminate MFN duties for goods meeting origin rules. They rarely eliminate Section 232 or 301.
  • De minimis (Section 321). The $800 threshold that once exempted low-value shipments from duty entirely. Ended for China on May 2, 2025 and globally on August 29, 2025.

Why This Distinction Costs Importers Money

Consider a steel derivative bracket imported from Mexico in 2026. It qualifies for USMCA preferential treatment — 0% MFN. But it is also on the expanded Section 232 derivative-product list (400+ HTS codes as of 2025), which carries a 50% duty on steel content. USMCA does not waive Section 232. The importer's "duty-free" assumption becomes a 50% hit on declared value.

A product can be exempt from the reciprocal tariff, exempt from MFN under a trade agreement, and still pay 50% under Section 232 plus 25% under Section 301.

The 2026 Reciprocal Tariff Exemption List (Annex II)

The reciprocal tariff framework established a 10% baseline duty on all countries effective April 5, 2025, with 57 trading partners assigned higher rates ranging from 11% to 50%. Those higher country rates were initially paused to July 9, 2025, then extended. Annex II is the list of product categories exempt from this entire structure.

The core Annex II categories are:

  1. Section 232 goods — steel, aluminum, autos, auto parts, copper, and their derivatives (because they are already covered under a separate duty authority)
  2. Copper — semi-finished copper products, subject to a 50% Section 232 duty effective August 1, 2025
  3. Pharmaceuticals — finished and active pharmaceutical ingredients
  4. Semiconductors — chips and related components
  5. Lumber and timber products
  6. Critical minerals — the USGS list of 50 minerals, including rare earths, lithium, cobalt, and graphite
  7. Energy and energy products — crude oil, natural gas, refined products, coal, uranium

The critical caveat: the Annex II exemption removes only the reciprocal duty. A semiconductor imported from China is exempt from the reciprocal tariff but still faces the 25% Section 301 duty on most chip categories. A pharmaceutical ingredient from a non-FTA country is exempt from reciprocal duty but may still pay MFN (typically 0% for most APIs, but not all).

Section 232 Goods: Exempt From Reciprocal, Buried by 232

Steel and aluminum are the most misunderstood category. They appear on every "exempt" list because they are exempt from reciprocal tariffs. But Section 232 steel and aluminum duties were raised from 25% to 50% effective June 4, 2025, and the derivative-product list was expanded to more than 400 HTS codes. Aluminum derivative products face a 25% duty on the aluminum content value, while steel derivatives face 50%.

In other words, if your product is exempt from the reciprocal tariff because it is a Section 232 good, you are not exempt. You are paying far more.

Critical Minerals: The Cleanest Exemption

The 50 minerals on the USGS critical minerals list are exempt from reciprocal tariffs and generally carry low or zero MFN rates. This is the closest thing to a true exemption in 2026 for raw materials — but only if the mineral is imported in a form not covered by Section 232 derivatives (processed copper, for example, is not exempt).

The De Minimis Era Is Over: No More $800 Safe Harbor

For nearly a decade, Section 321 allowed any shipment valued under $800 to enter the U.S. duty-free with minimal paperwork. CBP processed 1.36 billion de minimis shipments in 2024, up roughly 600% from 2015. That program is now gone.

  • May 2, 2025: De minimis eliminated for shipments originating in China and Hong Kong
  • August 29, 2025: De minimis eliminated globally for all countries
  • 2026 status: No $800 safe harbor for most shipments. Low-value e-commerce now requires full HTS classification and duty calculation.

This is the single biggest operational change of 2026 for direct-to-consumer importers, marketplace sellers, and anyone shipping small parcels. A $300 handbag from Vietnam that cleared duty-free in 2024 now needs an HTS code, a country of origin declaration, and a duty calculation — often 10% reciprocal + MFN.

Practical consequence: if your business model relied on de minimis, your landed cost just rose by the full duty rate on every shipment, plus the administrative cost of formal entry. There is no workaround in 2026 other than FTA-qualifying sourcing or shifting to bulk imports that were never de minimis eligible anyway.

Country-Based Duty-Free Access in 2026

Product exemptions only tell half the story. Country of origin determines the rest — and the country-based programs have changed dramatically.

USMCA — The Most Valuable Program Standing

The U.S.–Mexico–Canada Agreement grants 0% MFN duty on goods that qualify as originating under its rules of origin. Total 2024 U.S.–Mexico–Canada trade was roughly $1.8 trillion. If you import from Mexico or Canada and are not claiming USMCA preference, you are likely overpaying — though Section 232 duties on steel, aluminum, and autos still apply.

AGOA — Expired September 30, 2025

The African Growth and Opportunity Act covered 38 countries and roughly 6,500 product lines duty-free. It lapsed on September 30, 2025 and, as of 2026, has not been reauthorized in the same form. Importers who built African sourcing strategies on AGOA now face MFN + reciprocal duties unless a successor program is passed.

GSP — Still Lapsed Since December 31, 2020

The Generalized System of Preferences expired on December 31, 2020 and has not been renewed as of 2026. It previously covered 119 countries and roughly 3,500 products. Do not assume GSP comes back; it has been dead for five years.

KORUS and CAFTA-DR — Still Active

The U.S.–Korea Free Trade Agreement and the Dominican Republic–Central America Free Trade Agreement remain in force. Goods that meet their origin rules receive 0% or reduced MFN treatment. Note again that neither waives Section 232 or Section 301 duties.

How Duty Stacking Actually Works in 2026

The single most important concept for 2026 importers is stacking. U.S. tariff authorities compound; they do not replace one another.

The Stacking Formula

Effective duty rate = MFN (HTSUS Column 1) + Section 301 + Section 232 + Reciprocal (Chapter 99) − FTA preference − valid exclusions.

Chapter 99 Is Where the Exemptions Hide

All reciprocal tariff logic lives in HTSUS Chapter 99, Subchapter I, in the 9903 series. Annex II exemptions and product-specific carve-outs are encoded there as Chapter 99 notes. A competent tariff calculator must cross-reference your 8-digit HTS code against Chapter 99 rules by country of origin — not just check a static list.

Section 232 and 301 Exclusions: How to Apply and When They Expire

Both Section 232 and Section 301 exclusions are granted through formal petition processes and are strictly time-limited. Most Section 301 exclusions expired May 31, 2025; as of 2026, the vast majority of Chinese-origin products have no active exclusion.

Applying for a Section 232 Exclusion

  • File through the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) docket during an open window
  • Demonstrate the product is not produced in the U.S. in sufficient quantity or quality
  • Identify the specific HTS code and the specific derivative carve-out sought
  • Exclusions, if granted, typically run 1–2 years and must be renewed

Applying for a Section 301 Exclusion

  • File with the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative during an announced docket period
  • Must show severe economic harm or lack of domestic sourcing alternatives
  • Historically one-year duration, renewable
  • Exclusions apply only to the specific HTS classification named in the grant

The practical implication: build an expiration-monitoring process into your compliance calendar. An exclusion that covered your product in Q1 2025 may be worthless in Q1 2026, and your landed-cost model will be wrong.

Decision Tree: Calculating Your Effective Duty Rate

Run every SKU through this sequence to determine the true rate.

  1. Classify the product. Assign the correct 8-digit HTS at the subheading level, then confirm the 10-digit statistical suffix.
  2. Determine origin. Where was the product substantially transformed? Not where it shipped from.
  3. Is the origin an FTA partner? If USMCA, KORUS, or CAFTA-DR, verify the rules of origin and claim preference — but continue, because FTAs do not waive 232/301.
  4. Is the product on the Section 232 list? Check steel, aluminum, autos, auto parts, copper, and the 400+ derivatives. If yes, apply 25% or 50%.
  5. Is the product covered by Section 301? Check the ~$350B Chinese goods list (rates 7.5%–100%). Confirm whether any exclusion is still active.
  6. Is the product in Annex II? If yes, no reciprocal tariff. If no, apply 10% baseline (or higher country rate).
  7. Is the shipment under $800? In 2026, this no longer exempts you. Assume full duty.
  8. Sum the applicable layers and subtract only the load-bearing exemptions (FTA preference and valid exclusions).

Common Mistakes That Cost Importers Money

  • Treating "exempt from reciprocal" as "exempt from duty." This is the #1 error of 2026 and can misprice landed cost by 50%+ on steel, aluminum, autos, and copper.
  • Relying on de minimis for e-commerce. The safe harbor is gone. Every parcel now needs an HTS code and duty calculation.
  • Assuming AGOA or GSP are still live. Both have lapsed; sourcing strategies based on them must be re-costed.
  • Not monitoring exclusion expiration dates. Exclusions expire quietly, and the duty flows back onto your next entry.
  • Ignoring Chapter 99. The reciprocal tariff and almost all exemption logic live in 9903-series HTS codes, which many ERP systems do not natively handle.
  • Misclassifying origin. Assembly does not always equal substantial transformation; getting this wrong can void FTA claims and trigger penalties.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Are any products completely exempt from all U.S. tariffs in 2026?

A: Practically, no. Some raw critical minerals from FTA countries can enter at 0% across every authority, but even that depends on HTS classification and origin. For almost every finished or intermediate product, at least one duty authority applies — typically MFN, Section 301, Section 232, or the 10% reciprocal baseline. "Exempt" in 2026 always means exempt from a specific authority, not from duty altogether.

Q: Does the $800 de minimis exemption still exist in 2026?

A: No. The Section 321 de minimis threshold of $800 was eliminated for China and Hong Kong on May 2, 2025 and globally for all countries on August 29, 2025. In 2026, low-value shipments require full HTS classification, country-of-origin declaration, and duty calculation. The program that processed 1.36 billion shipments in 2024 is effectively closed.

Q: Which products are exempt from the 2026 reciprocal tariffs?

A: The Annex II categories: Section 232 goods (steel, aluminum, autos, auto parts, copper), pharmaceuticals, semiconductors, lumber, the 50 USGS critical minerals, and energy/energy products. Exemption is determined by HTS code and is checked in Chapter 99 of the HTSUS. It removes only the reciprocal tariff, not MFN, Section 232, or Section 301.

Q: If my product is exempt from reciprocal tariffs, do I still pay Section 301 or 232?

A: Yes — and in many cases you pay more. Steel and aluminum are exempt from the reciprocal tariff precisely because they are covered by Section 232, which was raised to 50% on June 4, 2025. Semiconductors are exempt from reciprocal duty but face Section 301 rates of 25%–50% on most Chinese-origin chips. Exemption from one authority never cancels another.

Q: How do I apply for a Section 232 or Section 301 exclusion?

A: Section 232 exclusions are petitioned through the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) during open docket windows; you must demonstrate domestic unavailability of the specific product. Section 301 exclusions go through the USTR docket. Both are product- and HTS-specific, both expire (typically within 1–2 years), and must be renewed or reapplied for. Most Section 301 exclusions expired May 31, 2025.

Q: What countries get duty-free access to the U.S. in 2026?

A: USMCA partners (Mexico and Canada), KORUS (South Korea), and CAFTA-DR countries still receive preferential duty treatment on qualifying-origin goods. AGOA expired September 30, 2025 and GSP has been lapsed since December 31, 2020. Even for active FTAs, preference applies only to MFN duty — Section 232 and Section 301 still stack on top.

Q: How do I calculate my effective tariff rate after stacking?

A: Effective rate = MFN + Section 301 + Section 232 + Reciprocal (Chapter 99) − FTA preference − valid exclusions. Start with the 8-digit HTS code, confirm the country of origin, then layer each authority in order. Use a Chapter 99-aware calculator, because the reciprocal tariff and most exemption logic lives in the 9903-series codes that generic duty lookups ignore.

The Bottom Line for 2026 Importers

The phrase "tariff exemption list" is a trap in 2026. There is no master list of products that escape duty. There are dozens of overlapping authorities, most of which stack, several of which have expired, and one of which — de minimis — no longer exists at all.

Your actual playbook is narrower and more disciplined: classify accurately at the 8-digit level, verify substantial transformation for origin, claim every FTA benefit you genuinely qualify for, monitor Section 232 and 301 exclusion expirations in a compliance calendar, and run every SKU through a Chapter 99-aware effective-duty calculation. Importers who do this will find real savings. Importers who rely on a green-checkmark exemption list will discover their exposure on the entry summary — usually the expensive way.
