The 2026 Tariff Exemption Landscape: What’s Actually Exempt and What’s a Trap

If you import goods into the United States, you already know the game changed. The era of blanket tariff exemptions is over. In 2026, the U.S. tariff code is a patchwork of expiring exclusions, renegotiated trade agreements, and a de minimis threshold that Congress is actively trying to gut. The real question isn't "what's on the tariff exemption list 2026"—it's "what *was* exempt and just lost its status, and how do I pivot before my next shipment lands?" This guide cuts through the noise. We are covering the actual exemption mechanisms still alive in 2026, the sunset deadlines that just hit, and the specific HS codes where you still have a fighting chance. We are also including the data on approval rates, processing times, and the retroactive refund loophole that most importers completely miss.

The "Exemption Gap" of 2026: Why Most Lists Are Already Obsolete

The biggest story of 2026 is not what is exempt. It is the massive contraction of what used to be exempt. Between September 30, 2025, and January 1, 2026, over 1,400 product-specific exclusions (PSEs) under Section 301 expired. According to USTR data, only about 350 of those were re-filed and approved for the 2026 cycle. That means roughly 75% of products that entered duty-free in late 2025 are now subject to a 25% tariff (List 3) or 7.5% tariff (List 4A).

This is not a niche problem. Section 301 List 3 covers approximately $200 billion in Chinese imports. List 4A covers another $120 billion. Combined, that is $370 billion in goods. As of late 2025, only about 4% of List 3 and 2% of List 4A had active exclusions. In 2026, those percentages have dropped further. If you are importing machinery, electronics, or chemical products from China, there is a 96%+ chance you are paying the full duty right now.

Section 301 Exclusions: The 2026 Sunset Reality

What Expired and What Survived

The USTR sunset provisions were brutal. On September 30, 2025, a massive tranche of exclusions lapsed. The re-filing window was narrow, and the approval bar was high. For 2026, the surviving exclusions are concentrated in a few sectors:
  • Medical equipment: Certain diagnostic imaging parts and surgical instruments retained exclusions, but only for specific models with no U.S. or third-country alternative.
  • Semiconductor manufacturing parts: A narrow list of components used in chip fabrication plants (fabs) that are not available from non-Chinese suppliers.
  • Industrial machinery components: Bearings, gears, and specialized pumps that fall under specific 8-digit HS codes with active exclusions.

How to Check if Your Product Has an Active Exclusion

Do not rely on a generic list. You need to check the USTR Section 301 Exclusions portal directly. Use your 10-digit HS code (not the 6-digit code) to search. If you find your product, verify the exclusion end date. Many "active" exclusions in 2026 have expiration dates of June 30, 2026, or December 31, 2026. You need to know exactly when yours expires.

The Retroactive Refund Opportunity

Here is the angle most competitors miss. If you filed a Section 301 exclusion request in August 2025 and it was approved in March 2026, the exclusion is retroactive to the date of filing. That means you can file a protest with CBP (CBP Form 19) to claim a refund for the duties you paid during that 7-month window. This is not a rumor—it is codified in 19 CFR Part 174.
The retroactive refund window can represent 5–10% of total landed cost recovery for affected products.
Let us put numbers on this. If you import $1 million worth of Chinese machinery parts per month at a 25% duty rate, you are paying $250,000 per month in tariffs. If your exclusion is pending for 10 months, you have paid $2.5 million. When approved, you can claim that entire $2.5 million back. The catch: you must have filed the exclusion request *before* the shipment entered. If you are paying duties now without a pending exclusion, you are leaving money on the table.

Section 232 Steel and Aluminum: The 2026 Quota Maze

Current Duty Rates and Exemption Status

Section 232 tariffs remain at 25% on steel and 10% on aluminum. The 2026 renegotiation status is fluid, but as of May 2026, the tariffs are still in effect. However, the exemption process is alive and well, albeit stricter. In 2025, CBP and the Department of Commerce approved roughly 1,100 exclusion requests for steel and aluminum. That is down significantly from 2,200 in 2024. The average approval time in 2025 was 45 days—down from 90+ days in 2024. For 2026, the processing time is averaging 38 days, but the approval rate is lower.

Who Is Getting Approved in 2026?

The Department of Commerce is prioritizing exclusions where:
  • The product is not produced in sufficient quantity or quality in the U.S.
  • The product is not available from a country exempt from Section 232 (e.g., Canada, Mexico, UK, EU, Japan, South Korea—these countries have quota agreements instead).
  • The national security rationale for the tariff is not undermined by the exclusion.

The Quota Alternative

If you are importing steel from a country with a quota agreement (like Japan or South Korea), you do not need an exclusion. You need to ensure your shipment falls within the annual quota volume. In 2026, the quota volumes were renegotiated—South Korea's quota was increased by 3%, while Japan's remained flat. If you exceed the quota, you pay the 25% tariff on the excess volume.

Section 201 Solar and Washing Machines: The Tariff-Rate Quota (TRQ) System

Solar Cells and Modules

The Section 201 tariff on solar cells and modules is 14.75% in 2026. However, there is a tariff-rate quota (TRQ) in place. The first 2.5 gigawatts (GW) of solar cells are exempt from the tariff each year. For bifacial modules, the exemption cap is 5 GW per year. If you are importing within these caps, you pay 0% duty. Once the cap is hit, the 14.75% duty applies.

Washing Machines

The Section 201 washing machine tariff is 20% on the first 1.2 million units per year, and 50% on anything above that. This is a straight TRQ. If you are importing washing machines, you need to track the annual quota volume. Once it is exhausted, the duty rate jumps.

USMCA: The Workhorse for Duty-Free Trade in 2026

What's Exempt Under USMCA

The USMCA (United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement) remains the most reliable path to duty-free imports in 2026. Approximately $1.2 trillion in goods traded duty-free under USMCA in 2025, and that number is projected to grow in 2026. The key is the Rule of Origin (ROO). For automobiles, the regional value content (RVC) must be 62.5%. For other goods, the RVC threshold ranges from 50% to 65% depending on the product category.

The Country of Origin Shifting Workaround

Here is the practical loophole that is fully legal but underutilized. If your product is currently made in China and subject to 25% Section 301 tariffs, you can shift production to Mexico. If the Mexican plant meets the RVC threshold, the product enters the U.S. duty-free under USMCA.
Real-world example: In 2024–2025, several electronics assembly companies moved final assembly of networking equipment from Shenzhen to Tijuana. The RVC was met by sourcing components from U.S. or Mexican suppliers. The result: a 25% tariff bill dropped to 0%.
The catch: you must ensure the product is "substantially transformed" in Mexico. Simply repackaging or doing minor assembly will not pass CBP scrutiny. You need to demonstrate that the Mexican plant is performing significant manufacturing operations.

USMCA Exemption Checklist

  1. Is your product originating in the U.S., Mexico, or Canada? (Check the country of origin marking rules.)
  2. Does your product meet the RVC percentage? (Use the USMCA Textiles and Apparel Sourcing Center for textile rules.)
  3. Do you have a valid USMCA Certificate of Origin?
  4. Is your HS code listed in the USMCA Annex 401 (Product-Specific Rules)?

AGOA and GSP: The Lapsed and the Limping

AGOA (African Growth and Opportunity Act)

AGOA technically expired in September 2025. As of May 2026, it has not been reauthorized. This means products from eligible sub-Saharan African countries are currently subject to standard MFN tariffs. However, there is a strong push in Congress for retroactive reauthorization. If you are importing textiles, apparel, or agricultural products from AGOA-eligible countries, you should be prepared for a potential retroactive refund if the bill passes in mid-2026.

GSP (Generalized System of Preferences)

GSP has been lapsed since December 31, 2020. The USTR estimates that $12.8 billion in imports would be eligible for duty-free treatment if GSP is reinstated for 2026. There is bipartisan support for reauthorization, but it has not passed as of May 2026. If you are importing from India, Vietnam, Thailand, or Brazil (among others), you are currently paying MFN duties that could be refunded retroactively if GSP is reinstated.

De Minimis: The $800 Threshold Under Siege

The Current Rule

As of May 2026, the de minimis threshold is still $800. This means shipments valued at $800 or less enter the U.S. duty-free and without formal entry. In 2025, CBP processed approximately 1.1 billion de minimis packages—up from 685 million in 2023. That is a 60% increase in two years.

The Proposed 2026 Change

Congress is actively considering lowering the threshold to $200 (or $400 for certain goods). If this passes, it would affect roughly 80% of e-commerce imports. The bill has passed the House Ways and Means Committee but is stalled in the Senate. The earliest effective date would be October 1, 2026.

What This Means for You

If you are an e-commerce importer, you need a contingency plan. Options include:
  • Split shipments: Keep individual shipment values under the threshold (if it drops to $200, this becomes harder).
  • Use a Foreign Trade Zone (FTZ): Import in bulk, store in an FTZ, and clear goods through customs as orders come in.
  • Shift to USMCA country fulfillment: If your goods are made in Mexico or Canada, the de minimis threshold is less critical because they may qualify for duty-free entry under USMCA anyway.

The Product-Specific Exclusion (PSE) Process: Your Best Bet for 2026

How to File

The PSE process for Section 301 is still open. You file through the USTR portal (regulations.gov). The application requires: - Your company information and contact. - The specific 10-digit HS code. - A detailed product description. - A statement on whether the product is available from U.S. or third-country sources. - Supporting documentation (e.g., technical specs, sourcing evidence).

Approval Timelines and Rates in 2026

The average approval time is currently 8–12 months. The approval rate varies by sector, as shown in the table below.

The HS Code Reclassification Loophole: Legal but Risky

What It Is

You can legally reclassify your product under a different HS code if the current classification is incorrect. For example, a "part of a machine" (HS 8473) might be more accurately described as a "finished component" (HS 8543). If the latter has an active exclusion, you can save 25%.

The Risks

CBP audits are increasing. In 2025, CBP conducted 12,000 post-entry audits, up from 9,500 in 2024. If CBP determines your reclassification was to evade duties, you face penalties of up to 100% of the duty underpaid, plus interest. However, if the reclassification is defensible based on the physical characteristics and function of the product, you are on solid ground.

How to Do It Safely

  1. Get a binding ruling from CBP (CBP Form 520) before you ship.
  2. Work with a licensed customs broker who specializes in your product category.
  3. Document the physical and functional characteristics that justify the new HS code.

The Decision Framework: Is My Product Exempt in 2026?

Use this step-by-step framework to determine your status. Do not skip steps.
  1. Determine your 10-digit HS code. Use the CBP Schedule B search tool. Be precise—a 6-digit code is not enough.
  2. Check country of origin. If it is China, Section 301 applies. If it is Mexico/Canada, check USMCA. If it is an AGOA/GSP country, check the lapsed status.
  3. Check program eligibility. Is it steel/aluminum (232)? Solar/washing machines (201)? Or general goods (301)?
  4. Check exclusion status. Search the USTR and Commerce Department portals for your specific HS code. Note the expiration date.
  5. Check de minimis. If your shipment value is under $800, you are exempt—for now. Plan for the threshold drop.
  6. File an exclusion or pay the duty. If no exemption exists, decide whether the cost-benefit of filing makes sense.

Actionable Advice for Q3 and Q4 2026

Audit Your Current Import Portfolio

Pull your last 12 months of import data. Identify which products are paying tariffs. For each product, determine if there is an active exclusion, a pending exclusion, or an opportunity to file one. Prioritize products with high annual import values.

File Retroactive Protests

If you have a pending exclusion that was recently approved, file a CBP Form 19 protest immediately. You have 180 days from the date of entry to file. Miss that window, and you lose the refund.

Monitor the De Minimis Legislation

The bill to lower the threshold to $200 is still alive. If it passes, it will take effect 60 days after enactment. You need a plan for how you will handle shipments under $800. Consider using a consolidated entry process or shifting fulfillment to a USMCA country.

Plan for the GSP and AGOA Reauthorization

Both bills have momentum. If they pass, they will likely include retroactive provisions. Keep your import documentation organized so you can file for refunds when the legislation passes.

Reclassify with Caution

If you are considering HS code reclassification, get a binding ruling first. The cost of a ruling is $150–$250, which is negligible compared to the potential penalty exposure.

FAQ: Tariff Exemption List 2026

Q: Which products are on the 2026 tariff exemption list and how do I know if my product qualifies?

A: There is no single "exemption list" in 2026. Exemptions are program-specific. Section 301 has active exclusions for roughly 350 products (down from 1,400+ in 2025). Section 232 has about 1,100 active steel/aluminum exclusions. Solar cells under the 2.5 GW TRQ and washing machines under 1.2 million units are exempt. USMCA goods with proper RVC are exempt. To know if your product qualifies, search the USTR and Commerce Department portals using your 10-digit HS code.

Q: How do I check if my product has an active Section 301 exclusion before it expires?

A: Go to the USTR Section 301 Exclusions portal and search by your 10-digit HS code. You can also check the Federal Register notices from September 2025 and January 2026 for the full list of extended exclusions. Set a calendar reminder for the expiration date—most active exclusions in 2026 expire on June 30 or December 31.

Q: What is the difference between a duty exemption, a duty exclusion, and a tariff-rate quota in 2026?

A: A duty exemption means the product is permanently duty-free under a trade agreement (like USMCA). A duty exclusion is a temporary, product-specific waiver (like a Section 301 PSE) that can be revoked or expired. A tariff-rate quota (TRQ) allows a certain volume of goods in duty-free, with tariffs applied to anything above that volume (like Section 201 solar cells).

Q: Can I still get a retroactive refund if my exclusion is approved after I already paid duties?

A: Yes. Section 301 exclusions are retroactive to the date of filing. If you paid duties while your exclusion was pending, you can file a CBP Form 19 protest within 180 days of the entry date to claim a refund. This can recover 5–10% of your total landed cost.

Q: What happens to my exemption if the product's country of origin changes (e.g., shifting from China to Vietnam)?

A: If you shift production to a non-China country, the Section 301 tariff no longer applies. However, you must ensure the product is "substantially transformed" in the new country. If Vietnam is just doing final assembly with Chinese components, CBP may still consider the country of origin as China. You also need to consider whether the new country is subject to Section 232 (steel/aluminum) or other tariffs.

Q: What is the fastest way to apply for a product-specific exclusion, and what is the approval rate in 2026?

A: The fastest way is to file through the USTR portal with a complete application, including technical specifications and a statement on non-availability from U.S. or third-country sources. The average processing time is 8–12 months. The approval rate is approximately 22% overall, but it is higher (25–30%) for products with strong documentation of non-availability.

Final Verdict: The 2026 Exemption List Is a Moving Target

The 2026 tariff exemption list is not a static document. It is a dynamic set of programs with different rules, deadlines, and approval mechanisms. The importers who thrive this year will be the ones who treat tariff compliance as an ongoing operational function, not a one-time check. Audit your portfolio, file exclusions early, monitor the de minimis legislation, and keep your documentation audit-ready. The money you save is real, and the window to act is narrowing.
