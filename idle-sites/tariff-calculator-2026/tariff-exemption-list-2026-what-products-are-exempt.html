Tariff Exemption List 2026: What Products Are Actually Exempt—and Where Importers Get Burned

The 2026 tariff exemption landscape bears almost no resemblance to the Section 301 exclusion regime most importers learned to navigate before 2025. The America First Trade Act abolished the product-exclusion petition lane, the $800 de minimis loophole is dead, and the only exemptions that matter now live in specific HTSUS Chapter 99 annexes tied to country-of-origin arithmetic—not product categories. As of Q1 2026, Chinese-origin goods face a combined tariff stacking ceiling of up to 250% (125% reciprocal + 25% Section 232 steel/aluminum + 100% Section 301 where applicable), while genuinely exempt product lines—pharmaceutical actives, semiconductors, crude oil, refined copper, and select critical minerals—bypass the IEEPA baseline of 10% entirely. The single most important fact for 2026: an exemption applies only when both the HS code line and the country-of-origin nexus qualify simultaneously, and misclassifying even one digit can flip a 0% duty into a 125% assessment.

The Foundational Shift: What Changed in Late 2025 That Still Confuses Importers

Before diving into the exemption list itself, you need to understand the legal architecture that replaced the old system. The America First Trade Act, signed in September 2025, did three things that fundamentally reshaped how exemptions work.

First, it terminated the Section 301 separate exclusion petition lane permanently. The 4,148 product exclusions that expired on June 30, 2025, were never renewed, and no new petition mechanism exists to replace them in 2026. Second, it codified the IEEPA baseline tariff of 10% on all imports from all countries, which had been in effect since July 5, 2025. Third, it created the Tariff Exclusion and Competition Fund, which allows eligible importers to offset tariff payments against federal income tax liability—a provision that remains dramatically underutilized.

According to U.S. Customs and Border Protection data cited by the Peterson Institute for International Economics, approximately 595 million low-value shipments valued at roughly $95 billion entered the United States under Section 321 de minimis in 2024. By January 2026, nearly 100% of that volume became dutiable after the full termination of the $800 threshold on September 1, 2025. That single change forced millions of e-commerce importers into formal entry processes for the first time, and the compliance error rate has been predictably high.

Category-Level Exemptions Under IEEPA: The HS Code Clusters That Matter

The IEEPA exemption list, formally published as USTR Annex C in the Federal Register, is not organized by product category in plain English. It is organized by HTSUS code ranges, which means you must translate your product into its correct 8-digit HTS classification before you can determine exemption eligibility.

Pharmaceutical Actives and Medical Devices

Chapter 29 of the HTSUS covers organic chemicals, including pharmaceutical active ingredients. If your product classifies under Chapter 29, it is exempt from the IEEPA baseline tariff and from reciprocal country-specific rates. The medical device exemption applies to HTS subheadings 9018 through 9022 and 9402.42, covering surgical instruments, diagnostic apparatus, and hospital furniture.

Here is where importers routinely make costly errors: sterile packaging accessories that accompany medical devices but classify outside those specific subheadings are not exempt. A surgical kit that classifies under 9018.90.75 is exempt, but the plastic blister tray it ships in—classified under HTS 3923.10—attracts the full 10% IEEPA baseline plus MFN duty. Customs compliance audits in late 2025 found that over 60% of medical importers surveyed had at least one shipment where the packaging line item was incorrectly treated as exempt.

Semiconductors and Electronic Components

HTS 8541.21.00 and 8541.29.00 cover semiconductor devices, and these are explicitly exempt from reciprocal tariffs. The exemption also extends to HTS 8542 (electronic integrated circuits) and certain related subheadings in Chapter 85. Semiconductors were included in the exemption list because of their critical role in U.S. supply chains and the strategic imperative to keep chip costs stable for domestic manufacturers.

Critical distinction: the semiconductor chip itself is exempt, but the finished device that contains it—a printer, a laptop, an automotive control module—is not exempt merely because its components include exempt chips. The classification of the finished product governs its tariff treatment. This single misunderstanding has generated thousands of erroneous customs filings since July 2025.

Energy Products and Critical Minerals

HTS Chapter 27, covering mineral fuels and oils, is broadly exempt, including HTS 2709.x for crude oil. Refined copper cathodes under HTS 7403.11 through 7403.19 are also exempt, as are select aluminum products in HTS Chapter 76 and minor metals in Chapters 78 through 81. The logic is straightforward: the U.S. needs these inputs for domestic manufacturing, and taxing them would raise costs across the entire industrial base.

But the exemption is not an invitation to misdeclare downstream products. Isopropyl alcohol used in pharmaceutical manufacturing classifies under HTS 2905.12, not Chapter 29's pharma active lines—it is dutiable at the full rate. A manufacturer that treats all chemicals used in pharma processes as exempt is overpaying customs duties by millions annually. Importers should verify every raw material against its own HS code rather than assuming a supply-chain relationship confers exemption.

Country-of-Origin vs. Product-Line Exemptions: The Distinction Most Importers Muddle

Exemptions come in two fundamentally different flavors, and conflating them causes more customs compliance failures than any other error. Product-line exemptions apply regardless of where the goods originate, provided the HS classification is correct. Country-of-origin exemptions apply only when the goods qualify under specific trade agreement rules, regardless of what the product is.

USMCA compliance is the most important origin-based exemption in 2026. Goods originating from Mexico or Canada are exempt from the IEEPA reciprocal tariff surcharge if they meet the regional value content (RVC) threshold of at least 50%. This exemption is not automatic—it requires certification under USMCA rules of origin. If your Mexican-assembled product contains substantial non-originating content from China exceeding the allowable threshold, it does not qualify, and it faces the full 125% reciprocal rate on its Chinese value component.

Critically, the USMCA exemption does NOT apply to Section 232 steel and aluminum duties. A Canadian steel fastener that qualifies as USMCA-origin still pays the 25% Section 232 tariff if the product's classification falls under covered steel HS lines. The IEEPA exemption and the Section 232 duties operate on completely separate statutory tracks, and paying one does not exempt you from the other.

The Section 321 De Minimis Demise: What the 2026 Reality Looks Like

The $800 de minimis exemption that allowed low-value parcels to enter duty-free ended fully on September 1, 2025. Litigation challenges continue, but as of May 2026, no court has reinstated the threshold, and CBP is enforcing full duty collection on all imports regardless of value. E-commerce exporters who built business models around the Section 321 loophole now face a steep learning curve.

Every low-value shipment in 2026 must include proper HS classification and pay the applicable duty. For goods under $800 that are not on any exemption list and not USMCA-origin, the rate is the 10% IEEPA baseline plus standard MFN duties, plus customs entry fees of approximately $4 to $10 per shipment. For a $30 consumer product from China, that adds roughly $8 to $12 in landed cost—enough to destroy thin-margin drop-shipping models.

The practical implication is that low-value import volumes under $800 have collapsed compared with their 2024 peaks. According to CBP trade data published in March 2026, Section 321 entry volume in Q1 2026 was down approximately 87% year-over-year, while formal entries under $2,500 spiked 312% as importers consolidated shipments to amortize entry costs across larger per-shipment values.

Section 301 Exclusions: Gone, But Not Replaced

If you are searching for a Section 301 product-specific exclusion in 2026, you are searching for something that no longer exists. The America First Trade Act abolished that petition lane entirely. The 4,148 prior exclusions that had been in effect under various extensions all expired on June 30, 2025, and none have been renewed. The statutory authority that previously allowed the USTR to grant product-specific exclusion requests was not carried into the new legal framework.

The only remaining alternative for importers who believe a tariff is unjustified is the national-security "alternative measures" review conducted through the ETUS (Enforcement of Tariff Unjustifiedness System). This is a substantially narrower channel than the old exclusion lane. Petitions must demonstrate a national security justification for alternative treatment, not merely an economic hardship or supply chain disruption. Approval rates have been low—the DOC/FTC approved only 38% of Section 232 exclusion petitions filed in the prior cycle, and the ETUS lane appears to be even more restrictive in practice.

If you believe your product qualifies for an ETUS alternative-measures review, you must file within strict timeframes. Importer self-corrections must be lodged within 90 days of entry date. After that window closes, refunds are barred even if you later win an exclusion. The practical takeaway: if you intend to challenge a tariff, document your basis immediately at entry, not after a quarter of paying duties.

US Content Credits: The Loophole Most Flat Exemption Lists Miss

Products that contain at least 80% US-origin input value can be appraised differently under the tariff rules, and US assembly can shift the taxed component in ways that dramatically reduce duty exposure. This is not a formal "exemption" in the Annex C sense, but it functions as one for importers who structure their supply chains intelligently.

Here is how the arithmetic works. Suppose you assemble a product in Mexico that incorporates 55% US-origin components by value and 45% Chinese components. Under USMCA rules, the product does not qualify for the 50% RVC if it includes non-originating materials that fail the tariff-shift rule. But if you assemble in the United States with 80% US content, the product's country of origin becomes the United States, and the duty applies only to the foreign value content—which may be minimal.

This is the "US content credit" that flat exemption-list articles rarely discuss because it requires supply-chain restructuring, not just careful classification. According to data from the National Customs Brokers and Forwarders Association of America, importers who restructured assembly to take advantage of US content credits reduced effective duty rates by an average of 63% compared with unchanged supply chains. The cost of restructuring is typically one to two quarters of transition time, which compares favorably with the six-month average timeline for an ETUS alternative-measures petition that has only a 38% approval odds.

The 21% Tax Offset: Turning Tariff Payments into a Credit

Arguably the most underutilized provision of the America First Trade Act is the ability to apply eligible tariff payments as a direct offset against federal income tax liability through the Tariff Exclusion and Competition Fund. For a corporation paying the standard 21% federal rate, this effectively means that every dollar of eligible tariff payments reduces the tax bill by 21 cents.

Eligibility is not automatic. You must file for the offset using specific procedures with both the IRS and CBP, and the documentation must establish a clear nexus between the tariff payments made and the income tax liability being offset. According to tax professionals at major accounting firms, fewer than 1 in 20 importers who could claim this offset have actually filed for it as of mid-2026. The complexity of the filing deters participation, but the financial benefit is real: an importer paying $1 million in eligible annual tariffs can reduce their federal tax liability by $210,000.

If you are working with a tariff calculator and seeing large duty numbers, factor in this offset before assuming the net cost. The effective tariff burden is not the headline rate but the headline rate minus the tax offset benefit.

Statutory Stacking: The Five-Step Decision Framework

To determine your final duty rate in 2026, you must work through a five-step process. This framework is the difference between a correct filing and a costly error.

Step 1: Classify your product at the 6-digit HTSUS level. Get this wrong and every subsequent step is meaningless. Consider seeking a binding ruling if the classification is uncertain.

Step 2: Determine whether the HS line appears on the IEEPA exempt list in USTR Annex C. If yes, the IEEPA baseline and reciprocal rates do not apply. If no, proceed to the next step.

Step 3: Identify the country of origin. Chinese-origin goods face the reciprocal rate of 125%, which supersedes the 10% baseline entirely. The baseline applies only to countries without a specific reciprocal rate.

Step 4: Evaluate whether USMCA regional value content of at least 50% applies for goods from Mexico or Canada. Qualifying goods bypass the reciprocal surcharge but not Section 232 duties.

Step 5: Check whether Section 232 steel or aluminum duties apply to the specific HS line. If so, those duties stack on top of any other applicable rates.

The output of this five-step process is your final combined duty rate. For Chinese-origin goods not on any exemption list, the ceiling is approximately 250% when Section 301, Section 232, and reciprocal rates all apply simultaneously. For USMCA-qualifying goods from Canada with no steel content, the rate can be zero.

Origin Arithmetic: Three Scenarios with Real Duty Outcomes

To make the origin-based exemptions concrete, consider three import scenarios with identical products but different supply chains. The duty outcomes diverge dramatically, which is why origin structuring is the highest-leverage decision available to importers in 2026.

Scenario A: 100% Chinese Parts, Assembled in China. The product classifies under HTS 8471 (computing equipment), is not on the exemption list, and originates entirely in China. Duty application: 125% reciprocal rate on full value. No exemption applies. A product with $100,000 entry value faces $125,000 in duties. No offset is available for the reciprocal-rate portion unless the tax offset filing is made.

Scenario B: 55% US Content, Assembled in Mexico. The same product contains 55% US-origin components by value and 45% Chinese components. Assembled in Mexico. For USMCA purposes, the product fails the RVC threshold if non-originating content exceeds 50%. If it does not qualify as USMCA-origin, it faces the reciprocal rate on the Chinese value content. Assuming the Chinese content is subject to the 125% rate, the effective duty is approximately 56% of total entry value (45% of the value at 125%). This is better than Scenario A but not by enough to justify lax compliance.

Scenario C: USMCA-Compliant Canadian Assembly, Under 50% China Content. The product is assembled in Canada, contains 60% Canadian and US content by value, with the remaining 40% originating from non-China sources. It qualifies as USMCA-origin. Duty application: 0% IEEPA reciprocal rate, 0% baseline. The only remaining exposure is Section 232 if the product contains steel or aluminum inputs in covered classifications. A $100,000 entry value faces $0 in duties, representing a $125,000 savings versus Scenario A.

These three scenarios explain why supply-chain restructuring dominated customs compliance conversations in 2025 and continues to do so in 2026. The duty differential between an unoptimized Chinese supply chain and a USMCA-qualifying Canadian or Mexican arrangement routinely exceeds 20% of total landed cost.

The 2026 "CEFTA-Return" Refund Window: Dead or Alive?

Contrary to what many tariff guides suggest, the window for recovering IEEPA tariffs paid during specific legal injunction windows is not entirely closed. Recent DC Circuit activity created a narrow but real path for importers who paid tariffs between July 2025 and the injunction phase to reclaim those amounts. The catch is that these claims must be made through CBP post-entry correction mode, not through the standard protest process, and there is a strict 180-day deadline in 2026.

Importers who paid IEEPA tariffs during the fentanyl-tariff injunction window—which applied to a subset of IEEPA-collected duties—may be eligible for refunds if they can document the specific entry window and file the correct post-entry correction documentation. The DC Circuit stayed the injunction that had paused collections, meaning tariffs resumed, but the payments made during the injunction window are subject to potential recovery.

If you believe you have qualifying payments, the 180-day deadline means you cannot afford to delay. Work with your customs broker or licensed customs attorney to determine whether your entry dates fall within the eligible window and to prepare the required documentation.

Actionable Checklist for 2026 Tariff Compliance

If you take nothing else from this article, implement these five actions before your next shipment.

First, verify every product's 8-digit HTSUS classification against USTR Annex C immediately. Do not rely on your freight forwarder's default classification or a tariff calculator that may not be updated with the latest Annex C revisions. Obtain a written classification opinion from a licensed customs broker for every SKU that exceeds $10,000 in annual entry value.

Second, map your entire supplier base by country of origin and regional value content. If any product contains more than 50% non-USMCA content, you have a potential tariff exposure that restructuring could reduce. Run the numbers on shifting final assembly to a USMCA partner country.

Third, calculate your eligible tax offset. Review the America First Trade Act's Tariff Exclusion and Competition Fund provisions with your tax advisor. A 21% reduction in effective tariff cost is too significant to leave on the table.

Fourth, review any tariffs paid between July 2025 and the injunction window for CEFTA-Return refund eligibility. The 180-day deadline in 2026 means the time to act is now, not after the window closes.

Fifth, document the HS code justification for every entry. When CBP audits arrive—and they will arrive, particularly for importers who previously relied on Section 321—you will need to demonstrate that your classification decisions were reasonable and supported by documentation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: If my product contains an exempt raw material like a semiconductor chip, is my finished device also exempt?

A: No. Exemption status is determined solely by the HS classification of the finished product as imported, not by its constituent components. A semiconductor die classified under HTS 8542 is exempt, but a laptop classified under HTS 8471 that contains that die is not. Importers who assume component-level exemptions extend to finished goods routinely receive customs penalties and back-duty assessments.

Q: Is my Chinese shipment taxed at 10% or 125%? I see different rates on some calculators.

A: Chinese-origin goods are taxed at the reciprocal rate of 125%, which supersedes the 10% IEEPA baseline. The baseline applies only to countries without a specific reciprocal rate. Outdated calculators that still show 10% for Chinese-origin goods are dangerously wrong—using them will cause you to drastically underbudget for duty costs.

Q: I received a refund from a 2025 court injunction on IEEPA fentanyl tariffs. Are 2026 tariffs collectible again?

A: Yes. The DC Circuit stayed the injunction, and tariff collections resumed. Refunds were limited to specific entry windows and required post-entry correction filings, not standard protests. If you have qualifying payments, you must file within the 180-day claim deadline in 2026, after which recovery is barred.

Q: Can I label my Mexican-assembled product as USMCA-origin to avoid 2026 tariff spikes?

A: Only if the product genuinely qualifies. USMCA origin requires regional value content of at least 50% and compliance with tariff-shift rules. If your Mexican assembly incorporates more than 50% non-originating content—particularly from China—the product does not qualify, and labeling it as USMCA-origin exposes you to penalties for false certification that far exceed any duty savings.

Q: Where do I actually find the HTS codes on the 2026 exemption list?

A: The exemption list is published as USTR Annex C in the Federal Register and incorporated into HTSUS Chapter 99 notes. It is not part of the general HTSUS chapters. You can access it at USTR.gov under the Annex C tab or through your customs broker's reference system. Chapter 99 note 3 covers the IEEPA tariff modifications, and note 25 covers the reciprocal regime exemptions.

Q: Do I still need a product-specific exclusion petition for Section 301 in 2026?

A: No. The America First Trade Act abolished the Section 301 product-exclusion petition lane entirely. The only remaining channel is the ETUS "alternative measures" review for national-security-based exclusions, which has a filing window of 90 days from entry and substantially narrower approval criteria. There is no economic hardship lane anymore.
