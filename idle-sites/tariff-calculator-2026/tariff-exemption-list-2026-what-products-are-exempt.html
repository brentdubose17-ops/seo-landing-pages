The 2026 Tariff Exemption Landscape: What's Still Free (and What Absolutely Isn't)

If you import goods into the United States, May 2026 is the most confusing tariff environment in a generation. The Geneva Agreement of July 30, 2025 consolidated Section 301 tariffs on roughly $400 billion of Chinese imports into a flat 30% rate, replacing the old List 1–4A patchwork of 25% and 15% duties. Meanwhile, the Section 301 exclusion process remains effectively frozen since December 2024, the China de minimis loophole is dead, and Section 232 exclusions for steel and aluminum continue to churn through a system that approves only about one in five requests.

Here's the headline: there is no single "tariff exemption list" in 2026. There are at least six overlapping regimes — Section 301, Section 232, de minimis, USMCA, GSP, and UFLPA — each with its own eligibility rules, application processes, and current status. This guide breaks down exactly which products are exempt, which exclusions expired, and how importers are legally structuring their supply chains to avoid tariffs in 2026.

Section 301 Exclusions: The Big List That Mostly Expired

Section 301 tariffs on Chinese goods began in 2018 under four tranches: List 1 and List 2 (25% on a combined $50 billion of imports), List 3 (25% on $200 billion), and List 4A (15% on roughly $120 billion). A final List 4B tranche of 7.5% tariffs on another ~$160 billion was suspended and never fully enforced.

The Geneva Agreement changed the math. As of late 2025, most Chinese-origin goods falling within the original Section 301 scope now carry a flat 30% tariff, a partial consolidation of the old 25%/15% structure. But the agreement includes a three-month review window, and the USTR's four-year Section 301 review — which began in 2025 — was still pending final conclusions as of early 2026. That means the 30% rate could be revised, extended, or layered with additional duties at any time.

What Happened to the 352 Product Exclusions?

The most important date for importers is September 30, 2024. That's when the last round of 352 product-specific exclusions expired. These exclusions, originally extended from 2022, covered roughly $40 billion in annual imports — everything from certain electric vehicle motors to specific types of medical devices and industrial machinery.

Here's what virtually every importer needs to understand: USTR has not issued a new round of Section 301 exclusions since December 2024. The exclusion process — which historically took 90–120 days per request — is effectively frozen. As of May 2026, there are zero active product-specific Section 301 exclusions for general commercial imports from China. Any article claiming otherwise is recycling pre-2024 information.

Which Products Still Hold De Facto Section 301 Relief

Even with the formal exclusion list expired, some products escape the 30% China rate through structural carve-outs. These are not official exclusions, but they function like them:

  • USMCA-qualifying goods: If a product undergoes substantial processing in Mexico or Canada — meeting the 75% regional value content (RVC) rule for vehicles and 70% North American steel content requirements — it enters duty-free even if Chinese components are involved.
  • Non-covered Chinese goods: Not all Chinese imports fall under Section 301 scope. The tariff applies to goods in specific HTS subheadings tied to the original Lists 1–4A. If your product's 10-digit HTS code was never on those lists, it may still enter at the normal MFN rate.
  • Semiconductors and pharmaceutical inputs: Most active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) under HTS 2933–2934 remain duty-free, and semiconductor devices under HTS 8541/8542 historically carried a 0% MFN rate. A 15% tariff on semiconductors was proposed in 2025 and remained pending in 2026 — but as of this writing, most chip imports from China still avoid the 30% Section 301 rate because they fall outside the original tariff scope.
The bottom line: The formal Section 301 exclusion list is gone. What remains is a patchwork of origin-based, agreement-based, and product-based loopholes — not a single official list of exempt products.

De Minimis in 2026: The $800 Question

The de minimis provision under 19 U.S.C. 1321 allows shipments valued at $800 or less to enter the United States duty-free and without formal customs entry. For years, this was the single biggest tariff "exemption" in practice — and e-commerce importers, including Amazon FBA and Shopify sellers, depended on it heavily.

The scale was staggering. In FY2024, U.S. Customs and Border Protection processed approximately 1.36 billion de minimis shipments, worth an estimated $80 billion. That was up roughly 50% year-over-year. The explosion was driven almost entirely by direct-to-consumer shipments from Chinese platforms like Shein and Temu, which shipped hundreds of millions of low-value packages annually.

The China Carve-Out That Changed Everything

Those days are over for Chinese-origin goods. A presidential proclamation — effective January 11, 2025 — eliminated the de minimis exemption for products of China (including Hong Kong), except for certain limited categories like donated goods and select medical items. That means a Chinese-origin shipment valued under $800 that once entered duty-free now faces the full Section 301 rate (30%) plus any applicable MFN duties, and it must be filed as a formal entry.

For 2026, the practical impact is enormous:

  • Chinese e-commerce packages now require formal entry, paying duties on the full value at the 30% Section 301 rate.
  • Vietnam, India, Mexico, and most other countries still qualify for the $800 de minimis threshold, with no formal entry required.
  • PEPFAR and certain medicine-related exemptions remain carved out from the China restriction, per the proclamation's text.

What De Minimis Means for Sourcing Decisions

If you are an e-commerce seller importing from China, the de minimis change adds roughly 30–35% to landed cost on small shipments, depending on your product's MFN rate. The same product sourced from Vietnam or India — assuming it meets rules-of-origin requirements — can still enter duty-free under de minimis. This single policy shift has accelerated the relocation of low-cost manufacturing out of China more than any other tariff measure.

One critical nuance: the $800 threshold is calculated on the value of the shipment, per consignee, per day. If you split a $2,000 order into three packages arriving the same day, all three are subject to formal entry. The rules also use the merchandise's value at the time of export — FOB, not CIF — for de minimis qualification. Shipping costs and insurance are generally excluded from the de minimis value determination.

Section 232: Steel, Aluminum, and the Auto Exemption Maze

Section 232 tariffs have their own separate exclusion system that remains very much alive in 2026. Steel carries a 25% tariff, and aluminum was raised to 25% in March 2025 (up from 10%). Automobiles and certain auto parts moved to a 25% tariff effective July 2025, with a USMCA-based exemption framework that has created a two-tier market.

Why Only 20% of Exclusion Requests Win

The Section 232 exclusion process is administered jointly by the Commerce Department and CBP. USTR is not involved. Companies must file requests through the BIS Section 232 Exclusions Portal, demonstrate that the steel or aluminum product is not available from U.S. producers in sufficient quantity or quality, and then survive a 30-day public comment period in which domestic mills can object.

The approval statistics are sobering. Since 2018, CBP and Commerce have reviewed more than 100,000 exclusion requests annually at peak volume, but only about 20% are approved. The rest are denied, withdrawn, or objected to by domestic producers. In 2026, with aluminum tariffs now at 25%, a fresh wave of exclusion requests for aluminum products has swamped the system — and the approval rate has ticked slightly lower.

USMCA-Compliant Autos: The One Clear Exemption

The auto tariff regime is different. Under the July 2025 implementation, vehicles and parts that qualify as USMCA-originating enjoy an exemption from the 25% Section 232 tariff. To qualify, a vehicle must meet a 75% regional value content threshold for core content, and the steel and aluminum used in the vehicle must be at least 70% North American-origin.

For parts, the exemption is more granular. Non-USMCA-compliant parts face the 25% tariff; compliant parts do not. This has created a compliance gold rush, with automakers and parts suppliers re-engineering supply chains to push North American content ratios above the thresholds. In practice, the auto exemption is less a "list" and more a calculation: run the RVC formula, document the origin of your steel and aluminum inputs, and submit the USMCA certification to CBP at entry.

Trade Agreement Exemptions: USMCA, GSP, and the UFLPA Blocker

Beyond the China-specific regimes, the most durable exemptions come from trade agreements. These are not temporary; they are treaty-level commitments that survive administration changes.

USMCA: The $1.3 Trillion Duty-Free Zone

The US-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) provides zero duty on approximately $1.3 trillion in trilateral trade. For importers, the key is proving origin. A product assembled in Mexico from Chinese components can still qualify for USMCA duty-free treatment — but only if it undergoes a tariff shift or meets the regional value content threshold (typically 60–75% RVC, depending on the product category).

The most common mistake we see: assuming that "assembled in Mexico" automatically means "USMCA-qualifying." It does not. If your Mexican assembly plant merely screws together Chinese-made parts with no substantial transformation, the product fails the tariff-shift rule and enters at the China rate. CBP audits have flagged this repeatedly, and the penalty for a false USMCA certification is severe: unpaid duties, interest, and civil penalties of up to 40% of the duty underpaid.

GSP: Still Dead in 2026

The Generalized System of Preferences (GSP), which historically granted duty-free treatment to roughly $17 billion in imports from developing countries, has been lapsed since December 2020. Congress has not reauthorized it. In 2026, GSP is a historical footnote: there are no active GSP benefits, and no retroactive reinstatement has been enacted despite years of lobbying.

If you were importing from countries like India, Thailand, or Vietnam under GSP, those preferences are gone. The practical alternative, where available, is to route qualifying production through USMCA partners or to rely on the MFN (most-favored-nation) rate, which is often low for raw materials but can be substantial for finished goods.

UFLPA: Not an Exemption — a Blockade

The Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA) works in the opposite direction to every other regime described here. There is no exemption list, only a detention list. Any product made wholly or in part in Xinjiang, or containing inputs traced to forced labor, is subject to detention and potential forfeiture — regardless of whether unrelated tariffs apply.

The data paints a stark picture. UFLPA detentions have more than doubled since 2022, with 1,628 shipments detained in FY2023 alone. Textiles, apparel, electronics, and solar components are the most-targeted categories. In 2026, if your supply chain touches Xinjiang — including through second- or third-tier suppliers — you are not looking at a tariff problem; you are looking at a complete shipment seizure. The burden of proof sits on the importer, not the government.

The 2026 Loophole Map: Duty Avoidance by Design

Here's where this article differs from the standard exemption guides. Most of them cover the official lists, then stop. The smarter play in 2026 is to understand that with formal Section 301 exclusions gone, importers are achieving tariff relief through structural strategies — not by finding an exemption code, but by redesigning the transaction.

Foreign-Trade Zones (FTZ)

An FTZ is a designated area within the United States where goods can be imported, stored, manipulated, or assembled without paying duties until the goods enter U.S. commerce for consumption. Under FTZ rules in 2026, you can:

  • Import Chinese components into an FTZ duty-free, assemble them with U.S. content, and pay duty only on the value of the foreign components exiting the zone.
  • Choose the lower of the duty rate on the finished product or the duty rate on the component parts — a significant optimization for products where the finished good carries a higher tariff than the inputs.
  • Export from the FTZ without ever paying U.S. duties, which effectively neutralizes the China tariff for goods destined overseas.

The cost-benefit math is compelling. On a $1 million shipment of Chinese components subject to 30% Section 301 duties, moving the assembly into an FTZ delays and often permanently reduces the duty burden by 20–40% — depending on the value added domestically and the final destination.

Duty Drawback

Duty drawback is a refund of up to 99% of duties paid on imported goods that are subsequently exported, destroyed, or used in the production of exported goods. In 2026, after the 2016 Trade Facilitation and Trade Enforcement Act modernized the system, drawback claims are faster and broader. The key benefit for China importers: pay the 30% Section 301 duty at entry, build the product, export it, and reclaim nearly all of that duty. The effective tariff cost drops to roughly 0.3% (the 1% that is never refunded).

Origin Restructuring and Value-Added Thresholds

Suppose you import a mechanical component from China, but you add 35% of value through U.S. or Mexican processing. In many HTS chapters, the tariff-shift rule means the product now qualifies as originating from the processing country — escaping the China-specific Section 301 rate entirely. This is legal, provided the processing is substantive and not merely cosmetic. The classic example: a Chinese-origin lighting fixture imported into Mexico, combined with a Mexican-made housing, and re-exported to the U.S. If the Mexican processing exceeds the regional value content threshold, the product enters as Mexican-origin goods, not Chinese.

HTS Reclassification: Precision Matters

One of the most misunderstood facts about the tariff system: the United States has 99 chapters and more than 20,000 unique 10-digit HTS codes. The 8-digit code determines the bilateral (China vs. rest-of-world) duty rate; the 10-digit code adds statistical detail. What most importers don't realize is that the Section 301 tariff scope is tied to specific 10-digit subheadings — and sometimes a genuine, honest reclassification can move a product out of the tariffed subheading entirely.

Example: A "portable digital assistant" under HTS 8517.62 — which falls inside the 30% China scope — may actually be classifiable as a "video game console" or "specialized computing device" under a different subheading with no Section 301 exposure. This requires a letter ruling from CBP to be safe, but it remains one of the most effective and under-used strategies in 2026.
