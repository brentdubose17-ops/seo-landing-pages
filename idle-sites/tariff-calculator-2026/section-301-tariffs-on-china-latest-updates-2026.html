Section 301 Tariffs on China: The 2026 Importer's Guide to Rates, Stacking, and Compliance

Section 301 tariffs on Chinese goods remain in force in 2026 across roughly $370 billion in annual import value, with base rates of 7.5% on List 4A and 25% on Lists 1, 2, 3, and 4A's earlier tranches, plus strategic-sector rates as high as 100% on electric vehicles and 50% on solar cells, semiconductors, polysilicon, and wafers. On January 1, 2026, four new product categories — non-EV lithium-ion batteries, natural graphite, permanent magnets, and medical gloves — stepped up to 25% under the four-year review schedule USTR finalized in 2024. Critically, Section 301 is only one layer of the 2026 duty stack: most Chinese imports also carry an additional ~20% in IEEPA tariffs (10% fentanyl-related plus a 10% reciprocal baseline, with the 24% higher reciprocal tier paused through November 10, 2026), and product-specific Section 232 or antidumping/countervailing duties can push total effective rates above 100%. The bottom line for importers: the average effective U.S. tariff on Chinese goods sits near 30% in early 2026 versus roughly 10.4% in 2024, so landed-cost models built before 2024 are now materially wrong.

The 2026 Section 301 Rate Architecture, Explained

Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 authorizes the U.S. Trade Representative to impose duties in response to foreign unfair trade practices. The China tariffs rest on that authority — not on IEEPA, and not on Section 232. That legal distinction matters enormously in 2026, because it means Section 301 duties survive independently of any court ruling on the IEEPA tariffs.

USTR administers the tariffs through Chapter 99 of the Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States (HTSUS), using subheadings in the 9903.88.xx range. The 2024 strategic-sector increases were implemented in the 9903.88.67 through .75 range, and subsequent modifications have extended that block. When you classify a Chinese-origin product, you are effectively looking for two things: its normal Chapter 1–97 HTS code (which sets the MFN rate) and any applicable 9903.88.xx heading (which adds the Section 301 rate).

The 2024 strategic-sector increases — still escalating into 2026

In May 2024, following its statutory four-year review, USTR announced targeted increases on strategic sectors. Those increases were phased in, and the final tranche landed on January 1, 2026. The current landscape:

  • Electric vehicles: 100% (effective 2024)
  • Solar cells: 50% (effective 2024)
  • Semiconductors: 50% (phased from 2025)
  • Polysilicon and wafers: 50% (effective January 1, 2025)
  • EV lithium-ion batteries and battery parts: 25% (effective 2024)
  • Steel and aluminum products: 25% (effective 2024; separate from and in addition to Section 232)
  • Syringes and needles: 100% (effective 2024)
  • Ship-to-shore cranes: 25% (effective 2024)
  • Non-EV lithium-ion batteries, natural graphite, permanent magnets, medical gloves: 25% (effective January 1, 2026)

This is where most landed-cost models break. A lithium-ion battery is not one tariff outcome — it is three. An EV battery (HTS 8507.60.0010) has carried a 25% Section 301 rate since 2024 and rises to 25% at the base level for non-EV cells (8507.60.0020) only as of January 1, 2026, meaning the same physical cell can face 7.5% or 25% depending on end-use classification and the specific statistical suffix. Permanent magnets under HTS 8505.11.00 moved from 0% to 25% on the same date, a change that hits EV drivetrains, wind turbines, hard drives, and consumer audio simultaneously.

Stacking: Section 301 Is Only One Layer of Your Duty Bill

The single biggest analytical failure in most importer calculations is treating Section 301 as the whole story. In 2026, it rarely is. Duties from different authorities generally stack, with specific carve-outs for certain combinations.

IEEPA fentanyl and reciprocal tariffs

As of early 2026, the IEEPA layer on Chinese goods consists of a 10% fentanyl-related tariff (reduced from 20% in November 2025) plus a 10% reciprocal baseline. The higher 24% reciprocal tier applicable to China is paused through November 10, 2026. That produces a combined IEEPA add-on of approximately 20% on most Chinese-origin entries.

A commonly cited figure — that the average effective U.S. tariff rate on Chinese imports reached roughly 30% in early 2026, up from about 10.4% in 2024 — reflects exactly this stacking effect, not the Section 301 rate alone.

Section 232 and the reciprocal carve-out

Section 232 national security tariffs on steel and aluminum were raised to 50% effective June 4, 2025, and copper derivatives followed at 50%. Notably, products subject to Section 232 are generally excluded from the IEEPA reciprocal tariff to avoid duplicative stacking — but they still carry Section 301 and any AD/CVD orders on top. This produces some of the highest combined rates in the schedule.

Antidumping and countervailing duties

AD/CVD orders are separate from tariffs and can dwarf them. Chinese solar cells, aluminum extrusions, wood cabinets, quartz surfaces, and steel products routinely carry AD/CVD rates in the triple digits. On top of a 25% Section 301 rate and 20% IEEPA, a 250% AD margin is not an abstraction — it is a line item.

How to Calculate Total Landed Duty: A Step-by-Step Framework

Here is the sequence a competent customs professional runs for every Chinese-origin SKU.

  1. Classify to the 10-digit level. "Battery" is not a classification. 8507.60.0010 (EV) and 8507.60.0020 (other) produce different results, especially before and after January 1, 2026.
  2. Determine country of origin — not country of shipment. Origin is based on where the article was wholly obtained or underwent substantial transformation. Assembly alone frequently does not confer origin.
  3. Identify the Chapter 99 heading. Scan the 9903.88.xx block for your HTS. This tells you whether you are on List 1, 2, 3, 4A, or 4B, and whether a 2024/2026 strategic-sector increase applies.
  4. Check for a valid exclusion. Exclusions are product-and-heading specific and time-limited. Most of the 352 exclusions extended through May 31, 2025 expired by late 2025; verify current status before claiming.
  5. Add the IEEPA layer. Apply the applicable IEEPA heading for Chinese origin (fentanyl plus reciprocal baseline), checking whether the product is 232-excluded.
  6. Add Section 232 if applicable. Steel, aluminum, copper, and derivative products.
  7. Add AD/CVD. Look up the Commerce Department case number for your scope. If your product is within scope and you are not an exporter with a separate rate, you may be paying the "all others" or adverse facts available rate.
  8. Apply the correct customs value. Duties are assessed on transaction value — generally the price actually paid or payable, plus certain assists, royalties, and packing, excluding international freight and insurance for most entries.
  9. Reconcile with a Post Summary Correction or protest. If you over-declared, you have 300 days from entry summary to file a PSC.

De Minimis Repeal: What Changed and Why It Matters

The $800 de minimis exemption under Section 321 is gone. It was eliminated for Chinese-origin shipments on May 2, 2025, and suspended globally on August 29, 2025. Every shipment — including a single $12 phone case shipped direct to a consumer — is now dutiable.

For postal shipments, CBP established a simplified flat-duty option (a per-item flat fee) as an alternative to formal entry, but the underlying principle holds: there is no longer a duty-free lane for small Chinese parcels. Sellers on direct-to-consumer marketplaces who built margins around Section 321 have had to reprice, consolidate into bulk entries, or shift to U.S. warehousing.

Exclusions in 2026: What Is Left

The exclusion picture has narrowed dramatically. The 352 exclusions that had been extended ran through May 31, 2025, and most lapsed by the end of 2025. In 2026, the practical reality is that a small, product-specific tail of exclusions remains — largely tied to machinery, certain chemicals, and solar manufacturing equipment — and everything else pays.

Origin, Transshipment, and Enforcement Risk

The gap between a 25% Section 301 rate and a 0% rate on a Vietnamese or Mexican origin product has created enormous incentive to misrepresent origin. CBP has responded with a marked increase in enforcement, using EAPA (Enforce and Protect Act) investigations, audit teams, and its trade fraud task forces.

Two points matter here. First, substantial transformation is the standard for non-FTA origin determinations. Simple assembly, packaging, or minor finishing does not confer origin. Moving a Chinese-produced permanent magnet into a Vietnamese housing and exporting it does not make the magnet Vietnamese. Second, penalties under 19 U.S.C. § 1592 can reach the domestic value of the merchandise for fraud, with negligence and gross negligence tiers carrying lower but still severe exposure — plus loss of import privileges.

For USMCA, the analysis is different: origin is determined by tariff shift and regional value content rules, not substantial transformation. A product assembled in Mexico from Chinese inputs may qualify for USMCA duty-free treatment — or may fail the rule of origin entirely. It depends on the specific product-specific rule, and it must be supported by a valid certification of origin.

Legal Mitigation: Drawback, FTZ, and Bonded Warehouses

There are legitimate tools, and they matter more in 2026 than they ever have.

  • Duty drawback: Section 301 duties are drawback-eligible. If you import Chinese components and export the finished product — or destroy it under CBP supervision — you can recover up to 99% of the Section 301 duty paid. Section 232 duties are generally not drawback-eligible, and IEEPA drawback treatment should be confirmed with CBP given evolving guidance.
  • Foreign Trade Zones: FTZ savings have narrowed considerably. Under current IEEPA implementation, goods must generally be admitted to a zone under privileged foreign status, which locks in the tariff treatment at admission and eliminates the "inverted tariff" benefit for many products. FTZs still provide value for cash-flow deferral and for products whose tariff treatment is favorable at admission.
  • Bonded warehouses: Useful for deferring duty until withdrawal, and strategically valuable if an exclusion or litigation outcome is pending. Duty is owed at the rate in effect on the date of withdrawal for consumption.
  • Tariff engineering: Legitimate redesign — changing a product's essential character or classification through genuine physical change — is legal. Paperwork that reclassifies without changing the goods is fraud.

Litigation, Refunds, and the IEEPA Question

The most consequential open question in 2026 is the legal fate of the IEEPA tariffs. The Supreme Court has taken up the challenge to the president's authority to impose tariffs under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, and the outcome could invalidate the fentanyl and reciprocal tariffs entirely.

Three things follow from that for importers:

  • Section 301 is a different statute. Even a sweeping ruling against IEEPA would not disturb the Section 301 China tariffs, because those rest on Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. Model your Section 301 exposure as durable.
  • Refunds would require action. If IEEPA tariffs are invalidated, recovery is likely to require protests or designated refund processes, not automatic payment. Importers should maintain entry-level records — entry numbers, dates, HTS codes, and duty amounts — for every IEEPA-covered entry.
  • Protests have a deadline. You have 180 days from liquidation to file a protest. Do not let this window close while waiting for a court decision if you believe a refund is due.

Outlook: What to Watch Through the Rest of 2026

USTR's statutory review process means the Section 301 architecture is continuously open for modification. Watch for new investigations into sectors not yet covered — legacy-node semiconductors, shipbuilding inputs, and logistics equipment have all been discussed — along with adjustments to the 2024 increase schedule.

China's retaliation posture is the second variable. Chinese export controls on gallium, germanium, graphite, and rare earths have directly shaped U.S. sourcing decisions and may trigger additional U.S. measures. Meanwhile, the November 10, 2026 expiration of the reciprocal tariff pause is a hard calendar deadline that every importer should have marked. If the 24% tier snaps back, the effective rate on Chinese goods could jump meaningfully overnight.

The practical posture for 2026: assume the current stack persists, build models that can flex, and keep your documentation audit-ready.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What are the current Section 301 rates on Chinese goods in 2026?

A: Base rates are 25% for Lists 1, 2, 3, and 4A tranches, and 7.5% for the remainder of List 4A. Strategic-sector products carry higher rates: 100% on electric vehicles and syringes/needles, 50% on solar cells, semiconductors, polysilicon, and wafers, and 25% on EV batteries, steel and aluminum products, ship-to-shore cranes, non-EV lithium-ion batteries, natural graphite, permanent magnets, and medical gloves (the last four effective January 1, 2026). List 4B remains suspended.

Q: Which new products are hit by Section 301 in 2026?

A: The January 1, 2026 step-up applies to non-EV lithium-ion batteries (HTS 8507.60.0020 and related), natural graphite (2504.10.50 and 2504.90.00), permanent magnets (8505.11.00), and medical gloves (4015.19.xx). Each moved to a 25% Section 301 rate. If your product was previously at 0% or 7.5% under these headings, your landed cost increased by 17.5 to 25 percentage points on January 1.

Q: Do Section 301 tariffs stack with IEEPA and Section 232 tariffs?

A: Yes, generally. Section 301, IEEPA, and AD/CVD duties stack on the same entry. The main exception is that products subject to Section 232 (steel, aluminum, copper and derivatives) are generally excluded from the IEEPA reciprocal tariff — but they still carry Section 301 and any AD/CVD orders. A Chinese aluminum extrusion can therefore face MFN duty, 25% Section 301, 50% Section 232, and AD/CVD simultaneously.

Q: Are Section 301 exclusions still available in 2026? How do I claim them?

A: The 352 extended exclusions ran through May 31, 2025 and most lapsed by late 2025. A narrow set of product-specific exclusions remains, primarily in machinery, chemicals, and solar manufacturing equipment. To claim one, cite both the otherwise-applicable 9903.88.xx heading and the specific exclusion subheading on your entry summary, and ensure your invoice description falls squarely within the exclusion scope. Verify current status in the HTSUS and the relevant Federal Register notice before filing.

Q: How do I calculate total landed cost for a Chinese import in 2026?

A: Classify to the 10-digit HTS level, verify country of origin by substantial transformation, find the applicable 9903.88.xx Section 301 heading, check for a valid exclusion, add the applicable IEEPA heading (~20% combined for China in early 2026), add any Section 232 rate, add AD/CVD at the applicable case rate, and apply all of it to the correct customs value (generally transaction value excluding international freight and insurance). Recheck every component each quarter — USTR modifies these headings by notice.

Q: Did the Supreme Court strike down IEEPA tariffs, and would that affect my Section 301 duties?

A: The IEEPA litigation has been the central tariff question of 2025–2026 and is subject to change, so confirm the current status before acting. The critical point for planning is that Section 301 rests on a completely different statute — Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 — so even a ruling invalidating IEEPA tariffs would not eliminate the China Section 301 duties. Any IEEPA refund would likely require you to file protests or participate in a designated refund process, which is why maintaining entry-level records matters now.

Q: Can I avoid Section 301 by moving final assembly to Vietnam or Mexico?

A: Not automatically. For Vietnam or any non-FTA country, the test is substantial transformation — simple assembly, packaging, or finishing does not confer origin, and CBP actively pursues transshipment through EAPA investigations and penalties under 19 U.S.C. § 1592. For Mexico, the test is the USMCA product-specific rule of origin, which requires a tariff shift and often a regional value content threshold. A genuine manufacturing relocation that meets the applicable test is legitimate; a re-labeling operation is not, and carries fraud exposure.

Q: How does de minimis repeal affect small shipments from China?

A: The $800 Section 321 de minimis exemption ended for Chinese-origin shipments on May 2, 2025, and was suspended globally on August 29, 2025. All shipments are now dutiable. Postal and express consignment shipments may use a simplified flat-duty option instead of formal entry, but there is no duty-free lane remaining. Direct-to-consumer sellers should model full stacked duty into pricing or consolidate into bulk entries.

Action Checklist for Importers in 2026

  1. Reclassify every Chinese-origin SKU to the 10-digit HTS level and map it to its 9903.88.xx heading.
  2. Flag the January 1, 2026 increase categories — batteries, graphite, magnets, gloves — and update pricing immediately.
  3. Confirm the origin of every non-China supplier with documentation sufficient to survive a CBP audit.
  4. Audit your AD/CVD scope exposure; scope rulings are the most common source of surprise liability.
  5. File drawback claims if you export or destroy imported Chinese goods — 99% recovery on Section 301 duties is real money.
  6. Record every IEEPA-covered entry with entry number, date, HTS, and duty paid, and calendar the 180-day protest deadline from liquidation.
  7. Mark November 10, 2026 — the expiration of the reciprocal tariff pause — as a budget-planning milestone.

Tariff rates, Chapter 99 subheadings, and exclusion status change frequently by executive action and Federal Register notice. Verify current rates against the HTSUS and CBP guidance, and consult a licensed customs broker or trade attorney for entry-specific advice.
