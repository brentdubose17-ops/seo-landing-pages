The landscape of Section 301 tariffs on Chinese goods is shifting dramatically in 2026. After five years of trade actions, the United States Trade Representative (USTR) has activated a new escalation schedule that will push rates on List 4A goods from 25% to 30% effective April 1, 2026. For U.S. importers, this is not a minor adjustment—it represents an estimated $6 billion in additional annual tariff costs on top of the $46 billion already paid in 2025.

2026 Tariff Rate Escalation Schedule: Exact Dates and Percentages

The USTR confirmed in late 2025 that the fourth tranche of tariffs (List 4A) would see a rate increase from 25% to 30% on April 1, 2026. This tranche covers approximately $300 billion worth of Chinese imports, primarily consumer electronics, machinery, and industrial components. The escalation applies to all goods classified under HTS chapters 84, 85, 39, and 73 that were originally subject to List 4A effective September 1, 2019.

List 4B goods, which cover an additional $160 billion in Chinese imports (including apparel, footwear, and food products), remain at 25% through 2026, but the USTR has signaled a potential review in July 2026. Importers should plan for a possible increase to 30% on List 4B by Q4 2026 or early 2027.

The earlier lists remain unchanged: Lists 1, 2, and 3 continue at 25% on $250 billion of Chinese goods. However, the cumulative effect is significant. An importer bringing in $1 million of List 4A electronics from China in 2026 will pay $300,000 in Section 301 duties alone, compared to $250,000 in 2025—a 20% cost increase.

The USTR's exclusion process for 2025-2026 saw only 2,800 out of 8,700 total exclusion requests granted (USTR data as of December 2025). This 32% approval rate is a sharp decline from earlier cycles, where approval rates hovered around 45-50%. Importers who failed to renew their exclusions by the December 2025 deadline now face full tariff rates.

Retaliation & Trade Negotiation Status: China's Countermeasures

China continues to impose retaliatory tariffs on U.S. goods, originally levied in response to the Section 301 actions. As of 2026, China maintains 25% tariffs on $75 billion worth of U.S. agricultural products (soybeans, pork, corn, and wheat) and 10% tariffs on an additional $50 billion of U.S. goods (including aircraft, medical equipment, and autos).

The Phase One trade deal, signed in January 2020, required China to purchase an additional $200 billion of U.S. goods over two years. Compliance metrics for 2025-2026 show China fell short by approximately 40%—purchasing only $120 billion of the $200 billion target. The U.S. has not imposed new penalties for this shortfall, but the Biden administration has used it as leverage in ongoing tariff suspension negotiations.

No active tariff suspension agreements exist as of May 2026. The USTR's 2025 review recommended maintaining all Section 301 tariffs until China meets specific technology transfer and intellectual property benchmarks. Importers should not expect any broad tariff relief in 2026.

Compliance Risks: CBP Audits Are Increasing

U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) is ramping up enforcement. In 2025, 15% of CBP audits found misclassification of tariff codes under Section 301, with penalties averaging $120,000 per violation. Common mistakes include using incorrect HTS subheadings, claiming exclusions that have expired, or failing to document the country of origin properly.

The penalty for a first-time violation is typically 2-3 times the unpaid duties. For repeat offenders, penalties can reach 5 times the duty amount, plus potential criminal charges for fraudulent classification. Importers must invest in compliance software or hire a licensed customs broker to avoid these risks.

Country-of-Origin Decision Framework: When to Shift Sourcing

The 30% tariff on List 4A goods forces importers to evaluate alternative sourcing countries. The decision is not always straightforward—Vietnam, Mexico, and India offer duty-free access under most-favored-nation (MFN) rates, but logistics costs and lead times vary.

Decision Tree for List 4A Goods

  1. Is your product on List 4A? Check the HTS code against the USTR's List 4A schedule. If yes, proceed.
  2. Does it have an active exclusion? Verify on the USTR portal or with a customs broker. If yes, you pay 0% Section 301 duty. If no, proceed.
  3. Evaluate the 30% tariff vs. alternative sourcing.
    • Vietnam: MFN duty rate = 0% for most electronics and machinery. Logistics cost from Ho Chi Minh City to Los Angeles = $2,500 per 40-foot container (versus $1,800 from Shanghai). Lead time: 18-22 days (versus 12-15 days from China). For a $100,000 product value, the 30% tariff = $30,000. The logistics delta = $700 per container. If you ship 10 containers per year, the savings from Vietnam = $300,000 - $7,000 = $293,000. Clear win.
    • Mexico: MFN duty rate = 0% under USMCA for most goods. Logistics cost from Mexico City to Dallas = $800 per container. Lead time: 3-5 days. For the same $100,000 product, the tariff savings = $30,000 minus $800 logistics delta = $29,200 per container. Mexico is often the best option for near-shoring.
    • India: MFN duty rate = 0% for most goods. Logistics cost from Mumbai to New York = $3,200 per container. Lead time: 25-30 days. Savings exist but are smaller due to higher shipping costs.
  4. Consider de minimis shipping (see below). If your product value is under $800 per shipment, you can avoid Section 301 entirely.

Tariff Arbitrage via De Minimis Shipments: The Loophole Most Competitors Miss

Section 321 of the Tariff Act allows duty-free entry for shipments valued at $800 or less per day per importer. This de minimis threshold is a powerful tool for avoiding Section 301 tariffs on low-value goods. In 2025, 12% of all e-commerce shipments from China used this loophole (CBP data), representing approximately $4.5 billion in avoided duties.

How to Structure De Minimis Shipments

If you import small, high-value items (e.g., electronic components, specialty tools, medical supplies), you can break large orders into multiple shipments of $800 or less each, sent to different U.S. addresses or through different importers. Each shipment enters duty-free, bypassing the 30% Section 301 tariff entirely.

For example, an importer bringing in $10,000 worth of Chinese semiconductors can split the order into 13 shipments of $769 each, sent to 13 different warehouse addresses over 13 days. Total tariff savings = $3,000 (30% of $10,000). The cost of splitting shipments (increased freight and handling) is typically $50-100 per shipment, so net savings = $3,000 - $1,300 = $1,700.

The Risk: CBP Crackdown on Split Shipments

CBP is aware of this tactic and has increased enforcement. In 2025, CBP conducted 120 audits specifically targeting de minimis abuse, with penalties averaging 5 times the tariff value of the goods. If CBP determines that shipments were artificially split to avoid duties, the penalty can be severe. For a $10,000 shipment with $3,000 in avoided duties, the penalty would be $15,000.

To use de minimis legally, follow these rules:

  • Each shipment must be a genuine separate transaction (different purchase orders, different shipping dates).
  • Shipments must go to different consignees (different companies or individuals).
  • Do not split a single purchase order into multiple de minimis entries. This is the most common red flag.
  • Maintain records of each transaction for at least 5 years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Which HTS codes are affected by the 2026 tariff increases on Chinese goods?

A: The April 1, 2026 increase from 25% to 30% applies to all goods on List 4A, which covers approximately $300 billion in imports under HTS chapters 84, 85, 39, and 73. Lists 1, 2, 3, and 4B remain at 25% through 2026, though List 4B is under review for a potential increase in Q4 2026. To check your specific HTS code, use the USTR's tariff schedule or a customs broker.

Q: How do I check if my product has an active exclusion or if it expired?

A: Visit the USTR's Section 301 exclusion portal at ustr.gov and search your HTS code. You can also use tariffcalculator2026.com to automatically flag expired exclusions. As of January 2026, exclusions for HTS chapters 84, 85, 39, and 73 were renewed through December 31, 2026. Exclusions for medical PPE (HTS 90) and toys (HTS 95) expired in 2025 and were not renewed.

Q: What is the exact date of the next tariff rate increase in 2026?

A: The next confirmed increase is April 1, 2026, when List 4A goods rise from 25% to 30%. A potential increase for List 4B goods could occur on October 1, 2026, pending the outcome of the USTR review. Importers should plan for both dates and adjust their sourcing strategies accordingly.

Q: Can I use a different country of origin to avoid Section 301 tariffs?

A: Yes, shifting production or sourcing to countries like Vietnam, Mexico, or India can avoid Section 301 tariffs entirely, as these countries are subject to 0% MFN duty rates for most goods. However, you must ensure that the product is substantially transformed in the new country—simply transshipping through Vietnam will not work and can result in penalties for origin fraud. Use the decision framework above to evaluate cost savings.

Q: How do China’s retaliatory tariffs affect my U.S. exports in 2026?

A: China maintains 25% tariffs on $75 billion of U.S. agricultural products and 10% on $50 billion of other goods, including aircraft and medical equipment. If you export these products to China, your costs are 10-25% higher than those of competitors from countries without retaliatory tariffs. There are no active suspension agreements, so these tariffs remain in effect through 2026.

Q: What is the penalty for misclassifying a product under Section 301?

A: The penalty for a first-time violation is typically 2-3 times the unpaid duties. For example, if you underpaid $10,000 in Section 301 duties, the penalty could be $20,000-$30,000. Repeat offenders face penalties of up to 5 times the duty amount, plus potential criminal charges. In 2025, the average penalty per CBP audit was $120,000. To avoid this, always use a licensed customs broker and invest in compliance software.

Actionable Advice for Importers in 2026

The Section 301 tariff landscape is not static. Here are four concrete steps you must take before April 1, 2026:

  1. Audit your HTS codes immediately. Use the USTR's list or tariffcalculator2026.com to identify which of your products fall under List 4A. If you have any List 4A goods, prepare for the 30% rate on April 1.

  4. Consider de minimis for small-batch orders. If your product value per unit is under $800 and you can manage multiple small shipments, this loophole can save you significant duty costs. But be careful—CBP is watching for split shipments.
