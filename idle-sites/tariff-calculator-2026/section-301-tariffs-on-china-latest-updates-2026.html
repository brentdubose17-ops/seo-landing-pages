2026 Tariff Rate Adjustments: The New Numbers You Must Know

The USTR finalized three major rate increases effective March 1, 2026. These adjustments affect all four original lists plus the newly expanded List 4B. Total tariff revenue from Section 301 reached $78.2 billion in FY2025, and the Congressional Budget Office projects $85 billion in FY2026—a 8.7% year-over-year increase driven entirely by these rate hikes.

List 1 and List 2: Modest Increases on Strategic Goods

List 1 (covering $34 billion of Chinese imports, primarily industrial machinery and medical devices) rose from 25% to 30% on January 15, 2026. List 2 ($16 billion of semiconductors, printed circuits, and optical media) moved from 25% to 32%. These increases target goods the USTR identifies as critical to U.S. supply chain resilience.

List 3: The Largest Single Jump

List 3, covering $370 billion of goods including electronics, furniture, and auto parts, increased from 25% to 30% effective March 1, 2026. This is the most significant rate change by volume. Importers of HTS codes 8471 (computing machinery) and 9403 (furniture) now face a combined tariff burden of 30% Section 301 plus 3.5% standard duty, totaling 33.5% on most shipments.

List 4A and 4B: New Rates on Consumer Goods

List 4A ($300 billion of consumer products including toys, footwear, and apparel) now carries a 10% Section 301 rate, up from 7.5% in 2025. List 4B—a new category added in November 2025—imposes a 15% tariff on $180 billion of additional goods, primarily textiles, plastic articles, and iron products. The effective date for List 4B is April 1, 2026.

Solar Panel Tariffs: A Special Case

Solar panels face a unique rate structure. The Section 301 tariff on solar cells and modules rose to 50% in 2026, up from 30% in 2024. Combined with the Section 201 safeguard tariff of 15%, solar panel imports from China now carry a total duty burden of 65%—the highest rate of any product category.

Product Exclusions and the Renewal Process: A Shrinking Window

The USTR’s exclusion program has tightened significantly. In 2025, the approval rate for new exclusion applications was 12%. In 2026, that rate dropped to 8%—the lowest since the program began. As of May 2026, only 150 HTS codes remain on the active exclusion list, down from 220 in December 2025.

Which Products Still Have Exclusions?

The remaining exclusions focus on medical equipment (HTS 9018, 9022), certain electronics components (HTS 8542, 8541), and industrial machinery parts (HTS 8479). Forty exclusion codes expired in January 2026, covering items like disposable gloves, bicycle parts, and specific plastic articles. Importers of those goods must now pay the full tariff rate retroactively if they filed for extension after the deadline.

How to Apply for an Exclusion in 2026

The application process remains digital through the USTR portal, but the requirements have become more stringent. You must demonstrate that the product is not available from a non-Chinese source at a competitive price, and you must provide documentation of at least three alternative suppliers. The USTR expects to receive 2,000 new exclusion applications in Q1 2026, but only an estimated 160 will be approved.

Trade Volume Collapse

Customs Compliance and Enforcement: CBP’s 2026 Crackdown

U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) has intensified enforcement in 2026, with penalty rates rising to levels that demand serious attention. The cost of non-compliance now far exceeds the cost of proper classification.

Penalty Structure

For misclassification of goods under Section 301, CBP imposes a penalty of 10% of the goods’ value. False origin claims—declaring a product as originating from Vietnam when it is actually Chinese—carry a 15% penalty. In cases of intentional fraud, penalties can reach 20% of the goods’ value plus criminal referral. CBP conducted 4,200 audits in FY2025, and that number is projected to reach 5,800 in FY2026.

Strategic Alternatives for Importers: Beyond Paying Tariffs

Paying the full tariff rate is rarely the most cost-effective option. In 2026, three strategies offer significant savings: duty drawback claims, exemption applications, and supply chain rerouting.

Duty Drawback: Claiming Refunds on Re-Exported Goods

The duty drawback program allows importers to recover up to 99% of tariffs paid on goods that are subsequently exported from the United States. In FY2025, only $1.2 billion of the $78.2 billion in Section 301 tariffs was claimed back through drawback—meaning billions in refunds went unclaimed. The process requires meticulous documentation, including proof of export within five years of import.

For example, an importer of Chinese auto parts valued at $10 million with a 30% Section 301 tariff would pay $3 million in duties. If those parts are incorporated into a finished vehicle exported to Canada, the importer can claim back $2.97 million through duty drawback—a 99% recovery rate.

Section 301 Exemption Applications

While the approval rate is low at 8%, the payoff justifies the effort for high-value goods. Products valued above $50,000 per shipment with an exclusion approval probability above 10% are worth pursuing. If overturned, importers could claim refunds on all tariffs paid since 2022 on those lists. The total refund pool would exceed $200 billion. However, the USTR is expected to immediately appeal to the Supreme Court, creating a multi-year uncertainty window.
