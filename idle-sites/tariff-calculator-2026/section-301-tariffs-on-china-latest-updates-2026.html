Section 301 Tariffs on China: The Complete 2026 Rate Stack, Exclusions & Duty Recovery Playbook

Section 301 tariffs on China remain fully in effect in 2026, with statutory review modifications confirmed through the 2024-2025 cycle. The average effective duty on Chinese imports now ranges from 45% to 65% depending on product category, thanks to the triple-stack of Section 301 (25-100%), IEEPA fentanyl tariffs (20%), and Section 232 metals duties (25-50%). With $150 billion+ in Section 301 duties collected from U.S. importers since 2018, and an estimated $2-5 billion in unclaimed refunds available through duty drawback and protest filings, the 2026 playbook is about both compliance and cash recovery. Since de minimis exemption was eliminated for China-origin goods, even e-commerce shipments under $800 now face full tariff stacks.

The 2026 Landscape: What Has Actually Changed

The four-year statutory review of Section 301 tariffs, initiated by the USTR in May 2024, concluded with the confirmation and modification of tariffs across roughly $370 billion in Chinese imports. The review did not eliminate the tariffs — it refined them, increased rates on strategic sectors, and set the stage for the next statutory review cycle beginning January 2026.

This is not a policy question anymore. Tariffs are a locked-in cost of importing from China. The 2026 question is how to calculate the true landed cost, which exemptions still apply, and how to reclaim overpaid duties.

According to the U.S. Congressional Budget Office, the average effective tariff rate on Chinese goods was approximately 3% before 2018, rising to 19.3% by 2023 — and now projecting to 45-65% across most categories by 2026 after the IEEPA stacking went live in March 2025.

The "Triple Stack" Trap: Why Basic Tariff Calculators Give You the Wrong Number

Most articles and most importers treat Section 301 as a standalone duty. That is a costly mistake in 2026. Here is the reality: your product's total tariff is the sum of three distinct tariff layers, each with its own legal basis, customs collection mechanism, and potential mitigation strategy.

Layer 1: Section 301 (Trade Remedy)

Basis: Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 — retaliation for unfair trade practices. Rates range from 7.5% to 100% depending on the tranche and sector. This is the core layer most people understand.

Layer 2: IEEPA Fentanyl Tariffs (National Security)

Basis: International Emergency Economic Powers Act — imposed in February and March 2025 for fentanyl trafficking. As of March 4, 2025, a 20% tariff applies to all Chinese-origin goods, regardless of HTS code. This stacks on top of Section 301 with no exceptions.

Layer 3: Section 232 (National Security — Metals & Derivatives)

Basis: Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962. Steel and aluminum — plus roughly 55 new derivative subheadings added in the August 2025 expansion — carry between 25% and 50% tariffs. If your product uses steel or aluminum components, this layer stacks with the others.

Do these layers replace each other? No. Each is assessed separately, and all three stack cumulatively. MFN (Most-Favored-Nation) base rates apply as well. The total effective duty is:

Total Effective Rate = MFN Base + Section 301 + IEEPA (20%) + Section 232 (if applicable)

Worked Example: The Battery Pack vs. The Steel Fastener

Let's put real numbers behind this. We build two scenarios: a lithium-ion battery pack and an industrial steel fastener, both imported from China into the United States.

Scenario A — Lithium-Ion Battery Pack (HTS 8507.60):

  • MFN Base: 3.4%
  • Section 301 (Tranche 1, May 2024 increase): 25%
  • IEEPA: 20%
  • Section 232: N/A (batteries not covered)
  • Total: 48.4% — not 25% as the basic calculator said

Scenario B — Industrial Steel Fastener (HTS 7318.15):

  • MFN Base: 0% (steel fasteners have zero base duty)
  • Section 301 (Tranche 1): 25%
  • IEEPA: 20%
  • Section 232 (steel derivative): 25%
  • Total: 70% — a 70-point tariff stack on a single product

When you type "fastener" into a standard tariff finder, you get 25%. The actual cash outlay at the border is 70% of the shipment value. That 45-percentage-point delta is the difference between a profitable import and a catastrophic loss — and it is exactly why CBP is collecting far more per entry than importer expectations.

The May 2024 USTR action and the subsequent Section 232 expansion in August 2025 added significant new tariff exposure across several high-importance sectors. If you import any of these, your rate is not the same as it was in 2023:

  • Electric vehicles — from 25% to 100% (Section 301) + 20% IEEPA = 120%+ effective
  • Lithium-ion batteries (for EVs) — from 7.5% to 25% Section 301, plus 20% IEEPA = 45%+
  • Semiconductors — from 25% to 50% Section 301, plus 20% IEEPA = 70%+
  • Solar cells (whether assembled into modules or not) — from 25% to 50%, plus 20% IEEPA = 70%+

CBP also issued circumvention findings in 2025 targeting transshipment of solar panels, lithium-ion batteries, and steel products through Vietnam, Malaysia, and Thailand. If CBP determines your shipment attempts to avoid Section 301 via third-country routing, you face retroactive duties, penalties, and potential False Claims Act exposure.

The Exclusion Maze: What Still Works in 2026

The exclusion process remains the single most effective way to reduce Section 301 exposure. But it is now far narrower than in 2018-2020. As of early 2025, only roughly 4,000 HTS lines retained active exclusions out of the original ~6,300 approved in earlier rounds.

USTR received more than 4,600 reinstatement requests during the 2024 process, and the approval rate fell below 20%. If you are applying in 2026, here is what to expect.

Two Paths: Existing Exclusions and New Applications

Path 1 — Check if your HTS code has a valid exclusion. USTR publishes the full list in the Federal Register and on its dedicated exclusion portal. Many importers fail because they assume their code is covered when it was not, or their exclusion expired and was not extended.

Path 2 — File a new exclusion request during the open public comment periods. The 2026 statutory review (beginning January 2026) will open Federal Register notices with 60-day comment windows in Q1 and Q2. This is your window. You must submit product-specific requests via the USTR portal, including HTS codes, product descriptions, importer information, and a justification for why exclusion is necessary.

USTR evaluating requests weighs three factors: whether the tariff causes severe economic harm to U.S. interests, whether the product is available from U.S. or third-country sources, and whether the product is strategically important to China's industrial policy.

Reinstatement Backlog in 2026

USTR still lists hundreds of pending reinstatement requests from the 2024-2025 cycle. Do not rely on a pending request to protect you at the border — CBP requires payment of duties at entry even if an exclusion request is under review. File a protest (CBP Form 19) within 180 days of liquidation if your exclusion is retroactively approved.

The De Minimis Cliff: E-Commerce Just Got Expensive

Most tariff guides completely miss the de minimis change. The $800 de minimis exemption (Section 321) was eliminated for China-origin goods under the 2025 tariff rules. Every package imported from China — even a $15 phone case — now faces the full Section 301 + IEEPA tariff stack.

This creates an enormous compliance cliff for e-commerce sellers. Platforms like Shopify, Amazon FBA, and Etsy sellers who source from China and ship directly to U.S. consumers are now legally responsible for duties that CBP previously did not assess. With the average effective rate at 45-65%, a $30 product now incurs $13.50-$19.50 in tariffs.

CBP has deployed new automated entry systems for low-value shipments, and major carriers (UPS, FedEx, DHL) are collecting duties at delivery. Businesses that fail to register for a CBP importer bond and file formal entries face customs penalties and shipping holds on their products.

The Duty Recovery Playbook: $2-5 Billion in Unclaimed Refunds

Here is the angle most competitors ignore: the money is not just going out, some of it can come back. An estimated $2-5 billion in unclaimed refunds sits in Section 301 duties paid between 2018 and 2025. That cash is recoverable through two primary tools.

Tool 1: Duty Drawback (19 USC 1313)

If you imported goods, paid Section 301 duties, and then re-exported them OR used them in manufacturing goods that were exported, you may be eligible for a refund of 99% of the duties paid. The claim window is five years from the date of import.

This is not obscure law — it is a statutory right that importers routinely fail to exercise. The filing is done via CBP Form 7551, and the refund processing time typically runs 3-6 months. With Section 301 duties alone exceeding $25 billion per year in 2022-2024, the refund pool is enormous.

Tool 2: Protests and Erroneous Liquidation Challenges

If you believe CBP assessed the wrong rate on your entry, you have 180 days from liquidation to file a protest (CBP Form 19). Common grounds include claiming a HTS misclassification, improper stacking of Section 232 on non-covered products, or inapplicability of IEEPA to certain product lines. Many importers have discovered that their freight forwarder overpaid due to an incorrect HTS code assignment — a fixable error that creates immediate refunds.

In FY2023, CBP reported more than $800 million in protested refunds paid across all duty classifications. Your slice of that is real but requires proactive action.

Doing the Retroactive Audit

The single most profitable action an importer can take in 2026 is a retroactive customs audit. Work backward through your 2018-2025 entries:

  1. Pull your ISF (Importer Security Filing) and CBP entry documents (CBP Form 7501).
  2. Check the HTS codes assigned by your broker for accuracy.
  3. Identify whether Section 232 was applied to products not actually covered by the derivative list.
  4. Determine if any of those imported goods were subsequently re-exported (eligible for drawback).
  5. File drawback claims within the 5-year window and protests within the 180-day liquidation window.

Steps to get your number right:

  1. Confirm your HTS code at the 10-digit level (8-digit Schedule B classification is not sufficient).
  2. Check the Section 301 tranche list for your code, plus any sector-based rate increases from 2024.
  3. Add 20% IEEPA automatically for all Chinese-origin goods.
  4. Verify whether your product falls under Section 232 coverage — including the 55 new derivative subheadings added August 2025.
  5. Add your MFN base rate (typically 0-5% for raw materials, 15-40% for consumer goods).
  6. Run the calculation, then re-run it with a sensitivity analysis at ±5 percentage points.

Country-of-Origin Rerouting: The Risk That Does Not Go Away

Even if you legally source from Vietnam or Malaysia, you need to prove substantial transformation in that country. CBP's 2024 circumvention findings on solar cells and batteries were triggered when importers shipped Chinese components to third countries for minimal processing (e.g., just assembly of pre-manufactured parts) before export to the U.S. CBP assesses combined duties on the entire shipment plus penalties up to 100% of the value.

To qualify as a valid origin change, the transformation must alter the HTS subheading, and the cost or value added in the third country is typically expected to exceed a set threshold. Current industry benchmarks suggest at least 25-30% value added is a safer margin against CBP scrutiny.

This is not just a compliance issue — it is also a duty recovery issue. If CBP liquidates your entry with the stacked tariff after you assumed a lower rate, you have 180 days to protest. The burden of proof is on you to demonstrate the third-country transformation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Are Section 301 tariffs on China still in effect in 2026, and what's the actual rate for my product's HTS code?

A: Yes, Section 301 tariffs remain in full effect, with modifications confirmed through the 2024-2025 statutory review. Actual rates vary by HTS code and tranche group (7.5% to 100%), but the total effective rate after stacking IEEPA (20%) and any Section 232 layers lands between 45% and 65% for most categories. Use the current USTR tariff lists in combination with your 10-digit HTS code to get the precise number.

Q: What's the difference between Section 301, Section 232, and IEEPA tariffs — do they stack or replace each other?

A: They stack. They do not replace each other. Section 301 (Trade Act of 1974) addresses unfair trade practices; Section 232 (Trade Expansion Act of 1962) covers steel and aluminum for national security; IEEPA (20% since March 2025) responds to fentanyl trafficking. All three are assessed separately, and MFN base duties still apply. A steel fastener from China now carries a 70% stack (0% MFN + 25% Section 301 + 20% IEEPA + 25% Section 232).

Q: Can I get a Section 301 exclusion in 2026, and how long does the process take?

A: Yes, but approval rates are below 20%, and only ~4,000 HTS lines have active exclusions as of early 2025. The timeline from application to decision typically runs 4-6 months after the comment window closes. The next window opens in Q1-Q2 2026 with 60-day comment periods. Submit a detailed, product-specific justification through the USTR portal, including alternative-source analysis and economic harm evidence.

Q: How do I calculate landed cost with 301 tariffs using a 2026 tariff calculator — what's the effective rate formula?

A: The formula is: Total Effective Rate = MFN Base + Section 301 + 20% IEEPA + Section 232 (if applicable). Multiply that rate by your (product value + freight + insurance) to get the tariff amount, then add broker fees, bond fees, harbor maintenance fees, and merchandise processing fees. For example, a lithium-ion battery pack (MFN 3.4% + Section 301 25% + IEEPA 20%) totals 48.4% before fees.

Q: Does importing through Vietnam, Mexico, or Malaysia avoid Section 301 tariffs, or is there circumvention risk?

A: It can avoid them only if the third country performs substantial transformation (HTS subheading change and minimum 25-30% value-added). CBP issued circumvention findings in 2024-2025 on solar, batteries, and steel transshipped via Vietnam, Malaysia, and Thailand — those importers faced retroactive duties plus penalties. Mexico via USMCA can be zero-duty, but the origin certification burden is significant.

Q: What are the duty drawback rules for Section 301 tariffs if I re-export the goods or use them in manufacturing?

A: Under 19 USC 1313, you can recover 99% of Section 301 duties paid if the imported goods are re-exported within 5 years of import, or if they are used in manufacturing and the finished product is exported. File via CBP Form 7551, and processing typically takes 3-6 months. This is the most overlooked cash-recovery tool in the tariff landscape.

Q: Which products got new or increased Section 301 tariffs between 2024-2026, and what sunset dates apply?

A: The May 2024 action increased rates on EVs (25%→100%), semiconductors (25%→50%), solar cells (25%→50%), lithium-ion batteries (7.5%→25%), and syringes/needles (to 100%). The August 2025 Section 232 expansion added 55 new steel and aluminum derivative HTS subheadings. There are no sunset dates — tariffs remain until the next statutory review cycle (beginning January 2026) modifies or revokes them.
