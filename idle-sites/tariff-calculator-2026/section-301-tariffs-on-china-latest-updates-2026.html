Section 301 Tariffs on China in 2026: The Complete Rate Stack, Review Outcomes, and Compliance Playbook

If you imported goods from China in 2025, you already know the drill: your landed costs have been whipsawed by a cascade of tariff actions that show no signs of slowing in 2026. The Section 301 tariffs, originally imposed between 2018 and 2019 under the Trump administration and largely retained under the Biden and current administrations, have evolved into a complex, multi-layered system that now touches over $380 billion in annual Chinese imports.

As of May 2026, the statutory four-year review of these tariffs has concluded, and the results are significant. The USTR issued its final determination in February 2026, and while the headline rates on most tranches remain intact, there are critical changes to exclusions, enforcement, and the de minimis regime that directly impact your bottom line. This guide breaks down the current rate structure, the 2026 review outcomes, and the specific strategies—including duty drawback and CBP protests—that can save your business real money this year.

The 2026 Statutory Four-Year Review: What Changed and What Didn't

The Trade Act of 1974 requires USTR to conduct a mandatory four-year review of all Section 301 tariffs. For the tariffs imposed in 2018–2019, that review window opened in October 2025, with a 60-day public comment period that closed in December 2025. The final determination, released on February 14, 2026, kept the overall structure intact but made several targeted adjustments.

Here is the key outcome: No broad tariff repeal occurred. The 25% tariffs on Lists 1, 2, and 3 (covering approximately $250 billion of goods) remain in effect. The 7.5% tariffs on List 4A (approximately $130 billion) also remain. However, USTR did announce a formal product exclusion process for 2026–2027, which opened on March 15, 2026, and will accept applications through June 30, 2026. This is a narrower window than the 2025 cycle, so timing is critical.

More importantly, the review confirmed the escalated rates on strategic sectors that took effect in late 2024 and early 2025. The 100% tariffs on electric vehicles (EVs), solar cells, and certain critical minerals remain locked in. The 50% tariffs on semiconductors, steel, aluminum, and medical supplies (syringes, needles, and PPE) also remain. There were no reductions in these tranches, despite heavy lobbying from US manufacturers who rely on Chinese inputs for these components.

China's response was immediate. In March 2026, Beijing announced retaliatory tariffs on an additional $25 billion of US goods, bringing the total value of US exports subject to Chinese counter-tariffs to approximately $175 billion. These range from 10% to 25% on agricultural products, autos, and energy exports. The escalation has effectively ended any near-term prospect of a "Phase One" style truce, despite ongoing technical talks in Geneva that stalled in April 2026.

The 2026 Rate Stack: Why You're Paying 200%+ on Some Goods

The single biggest mistake importers make is analyzing Section 301 in isolation. In 2026, the effective duty rate on Chinese goods is a stacked combination of four separate tariff regimes, plus antidumping and countervailing duties (AD/CVD) where applicable. Ignoring this stack leads to massive under-estimation of landed costs and missed optimization opportunities.

Here's the current stacking reality as of May 2026:

  • MFN (Most Favored Nation) rates: The baseline duty, typically 2.5% to 25% depending on the product. For electronics, it's often around 2.5–5%. For textiles, it can be 15–25%.
  • Section 301 tariffs: 7.5%, 25%, 50%, or 100% depending on the HTS code and list.
  • IEEPA fentanyl tariffs: A flat 20% tariff on all Chinese-origin goods, imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act in 2025. This applies to everything from China, with no exceptions, and is not subject to the exclusion process.
  • Section 232 tariffs: 25% on steel and aluminum, and now 50% on certain downstream steel and aluminum products as of January 2026.

Let's model a concrete example: a semiconductor component with an MFN rate of 5%. In 2024, the total duty was 5% + 25% (301 List 3) = 30%. In 2026, it's 5% + 50% (301 semiconductor tranche) + 20% (IEEPA) = 75%. For a specific type of machinery with a 10% MFN rate, a 25% 301 rate, and the 20% IEEPA rate, the total is 55%. But when you add AD/CVD duties on top—which can range from 10% to 200% for products like aluminum extrusions or wooden cabinets—the total landed cost can exceed 200%.

CBP data from FY2025 confirms the scale: importers paid $48 billion in Section 301 duties alone, up from $32 billion in 2023. When you add the IEEPA fentanyl tariffs, the total additional duty burden on Chinese imports exceeded $80 billion in 2025. The average effective tariff rate on all Chinese goods is now between 41% and 45%, up from 19.9% in 2024. This is not a marginal increase—it's a structural shift in the economics of sourcing from China.

The De Minimis Elimination: E-Commerce Enforcement in 2026

If you're an e-commerce importer or a small business shipping goods from China via postal or express carriers, the de minimis elimination is the single most impactful regulatory change of the past two years. The $800 threshold (Section 321) exemption that allowed over 1 billion packages to enter duty-free annually was eliminated for China and Hong Kong on May 2, 2025. In 2026, enforcement has ramped up significantly.

As of January 2026, CBP now requires full electronic data transmission for all China-origin packages, including the buyer's identity, seller's identity, and a valid HTS classification. The postal integration system, which was in beta testing throughout 2025, is now fully operational. What this means in practice:

  • Every package from China is now subject to duty assessment, with no $800 floor.
  • Fines for non-compliance have reached up to $50,000 per violation for importers who fail to provide accurate data.
  • Customs brokers are now mandatory for all commercial shipments, even small ones.
  • Processing delays at major ports have increased from 2–3 days to 7–10 days, as CBP staff manually reviews a flood of new declarations.

One of the most actionable strategies in 2026 is to re-evaluate your Incoterms and delivery models. Many small e-commerce sellers are shifting to a "split shipment" model—sending goods to a US-based fulfillment center in bulk (which allows for proper duty payment and compliance) and then shipping domestically to end customers. This avoids the per-package customs headache and reduces the risk of fines.

The 2026 Exclusion Process: A Narrow Window of Opportunity

The reinstatement cycle for Section 301 exclusions has been chaotic. In September 2024, USTR reinstated exclusions for 352 products that had previously been covered. That reinstatement expired on May 31, 2025. The 2025–2026 cycle saw approximately 14,000 new exclusion requests filed, but the approval rate was a paltry 8–12%.

Now, the new 2026 exclusion window (March 15 – June 30, 2026) is open. This is your chance to get relief on products that were previously denied or expired. The key criteria USTR uses for approval are:

  1. Availability: Is the product available from sources outside China? If yes, exclusion is less likely.
  2. Strategic importance: Does the product support US manufacturing or critical infrastructure?
  3. Economic harm: Can the importer demonstrate that the tariff causes disproportionate economic harm to their US operations?

Here's the critical insight most importers miss: you don't need to wait for USTR's decision to claim a refund. If you file an exclusion request and it is ultimately approved, you can claim a refund for duties paid on that product going back to the date the exclusion request was filed. But the more powerful tactic is the CBP protest under 19 USC 1514.

If USTR removes a tariff on a product, or if a court ruling invalidates a tariff (which has happened in several cases), you have 180 days from the date of liquidation to file a protest with CBP. A protest is a formal legal claim that the duty was assessed incorrectly. The 2026 review created exactly this scenario for a handful of products that had their tariffs reduced from 25% to 7.5%. If you imported those products in the 180 days before the reduction, you can file a protest and receive a retroactive refund. This is not a hypothetical—it's a concrete, actionable money-saving tactic that most competitors fail to mention.

The "Duty Stacking Trap" and How to Escape It

The phrase "duty stacking trap" describes the situation where an importer focuses only on the Section 301 rate and ignores the IEEPA, Section 232, and AD/CVD layers. This is dangerous because it leads to incorrect pricing, missed optimization opportunities, and in some cases, penalties for underpayment.

Consider a real-world example: A US manufacturer of aluminum furniture imports extruded aluminum components from China. The MFN rate is 5%. The Section 232 rate on aluminum is 25%. The Section 301 rate on the product is 25%. The IEEPA rate is 20%. The total is 75% before AD/CVD. But aluminum extrusions from China are also subject to an AD/CVD order with duties ranging from 15% to 30%. The fully loaded duty rate is 90–105%.

Autos show the same trap in real time: a China-built Lincoln Nautilus carries a stacked 52.5% duty (25% Section 301 + 25% Section 232 + 2.5% MFN) — a level Ford has said drove its decision to move Lincoln production back to the US beginning in 2030. Under 19 USC 1313, you can recover up to 99% of duties paid on imported goods that are subsequently exported, either as finished products or as components of products that are exported. This is not a loophole—it's a statutory right that many importers underutilize. In 2025, CBP paid out $1.1 billion in duty drawback claims, but the total eligible amount was estimated at over $3 billion. The 2026 rate stack makes this even more valuable, as the 20% IEEPA tariff is also eligible for drawback.

The "China Plus One" Cost Inversion: When It Makes Sense to Leave

For years, the conventional wisdom was that "tariffs are just a tax on importers" and that China's manufacturing cost advantage was so large that it absorbed the tariff hit. That narrative has flipped in 2026. For a growing list of products, the fully loaded cost of importing from China—including the stacked tariffs, ocean freight (which has risen 30% year-over-year), and longer lead times—now exceeds the cost of sourcing from Vietnam, Mexico, or even domestic US production.

Let's quantify this inversion with a real example. Consider a mid-range consumer electronics device with a factory cost of $100 in China. The landed cost breakdown in 2026:

  • Factory cost: $100
  • Ocean freight: $8 (up from $4 in 2023)
  • MFN duty (2.5%): $2.50
  • Section 301 (25%): $25
  • IEEPA (20%): $20
  • Total duty: $47.50
  • Customs brokerage and compliance: $3
  • Total landed cost: $158.50

Now consider the same product made in Vietnam. Factory cost is slightly higher at $110 (labor costs are 10–15% higher, but component sourcing is similar). Vietnam has a free trade agreement with the US, so the MFN duty is often 0% or very low. There's no Section 301 or IEEPA tariff. Ocean freight is similar at $8. Total landed cost: $118. The savings of $40.50 per unit—a 25% reduction—is more than enough to offset the transition costs.

This is the "cost inversion" moment. For products where China's factory cost advantage is less than 25%, it now makes financial sense to shift sourcing. The tipping point, according to a 2025 MIT study, is when the combined tariff rate exceeds 30%. With the 2026 stack pushing most products to 45% or higher, the math works in favor of diversification for the majority of consumer goods.

However, there are exceptions. Products requiring highly specialized supply chains—such as rare earth magnets, advanced semiconductors, and certain pharmaceutical intermediates—still have no viable alternative outside China. For these, the tariff is simply a cost of doing business, and the focus should be on duty drawback, FTZs, and the exclusion process.

Timing is everything in tariff compliance. Missing a deadline by even one day can cost you thousands in unnecessary duties. Here are the critical dates for the remainder of 2026:

  • July 15, 2026: USTR will issue interim decisions on first batch of exclusion requests; retroactive refunds begin for approved products.
  • September 30, 2026: End of Q3 CBP enforcement reporting period; expect increased scrutiny on de minimis compliance.
  • October 1, 2026: China's expanded retaliation tariffs on US goods take effect (additional $25 billion).
  • December 31, 2026: End of the 2026 duty drawback filing window for goods exported in 2025 (must file within 3 years of export, so this is a rolling deadline).

For the CBP protest strategy, the 180-day window is calculated from the date of liquidation, not the date of import. You must check your CBP entry summaries to find the liquidation date for each entry. If you have entries that liquidated between August 2025 and February 2026 for products that had tariffs reduced in the February 2026 review, you have until approximately August 2026 to file a protest. This is a narrow, high-value opportunity.

Actionable Compliance Checklist for Q2–Q4 2026

Here is a step-by-step checklist to protect your margins and claim every dollar of relief available:

  1. Audit your HTS codes: Use the CBP's CSMS system to verify that your classifications are correct. An incorrect HTS code that understates duty is a violation, but one that overstates it is a missed savings opportunity.
  2. File for exclusions now: The June 30 deadline is non-negotiable. Prioritize products with high import volumes and evidence of non-China sourcing difficulty.
  3. Check your liquidation dates: Identify any entries that liquidated in the last 180 days. If the tariff rate on those products has changed due to the February 2026 review, file a protest immediately.
  4. Set up a duty drawback program: Even if you don't currently export, if you manufacture products in the US using Chinese inputs and export the finished goods, you are eligible. The setup cost is typically $5,000–$15,000, and the payback is often less than six months.
  5. Evaluate FTZ status: If you import high-value goods and re-export a portion, an FTZ can defer duties indefinitely. The application process takes 3–6 months, so starting now positions you for 2027.
  6. Re-negotiate Incoterms with Chinese suppliers: In 2026, many Chinese suppliers are willing to absorb a portion of the IEEPA tariff to maintain market share. Push for DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) terms to shift the compliance burden, or at minimum, request a 5–10% price reduction to offset the new costs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Will Section 301 tariffs on China be removed or reduced in 2026?

A: Based on the February 2026 four-year review determination, no broad removal occurred. The 25% and 7.5% rates on Lists 1–4A remain, and the 50–100% rates on strategic sectors are locked in. However, the new exclusion process (open until June 30, 2026) provides a pathway for product-specific relief. The IEEPA fentanyl tariff at 20% is separate and has no exclusion mechanism.

Q: How do I know if my product is on List 1, 2, 3, or 4?

A: You need to identify your product's 10-digit HTS code. The USTR maintains the official list on its website, and CBP's Automated Commercial Environment (ACE) system will flag entries subject to Section 301. If you're unsure, a licensed customs broker can verify the classification and applicable list for a nominal fee (typically $100–$300 per product).

Q: What is the process to apply for a Section 301 exclusion, and how long does it take?

A: The current 2026–2027 exclusion window is open until June 30, 2026. You must file through the USTR online portal with product-specific information, including HTS code, product description, and a justification for why the product cannot be sourced outside China. USTR aims to issue initial decisions within 120 days, but in practice, decisions have taken 4–6 months. If approved, the exclusion is retroactive to the date of your request, allowing for refunds on duties paid since that date.

Q: How does the de minimis elimination affect my small e-commerce shipments from China?

A: As of May 2, 2025, the $800 de minimis exemption no longer applies to China or Hong Kong-origin goods. Every package is now subject to full duty assessment, including the 20% IEEPA tariff. You must provide electronic data to CBP before arrival, and non-compliance can result in fines up to $50,000 per violation. Most small importers are shifting to bulk shipping to a US warehouse to manage compliance.

Q: Are there retroactive refunds if a tariff is repealed mid-2026?

A: Yes, but you must act within 180 days of the liquidation date of your entry. If USTR or a court reduces a tariff, you can file a protest under 19 USC 1514 to claim a refund for the difference. The February 2026 review created this opportunity for a small set of products. Check your CBP entry summaries for liquidation dates and file promptly.

Q: How do Section 301 tariffs stack with Section 232 and IEEPA fentanyl tariffs?

A: They stack cumulatively. For example, a product with a 5% MFN rate, a 25% Section 301 rate, a 25% Section 232 rate (if steel/aluminum), and the 20% IEEPA rate faces a total duty of 75%—before any AD/CVD. Drones and UAS components now carry their own Section 232 layer too — up to 100% effective September 3, 2026 (see the Section 232 drone tariff explainer). And the August 13 "Great Transshipment Scam" report names 40+ countries accused of routing China-origin goods around US tariffs — see our China transshipment crackdown explainer for the country list and country-of-origin compliance steps. The IEEPA tariff applies to all Chinese goods, so it's a fixed 20% on top of everything else. You must model all layers to determine true landed cost.
