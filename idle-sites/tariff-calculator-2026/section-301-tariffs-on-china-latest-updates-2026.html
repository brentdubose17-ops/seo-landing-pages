Section 301 Tariffs on China in 2026: The Complete Status Report, Rate Schedule, and Compliance Playbook

If you are importing goods from China in 2026, you are operating in a radically different trade environment than the one that existed just two years ago. The Section 301 tariffs—originally imposed in 2018 under the Trump administration and largely retained by the Biden administration—have undergone significant modifications, legal challenges, and enforcement changes that directly impact your landed costs. As of May 2026, these tariffs are very much in effect, with rates ranging from 7.5% to 100% depending on your product category. This guide breaks down the current rate structure, the legal status of each tariff tranche, the pending four-year review, and—most critically—the practical steps you need to take to calculate and minimize your duty exposure this year.

Executive Summary: The State of Section 301 in May 2026

Section 301 tariffs remain a permanent fixture of U.S.-China trade relations. The U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) has not only maintained the original four lists but has also expanded tariffs on strategic sectors like electric vehicles (EVs), semiconductors, and solar cells. The cumulative tariff revenue collected since 2018 has surpassed $143 billion through fiscal year 2025, with 2026 projected collections estimated at $32–35 billion according to U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) data.

The share of U.S. imports coming from China has dropped from 21.4% in 2017 to approximately 13.5% in 2025—a decline of nearly 8 percentage points. However, this does not mean trade has stopped. It means the trade that continues is happening under a complex web of tariffs, exclusions, and enforcement actions that require careful navigation.

The Complete 2026 Section 301 Tariff Rate Schedule

Understanding the current rate tiers is essential for any import calculation. As of January 2026, the tariff structure has been consolidated into four primary rate tiers, with specific product categories carrying higher rates based on the May 2024 modifications and subsequent 2025 adjustments.

List 1, 2, and 3: The 25% Baseline

Lists 1, 2, and 3—covering approximately $250 billion of Chinese imports—remain at a flat 25% tariff. These lists cover a wide range of products including industrial machinery, electrical equipment, furniture, auto parts, and consumer goods. There have been no reductions to these rates in 2026, and none are expected before the four-year review concludes.

List 4A: The 7.5% Tier

List 4A, which covers roughly $120 billion of imports including cell phones, laptops, and certain consumer electronics, retains the 7.5% tariff rate that was established in the January 2020 Phase One trade deal. Despite industry lobbying to eliminate this tariff entirely, the USTR has kept it in place throughout 2025 and into 2026.

The 50% and 100% Tiers: Strategic Sector Tariffs

The May 2024 modifications, which took full effect by January 2025, established elevated rates for products deemed strategically important:

  • 100% tariffs: Electric vehicles (HS 8703.80), certain semiconductors (HS 8541.51), solar cells (HS 8541.43), syringes and needles (HS 9018.31)
  • 50% tariffs: Certain steel and aluminum products (HS 7204, 7601), medical gloves (HS 4015.12)
  • 25% tariffs: Lithium-ion batteries (HS 8507.60), rare earth magnets (HS 8505.11), natural graphite (HS 2504.10)

These elevated rates have had a measurable impact. According to CBP import data, EV imports from China fell by 97% in the first year after the 100% tariff took effect, while solar cell imports dropped by 64%.

Mid-2026 Adjustments

As of May 2026, the USTR has not announced any new rate increases. However, the agency is actively considering extending the 100% tariff to additional semiconductor manufacturing equipment categories. A decision is expected in Q3 2026.

Legal and Administrative Status: What Is Actually Enforceable?

The legal landscape surrounding Section 301 tariffs has been turbulent. Multiple lawsuits have challenged the tariffs' legality, with mixed results. The U.S. Court of International Trade (CIT) issued rulings in 2023 and 2024 that briefly invalidated certain tranches of List 4A and List 3 tariffs, but those rulings were stayed pending appeal. As of May 2026, the tariffs remain fully enforceable.

Active Litigation

The most significant pending case is In re Section 301 Cases, consolidated at the Federal Circuit. In early 2026, the court heard arguments on whether the USTR exceeded its authority under Section 301 by modifying tariffs without a new investigation. A decision is expected by late 2026. Industry analysts largely expect the court to uphold the tariffs, given the broad deference historically afforded to executive branch trade actions.

Exclusion Notices and Extensions

The USTR has continued its exclusion process, though with a notably lower approval rate. In 2025, the agency received 8,400 exclusion applications and approved approximately 1,512—an approval rate of 18% per USTR quarterly reports. This is down from the 32% approval rate seen during the 2019–2020 exclusion wave.

Notably, the USTR has issued blanket exclusions for certain medical supplies and rare earth elements used in defense applications. These exclusions, initially set to expire in March 2026, were extended through December 2026 in a notice published on February 14, 2026.

The Four-Year Review: What Happens Next?

Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 requires a mandatory four-year review of tariff actions. The review for the 2018 tariffs was initiated in May 2024, and the statutory deadline for completing the review was May 2025. However, the USTR has not yet announced its final recommendations as of May 2026.

Here is the timeline you need to know:

  • May 2024: USTR initiated the four-year review, soliciting public comments
  • October 2024: USTR held public hearings; over 1,100 stakeholders testified
  • January 2025: USTR submitted interim recommendations to Congress (not publicly released)
  • June 2025: USTR announced it would extend the review period by one year
  • Q4 2026: Final recommendations expected

What does this mean for you? The tariffs are not going away in 2026. Even if the review concludes with recommendations to reduce rates, any changes would likely be phased in over 12–24 months. The most likely outcome, according to trade policy analysts at the Peterson Institute, is a retention of tariffs on strategic sectors (EVs, semiconductors, batteries) with possible modest reductions on consumer goods in Lists 1–3.

Real-World Impact on U.S. Industries and Importers

The macroeconomic effects of Section 301 tariffs are substantial and well-documented. But the micro-level impact on individual importers is where the real story lies.

Import Volume and Cost Pass-Through

U.S. importers have absorbed 80–90% of the tariff costs rather than passing them on to consumers, according to a 2025 NBER working paper (#31245). This has compressed profit margins significantly. The same study found that consumer prices for affected goods rose only 0.3–0.5% as a direct result of the tariffs, meaning most of the burden fell on businesses.

Import volumes from China have shifted dramatically. Using Census Bureau data:

  • Electronics: Down 34% from 2018 peak, with significant sourcing moved to Vietnam and Malaysia
  • Furniture: Down 41%, with major shifts to Vietnam and Mexico
  • Machinery: Down 22%, largely retained in China due to supply chain complexity
  • Chemicals: Down 15%, moderate shifts to India and South Korea
  • Consumer Goods: Down 38%, heavily diverted to Southeast Asia

Trade Diversion: Where Is the Business Going?

Since 2020, Vietnam has increased its share of U.S. imports from Asia by 18%, Mexico by 12%, and India by 6%. However, a significant portion of this "diversion" is actually transshipment—Chinese goods being sent to third countries for final assembly or minimal processing to avoid tariffs. CBP has increased enforcement against such practices, with 1,200+ anti-circumvention investigations opened in 2025.

The De Minimis Loophole Closure: A Game-Changer for Small Importers

The most significant—and most underreported—development in 2025–2026 is the partial closure of the Section 321 de minimis loophole. Previously, packages valued under $800 entered the U.S. duty-free without formal entry. This created a massive channel for Chinese e-commerce platforms like Shein and Temu to ship goods directly to U.S. consumers, bypassing Section 301 tariffs entirely.

Effective May 2025, CBP began requiring Section 301 tariffs to be applied to certain low-value packages from China. In 2026, the enforcement has expanded significantly. Key changes include:

  • Data requirements: Importers must now provide the 10-digit HS code and country of origin for all de minimis shipments from China
  • Rate application: The Section 301 rate for the specific HS code is now assessed on these packages
  • Proposed $200 threshold: A legislative proposal to lower the de minimis threshold to $200 is pending in Congress, with a vote expected in late 2026

For direct-to-consumer (DTC) brands and small importers, this is a massive shift. If you are importing goods from China via e-commerce fulfillment, you are now subject to Section 301 duties on packages that previously entered duty-free. A $50 shipment of electronics components now incurs a $12.50 Section 301 tariff (at the 25% rate), plus any applicable processing fees.

Tariff Engineering: Legal Strategies to Reduce Your Duty Exposure

While outright evasion is illegal and carries severe penalties, there are legal strategies to minimize your Section 301 exposure. The most effective is proper HS code classification and country-of-origin analysis under CBP's "substantial transformation" doctrine.

The Substantial Transformation Doctrine

Under CBP regulations, a product's country of origin is the country where it underwent its last "substantial transformation." If you are importing a product that is assembled in a third country from Chinese components, the country of origin may not be China—even if a significant portion of the components originate there.

For example, consider a company importing a power tool that is assembled in Vietnam from Chinese-made motors and Vietnamese-made housings. If the assembly process in Vietnam results in a new and different product with a different name, character, and use, CBP may determine the country of origin is Vietnam, making the product subject to Vietnam's tariff rates (generally 0–8%) rather than China's Section 301 rates.

However, CBP has become increasingly aggressive in cracking down on "minor processing" that does not constitute substantial transformation. A 2025 CBP ruling (H307921) determined that simply assembling a product from Chinese components in Vietnam without significant value-added processing does not change the country of origin. To qualify, the processing must be substantial—typically requiring at least 35–40% value-added in the third country.

HS Code Reclassification

Another legal strategy is ensuring your product is classified under the most accurate—and favorable—HS code. Many importers default to a broad classification that carries a higher Section 301 rate. A detailed product analysis may reveal a more specific classification with a lower rate.

For example, a company importing a multi-functional device might classify it as "machinery" (25% Section 301 rate) when it could be more accurately classified as "electrical apparatus" (7.5% Section 301 rate). The key is that the classification must be defensible under the Harmonized Tariff Schedule rules of interpretation. Aggressive misclassification is illegal and can result in penalties of up to 200% of the duties owed plus potential criminal prosecution.

Duty Stacking: How Section 301 Interacts with Other Tariffs

One of the most confusing aspects of tariff compliance is understanding how Section 301 duties stack with other tariff actions. For many products, you will owe multiple duties simultaneously.

Section 301 + Section 232

Section 232 tariffs (national security-based) apply to steel and aluminum products. These are additive to Section 301 duties. For example, a Chinese steel fastener is subject to:

  • Base MFN duty rate: 0–3%
  • Section 232 tariff: 25%
  • Section 301 tariff: 25%

The total tariff burden is approximately 50–53%, not including antidumping and countervailing duties (AD/CVD) that may also apply.

Section 301 + AD/CVD

Antidumping and countervailing duties are assessed in addition to Section 301 tariffs. There is no offset. For products like certain steel fasteners, aluminum extrusions, and chemicals, the combined duty rate can exceed 100%.

The 2026 Exclusion Application Playbook

Despite the low approval rate, applying for a product exclusion can still be worthwhile—especially if your product falls into a historically favored category. Here is the month-by-month calendar for 2026:

  • January–March: Gather documentation. You need import data, supply chain information, and evidence of alternative sourcing attempts.
  • April 1: USTR opens the quarterly exclusion application window (winter cycle).
  • April–June: Submit your application. The USTR accepts applications on a rolling basis during the window.
  • July–September: USTR review period. Agency consultations with Commerce and Defense occur during this time.
  • October 1: USTR publishes decisions for the winter cycle.
  • October 15: Next application window opens (fall cycle).

Which product categories have historically won exclusions? Based on USTR data from 2024–2025, the highest approval rates were in:

  • Medical devices: 34% approval rate
  • Rare earth elements: 41% approval rate
  • Semiconductors not available from U.S. sources: 29% approval rate
  • Industrial chemicals with no domestic substitute: 22% approval rate

Conversely, consumer goods, furniture, and electronics had approval rates below 10%.

Penalties for Non-Compliance: What You Risk

Misclassifying goods to avoid Section 301 duties is a federal offense with serious consequences. Under 19 U.S.C. § 1592, penalties can include:

  • Civil penalties: Up to 200% of the duties owed for negligent violations
  • Fraud penalties: Up to 200% of the duties owed plus potential criminal prosecution
  • Seizure: CBP can seize goods that are misclassified
  • Revocation: Your Importer Security Filing (ISF) bond can be revoked, halting all imports

In 2025, CBP assessed $412 million in penalties related to Section 301 tariff evasion, a 45% increase from 2023. The agency has also increased its use of DNA testing and X-ray scanning to detect misclassified goods.
