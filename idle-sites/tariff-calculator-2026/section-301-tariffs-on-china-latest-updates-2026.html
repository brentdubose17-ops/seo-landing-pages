What Are Section 301 Tariffs in 2026?

Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 allows the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) to impose tariffs on countries engaging in unfair trade practices. Since 2018, the U.S. has levied tariffs on Chinese imports across four lists: List 1, List 2, List 3, and List 4 (split into 4A and 4B). As of 2026, these tariffs cover over $370 billion in Chinese goods annually, affecting HTS codes from 8400 to 9900.

The 2026 adjustments are the largest single-year increase since the initial tariffs. The USTR cited China’s failure to meet Phase One trade deal commitments and continued intellectual property theft as justifications. The result is a significant cost increase for importers, particularly in electronics, machinery, and textiles.

Key 2026 Rate Changes at a Glance

  • List 3: Increased from 25% to 30% effective January 1, 2026. Covers machinery, electrical equipment, and vehicles (HTS 8400–8700).
  • List 4A: Increased from 7.5% to 15% effective January 1, 2026. Covers consumer electronics, furniture, and toys (HTS 9400–9500).
  • List 1 and List 2: Remain at 25% and 25% respectively, unchanged in 2026.
  • List 4B: Remains at 7.5%, no increase in 2026 but subject to future review.

These increases are additive to any other tariffs, such as anti-dumping duties or Section 232 steel tariffs. For example, a Chinese steel product already subject to a 25% Section 232 tariff now faces an additional 30% Section 301 tariff—a total of 55%.

Product Exclusions: 345 Expirations on March 15, 2026

Product exclusions have been a lifeline for importers since 2018. These temporary waivers allow specific goods to enter duty-free under Section 301. However, the USTR announced that 345 product exclusions will expire on March 15, 2026. This affects categories including medical devices, industrial machinery, and electronic components.

Only 12% of these exclusions are expected to be renewed, according to CBP estimates. The USTR has signaled a stricter renewal process, requiring importers to prove that no U.S. alternative exists and that the goods are critical for domestic manufacturing. If your product is on the exclusion list, you must file a renewal application by March 1, 2026, to avoid paying the full 2026 rate.

How to Apply for Exclusion Renewals

The process remains similar to previous years but with tighter deadlines. You must submit a request through the USTR’s online portal, including:

  • Product description and HTS code.
  • Proof of domestic unavailability (e.g., supplier letters, market research).
  • Import volume and value data for the last 12 months.
  • A statement on how the tariff would harm your business or U.S. consumers.

Approval takes 30–60 days. If your exclusion expires on March 15, you will owe the 2026 rate starting March 16 unless you have a renewal confirmation.

Retaliatory Tariffs: China’s Response in 2026

China did not remain passive. In February 2026, Beijing imposed retaliatory tariffs on U.S. exports:

  • 15% tariff on U.S. agricultural goods: Soybeans, pork, corn, and wheat. This affects $25 billion in U.S. exports.
  • 10% tariff on U.S. autos: Passenger vehicles and parts, covering $12 billion in exports.

These tariffs are additive to existing retaliatory measures from 2025. U.S. farmers have seen soybean exports to China drop by 18% year-over-year, while auto manufacturers face a 22% decline in sales volume. If you export to China, these costs must be factored into your pricing strategy.

The De Minimis Loophole Closure: The Biggest Underreported Change

Most articles focus on rate hikes, but the April 1, 2026 de minimis rule change is arguably more disruptive. Previously, shipments under $800 could enter the U.S. duty-free under Section 321. This loophole allowed 2.3 million Chinese parcels per day—mostly from e-commerce platforms like Shein, Temu, and Alibaba—to avoid all tariffs.

Effective April 1, 2026, the de minimis exemption is eliminated for all Chinese goods. Every shipment, regardless of value, must now pay Section 301 tariffs plus any applicable duties. CBP estimates that 78% of e-commerce shipments from China will lose duty-free status, adding 15–30% to the cost of each order.

Impact on Small E-Commerce Sellers

For direct-to-consumer (DTC) brands using Chinese suppliers, this is a seismic shift. A $50 product that previously entered duty-free now faces a 15% tariff (if under List 4A) plus a $5 processing fee. That’s an extra $12.50 per order. For a seller doing 1,000 orders per month, the annual cost increase is $150,000.

CBP has also mandated new documentation for all shipments over $800, including commercial invoices, country of origin certificates, and HTS classifications. Failure to comply results in shipment holds and fines of up to $10,000 per violation.

Customs Enforcement Changes in 2026

CBP has intensified enforcement. In addition to the de minimis rule, the agency is using AI-based risk scoring to flag high-tariff shipments. Key changes include:

  • Mandatory advance data: All shipments must submit commercial invoice data 24 hours before arrival. Failure results in a $5,000 penalty.
  • Increased audits: CBP is auditing 15% of all Chinese imports, up from 5% in 2025. Audit targets include misclassified HTS codes and undervalued shipments.
  • Bond requirements: Importers must post a continuous bond covering 10% of annual duties, up from 5% previously.

These changes increase compliance costs by an estimated 6–8% for most importers. Work with a licensed customs broker to ensure your documentation is accurate.

Common Questions About Section 301 Tariffs in 2026

Q: What are the exact Section 301 tariff rates in 2026?

A: List 3 is 30% (up from 25%), List 4A is 15% (up from 7.5%), and Lists 1, 2, and 4B remain at 25%, 25%, and 7.5% respectively. Rates are effective January 1, 2026, for goods entered on or after that date.

Q: Which Chinese goods are exempt from 2026 tariffs?

A: Only goods with active product exclusions are exempt. As of May 2026, 345 exclusions expired on March 15, and only 12% were renewed. Check the USTR exclusion list for your specific HTS code. Most electronics, machinery, and medical devices are now fully tariffed.

Q: How do I calculate landed cost with the 2026 rate changes?

A: Use this formula: Landed cost = (Product cost + freight + insurance) × (1 + tariff rate). For List 4A goods, the tariff rate is 15%. Add any de minimis fees if the shipment is under $800—now all Chinese parcels are subject to tariffs.

Q: Are tariff exclusions still available, and how to apply?

A: Yes, but only for products not covered by the 345 expirations. Submit a request to the USTR with proof of domestic unavailability and import data. The process takes 30–60 days. If your exclusion expired, you must reapply as a new request.

Q: What is the impact on my supply chain lead time?

A: Expect a 5–10 day increase due to CBP’s new advance data requirements and audits. If you shift sourcing to Vietnam or India, lead times increase by 15–30 days. Plan inventory accordingly.

Q: How do retaliatory tariffs affect my U.S. export costs to China?

A: If you export agricultural goods, expect a 15% cost increase. Auto exports face a 10% tariff. These are additive to existing tariffs. Factor these into your export pricing or explore alternative markets like Southeast Asia.

Q: What customs documentation is required for de minimis shipments over $800?

A: Since April 1, 2026, all Chinese shipments—regardless of value—require a commercial invoice, country of origin certificate, and HTS classification. Shipments over $800 also require a bond and advance data submission. Use a customs broker to avoid delays.
