Section 301 Tariffs on China in 2026: The Complete Update

As of January 2026, Section 301 tariffs on China remain fully in effect, with new 25% rates on non-EV lithium-ion batteries, critical minerals, and medical supplies taking effect on January 1, 2026. The total effective duty on a typical Chinese import now stacks MFN, 301, 232, and IEEPA fentanyl tariffs, often exceeding 50% — a $8,000 battery pack now carries $4,272 in duty versus $872 in 2023, a 390% increase. Are Section 301 Tariffs Still in Effect in 2026?

Yes. The May 14, 2024 Certificate of Modification retained all existing Section 301 tariffs and introduced a multi-year escalation ladder. There is no sunset clause; the next statutory review is scheduled for May 2028. According to USTR’s official notification, the four-year review concluded that the tariffs remain necessary to counter China’s trade practices—no reduction or rollback was granted. As of 2026, every HTS code that was subject to Section 301 prior to the 2024 modification remains in force, with some rates now higher than in 2024.

The 2026 step-up is the story most media outlets missed. When the May 2024 announcement hit, coverage treated it as a one-time event. In reality, the phased schedules under the Certificate of Modification meant that additional duty spikes would occur in 2025 (semiconductors) and 2026 (non-EV lithium-ion batteries, critical minerals, and medical supplies). For importers using a 2024-era tariff calculator, the numbers are now significantly understated.

The 2026 Phase-In Schedule: What Changed on January 1, 2026 vs. December 31, 2025?

Three major HTS clusters saw rate increases on January 1, 2026, per the 2024 Certificate of Modification:

  • Non-EV lithium-ion batteries (HTS 8507.60.00): The duty jumped from 7.5% to 25%. This applies to batteries used in consumer electronics, power tools, and stationary storage — not those for electric vehicles, which were already at 100% (for complete EVs) or 25% (for EV battery packs) since May 2024.
  • Critical minerals (HTS 2804–2812, certain 25.01/26.01): The final tranche of the 25% tariff (previously split as 7.5% and 15%) fully phased in, reaching 25% for all covered rare-earth elements, lithium carbonate, and certain processed ores.
  • Medical supplies (HTS 9018.31, 9018.32, PPE): Syringes, needles, and respirators moved from 0–7.5% to the full 25% rate. This was the last step of a two-tranche schedule.

Additionally, the semiconductor tariff that had jumped from 25% to 50% effective January 1, 2025, has now been in force for a full year. And the EV tariff remains at 100% — no changes in 2026.

Tariff Stacking: Why the 301 Rate Alone Understates Your Real Duty

If you’re still quoting “25%” for Chinese electronics, you’re under-budgeting by at least 2×. The true landed cost in 2026 requires sequential application of four layers:

  1. MFN base rate (0–25% depending on HTS)
  2. Section 301 (7.5% to 100% as per the table above)
  3. Section 232 on steel, aluminum, and copper (25% for steel, 50% for aluminum as of March 2025)
  4. IEEPA fentanyl tariff — a flat 25% on all Chinese imports since March 4, 2025 (incremented from 10% + 10% + 5%)

Additionally, anti-dumping and countervailing duties (AD/CVD) may apply to specific products (e.g., aluminum foil, wooden cabinets, certain chemicals). These must be layered on top.

Most tariff calculators found online stop at Step 2. That’s why we’re seeing real-world under-quotes of 1.5 to 2 times. Let’s walk through a concrete example.

Example: Chinese Lithium-Ion Non-EV Battery Pack (HTS 8507.60.00)

In 2023, a typical $8,000 battery pack imported from China had a total duty of 10.9% — that was MFN 3.4% + 301 7.5% (no IEEPA yet). Duty = $872. In 2026:

  • MFN: 3.4%
  • 301: 25% (since Jan 1, 2026)
  • IEEPA: 25%
  • Total effective tax rate: 53.4%

Duty = $4,272. That’s a +390% increase. If you’re buying 1,000 packs annually, the incremental duty is $3.4 million — enough to fund a reshoring pilot.

The IEEPA Fentanyl Tariff: The Hidden 25% Stack

The IEEPA (International Emergency Economic Powers Act) tariff was layered on China imports in February–April 2025. It started at 10% on February 4, 2025, increased to 20% on March 4, 2025, and then to 25% on April 9, 2025. As of 2026, this unchanged 25% remains in force, though it is subject to presidential action at any time. In practice, it applies to every Chinese origin product unless explicitly excluded (e.g., certain medical supplies, energy commodities, and items already covered by Section 232).

Why is this so often ignored? Mainstream tariff news focuses on the Section 301 “trade war” narratives, treating IEEPA as a separate political tool. But for your bottom line, it’s a flat 25% on every invoice. A $10,000 shipment now carries $2,500 in IEEPA duty alone. If your current calculator doesn’t include it, you’re underestimating your landed cost by 25% across the board.

Section 301 Exclusions in 2026: What’s Still Available and How to File

Importers often ask: “Can I still file for an exclusion?” The answer is complicated. The general Section 301 exclusion portal closed on December 31, 2020. Congress reauthorized a limited exclusion process in September 2022, but that program covered only 352 specific products and expired on September 30, 2023. Since then, USTR has not opened a new general portal.

However, a few narrow windows exist in 2026:

  • Medical/PPE exclusions: Certain pandemic-era exclusions were extended into 2026 through Federal Register notices. Check the current list on USTR’s site.
  • Critical minerals: A specific exclusion process for critical minerals was opened in late 2025 (per the Certificate of Modification’s requirement for review). You must file through the USTR portal keyed to the specific HTS and product description.
  • Machinery used in U.S. manufacturing: A few product-specific exclusions for industrial machinery have been reissued, but they are rare and product-specific.

Important: An “exclusion request” does not equal an exclusion granted. Of the ~12,000 requests filed across all cycles, USTR granted only ~2,200 (18%). In the 352-reauthorization cycle, the majority were denied. The typical submission-to-decision timeline in prior cycles was 90–150 days. So if you’re planning to use an exclusion to justify continued Chinese sourcing, build in that delay and the ~18% success probability.

Our recommendation: assume no exclusion will be granted for your product. Factor the full stacked rate into your landed cost. Only invest in filing if you’re in a strategic sector (medical, critical minerals, machinery) and you have a strong case for “no domestic alternative.”

The De Minimis Cliff and Retroactive Liability: 2024–2025 Backlog Exposure

2026 is the year retrospective duty exposure hits the books. Let’s recap: The $800 de minimis exemption was eliminated for China-origin goods on May 2, 2025. Then, on July 2, 2025, CBP issued a retroactive enforcement rule requiring formal entry for all Chinese-origin packages that had cleared under de minimis between May 2 and July 2, 2025. CBP also began reconciling 2024 and 2025 shipments that were misdeclared under the exemption.

The volume is staggering. In 2024 alone, 1.36 billion de minimis packages entered the U.S. under the $800 threshold, totaling roughly $49 billion in value. That’s an enormous pool of potential retroactive duty liability. CBP has already reported collecting over $40 million in assessed duties from retroactive reconciliation in the second half of 2025 — and that’s just the beginning.

If you’re an e-commerce importer using consolidation warehouses or a direct-to-consumer shipper, you may have unknowingly accumulated thousands of small packages that now require formal entry and duty payment. CBP can issue demand letters for back taxes plus liquidated damages penalties, which can be 2–10 times the duty owed. The 2026 action item: review your 2024–2025 shipping manifests, identify any packages that fell within the May 2–July 2, 2025 window, and proactively calculate and pay the duty to avoid penalties.

The Substantial Transformation Trap: Why Assembling in Vietnam or Mexico Doesn’t Save You

You can’t simply reroute your goods through a third country to dodge Section 301. CBP applies a “substantial transformation” test to determine country of origin. If the final product doesn’t undergo a substantial transformation — meaning it isn’t changed into a new and different article of commerce with a different name, character, or use — then the origin remains China, and the 301 tariff applies.

Since 2021, CBP has issued roughly 40 country-of-origin rulings on intermediate goods like battery packs, solar wafers, steel screws, and semiconductor assemblies. In nearly every case, CBP ruled that simple assembly or combining Chinese components does NOT constitute substantial transformation. For example, battery pack assembly from Chinese cells was ruled of Chinese origin — even when the pack was assembled in Mexico. Similarly, final module integration for solar panels was held to be Chinese origin when the cells were Chinese.

The implication for 2026: If you’ve been tempted to shift final assembly to Vietnam or Malaysia without changing the core manufacturing process, you’re still on the hook for full 301 + IEEPA duties. Worse, CBP has expanded its oversight with Regional Value Content (RVC) verification. If you can’t prove that a meaningful percentage of the value was added in the third country, you may face retroactive claims, duty differentials, and penalties.

What actually passes the test? U.S. courts have held that when a product’s essential character is established in a third country — e.g., when Chinese raw materials are transformed into a completely new product with a unique function — then origin changes. But that requires real manufacturing, not just assembly.

FAQ: Section 301 Tariffs on China in 2026

Q: Are Section 301 tariffs on China still in effect in 2026?

A: Yes. The May 2024 statutory review confirmed continuation, and no sunset exists. The next formal review is due by May 2028. All tariffs remain in force, with some rates increased as of January 1, 2026.

Q: What is the total tariff I pay on Chinese goods in 2026 — is it just the 301 percentage?

A: No. You pay a stack: MFN base rate + Section 301 + Section 232 (for steel/aluminum/copper) + the 25% IEEPA fentanyl tariff. For a typical non-EV lithium battery, the total in 2026 is 53.4% (MFN 3.4% + 301 25% + IEEPA 25%). Most published calculators stop at 301 and understate your landed cost by 1.5–2×.

Q: Can I still file a Section 301 exclusion request in 2026? Where is the portal?

A: The general portal closed on December 31, 2020. The COVID-era 352-exclusion program expired September 30, 2023. In 2026, only limited sector-specific windows exist (medical, critical minerals, and certain machinery) — each announced via Federal Register notices. Submission-to-decision timelines historically ran 90–150 days, with an overall approval rate of about 18%.

Q: If my goods are assembled in Vietnam or Mexico with Chinese parts, do I pay 301 tariffs?

A: Only if the final product undergoes “substantial transformation” — a legal test that it becomes a new article with a different name, character, or use. CBP rulings since 2021 have consistently held that simple assembly, final integration, or packing does not qualify. For example, battery packs made from Chinese cells remain Chinese origin even if assembled in Mexico. You must prove transformation with documented manufacturing processes.

Q: What changed on January 1, 2026 vs. December 31, 2025?

A: Three 301 rate increases took effect: non-EV lithium-ion batteries (8507.60.00) jumped from 7.5% to 25%; critical minerals reached the full 25% tranche; and medical supplies (syringes, needles, PPE) moved to 25%. Also, the semiconductor 50% rate is now fully a year old, and CBP began full enforcement audits for retroactive de minimis compliance.

Q: How does the de minimis elimination affect my 2025–2026 shipments, and do I owe retroactive duty?

A: Yes, if your packages cleared under the $800 exemption between May 2 and July 2, 2025, CBP may issue demand letters for back taxes plus penalties.
