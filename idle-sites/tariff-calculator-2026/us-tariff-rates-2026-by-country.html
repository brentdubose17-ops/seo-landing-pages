US Tariff Rates 2026 by Country: The Complete Importer’s Playbook

If you are sourcing goods internationally in 2026, you are navigating the most volatile tariff landscape in modern American history. The era of predictable Most-Favored-Nation (MFN) rates is over. Today, importers must manage a complex web of stacked duties—Section 301, Section 232, anti-dumping orders, and the new reciprocal tariff framework—that can turn a "cheap" product from Vietnam or China into a costly compliance headache.

This guide provides a country-by-country breakdown of effective US tariff rates for 2026, explains how to calculate your true landed cost, and highlights the operational traps that will catch most importers this year. We are using data from CBP filings, the USTR, and the ITC as of May 2026.

The 2026 Tariff Landscape: A New Baseline

Before diving into specific countries, you must understand the three layers of duties that apply to most imports. Layer one is the standard MFN rate, which is the base tariff applied to all WTO members. Layer two consists of "additional duties" under Section 301 (targeting China) and Section 232 (targeting steel, aluminum, and derivatives). Layer three is the new "Reciprocal Tariff Framework" implemented in early 2026, which aims to match US rates to the tariffs other countries impose on American exports.

The fiscal impact is staggering. In FY2024, US Customs collected roughly $80 billion in tariffs. For FY2026, the CBP projects collections of $90–$120 billion, driven by the expanded Section 301 scope and new reciprocal levies. This is not a minor tax adjustment; it is a fundamental restructuring of global supply chains.

How the Reciprocal Tariff Framework Works

The 2026 reciprocal tariff framework is not a flat rate. Instead, the USTR calculated a "tariff gap" for each country—the difference between what the US charges its imports and what that country charges US exports. For countries with a gap exceeding 5 percentage points, the US imposes an additional duty equal to half the gap, capped at 20 percentage points. This is why Vietnam, India, and Thailand face significant new levies, while Japan and South Korea, which have relatively low tariffs on US goods, face minimal changes.

Critical Note: The reciprocal tariff is additive. It stacks on top of MFN and any existing Section 232 duties. It does not replace them.

China: The 30%+ Reality

China remains the highest-tariff major trading partner. The average US tariff on Chinese goods was 19.9% in 2025. In 2026, that average has climbed to approximately 27.4%, driven by two factors. First, the Section 301 List 4A expansion added tariffs on an additional $300 billion of Chinese imports, mostly consumer goods and machinery. Second, the 2026 semiconductor tariff imposes a 50% duty on Chinese-made chips (HS 8542), which has a cascading effect on electronics supply chains.

Consider a specific example: a Chinese-made electric motor (HS 8501.31) with an MFN rate of 3.5%. Under Section 301, it faces an additional 25%. Because the motor contains a steel shaft, it also triggers the Section 232 derivative tariff of 25% (232 applies to steel-containing products, not just raw steel). The total stacked rate is 53.5%—not 25% or 3.5%, but all three added together.

Chinese EVs remain at a 100% tariff, and that rate is expected to hold through 2026 and beyond. The Biden-era policy has been retained and expanded by the current administration, making China an unviable source for any EV or EV component.

Canada and Mexico: The USMCA Cliff

For Canada and Mexico, the story is binary. Goods that qualify under the USMCA receive a 0% tariff on virtually all products. Non-qualifying goods face an average MFN rate of 3.5%, plus potential Section 232 exposure on steel and aluminum. The critical 2026 development is the expiration of Canada's Section 232 exemption for steel and aluminum. As of April 2026, Canadian steel imports face the full 25% tariff unless the goods are processed and certified as USMCA-originating under the new "steel melt and pour" rules.

Mexico, however, has retained its exemption for now, but the US has threatened to revoke it if Mexico does not curb Chinese transshipment. In Q1 2026, CBP seized $1.2 billion in goods at the Mexican border that were falsely labeled as Mexican-origin but were actually Chinese. This is a warning shot—if you are routing Chinese goods through Mexico to avoid tariffs, you are now operating in high-risk territory.

Vietnam and India: The Reciprocal Tariff Targets

Vietnam is the poster child for the reciprocal tariff framework. Its average MFN rate is a low 2.5%, but Vietnam imposes an average tariff of 18% on US exports. The tariff gap is 15.5 percentage points, resulting in a 15% reciprocal tariff on Vietnamese goods entering the US. This has hit apparel and footwear hard. A pair of Vietnamese-made sneakers (HS 6404.11) that previously entered at 2.5% now faces a 17.5% combined rate. For a $100 pair of shoes, that is $17.50 in duty—a significant margin hit for brands that shifted production from China to Vietnam in 2023-2025.

India is in a similar position. Its average tariff on US goods is 13.2%, creating a gap of 9.8 points, leading to a 10% reciprocal tariff. However, the US and India are actively negotiating a limited trade deal that could reduce this to 5% on select goods like medical devices and agricultural equipment. If you import from India, watch for the final deal terms expected in Q3 2026.

EU, Japan, and South Korea: Fewer Changes

European imports face minimal new tariffs. The EU's average tariff on US goods is 3.3%, nearly identical to the US MFN rate, so the reciprocal tariff is zero. However, the EU is not exempt from Section 232. European steel and aluminum face the same 25% steel / 10% aluminum duties as other countries. Additionally, in a retaliatory move over the EU's Digital Services Tax, the US has imposed a 25% tariff on approximately $2 billion of EU goods, including French cosmetics, German machinery, and Italian cheese. If you import these specific products, expect a 25% surcharge on top of the MFN rate.

Japan and South Korea are the safest bets for tariff stability. Both have free trade agreements (or in Japan's case, a digital trade agreement) that keep most industrial goods at 0%. The reciprocal tariff gap for Japan is just 1.2 percentage points, below the 5-point threshold, so no additional duty applies. South Korea benefits from the KORUS FTA, but beware: Korean steel that does not meet the "melt and pour" rules of origin faces the full 25% Section 232 duty.

The Tariff Stacking Trap: Three Real-World Examples

Most competitors quote a single tariff rate. That is a dangerous oversimplification. The real 2026 story is how multiple tariffs stack cumulatively. Here are three worked examples with exact HS codes.

Example 1: Chinese Steel Fasteners (HS 7318.15)

  • MFN rate: 3.5%
  • Section 301 (List 4A): 25%
  • Section 232 (steel derivative): 25%
  • Total stacked rate: 53.5%

On a $10,000 shipment, you pay $5,350 in tariffs. This is not 3.5% or 25%; it is the sum of all three. The only way to avoid this is to source from a country not subject to 301 or 232 (e.g., Taiwan or South Korea) or to apply for a 232 exclusion, which we discuss below.

Example 2: Vietnamese-Made Solar Panels (HS 8541.43)

  • MFN rate: 2.5%
  • Reciprocal tariff: 15%
  • Anti-dumping duty (from 2025 solar case): 18.5%
  • Total stacked rate: 36.0%

This is a hidden trap. The reciprocal tariff is new, but the anti-dumping duty on Vietnamese solar panels was already in place from a 2025 trade case. Most importers planned for only the reciprocal tariff, but the AD duty stacks on top, bringing the total to 36%. If you are in the solar industry, you need to reprice your entire 2026 inventory.

Example 3: Malaysian Semiconductor Chips (HS 8542.31)

  • MFN rate: 0% (ITA agreement)
  • Reciprocal tariff: 8%
  • Section 301 (if chip contains Chinese components): 25%
  • Total stacked rate: 33%

The ITA (Information Technology Agreement) normally zeroes out tariffs on semiconductors. But the reciprocal tariff overrides the ITA for Malaysia. And if your Malaysian chip uses a Chinese substrate, the 301 duty applies. This is the "tariff stacking trap" in its most insidious form—a product that should be duty-free now faces a 33% rate.

The De Minimis Collapse: The Biggest 2026 Disruption

Most tariff guides ignore the de minimis threshold, but it is the single largest operational change for 2026. In FY2024, CBP processed 1.04 billion de minimis shipments (packages valued under $800). These were duty-free and faced minimal scrutiny. That era is over.

As of February 2026, the de minimis threshold was lowered to $200 for most goods, with a complete elimination proposed for goods from China. This means:

  • Packages valued between $200 and $800 now face full MFN plus any applicable Section 301 duties.
  • The processing fee is $2.50 per package, plus a 0.35% ad valorem fee on the shipment value.
  • For a $500 package from China, the new duty is approximately $127.50 (25% 301 + 3.5% MFN + fees).

For e-commerce sellers using the "small package" model, this is existential. If you ship 10,000 packages a month at $400 average value, your new tariff exposure is over $1.27 million per year. The only mitigation is to shift to a US-based fulfillment model or raise prices to absorb the duty.

Tariff Exclusions, Drawbacks, and Refund Strategies

While the 2026 landscape is punitive, there are legitimate strategies to reduce your tariff burden. The most overlooked is the Section 301 retroactive exclusion process. Under the 2026 rules, importers can apply for exclusions on products that were not previously covered. If approved, the exclusion is retroactive to the date of application, and you can claim a refund for duties paid on those goods during the application period. The approval rate is roughly 30%, and the process takes 4-6 months. This is not a sure thing, but for high-volume items, the refund can be substantial.

The second strategy is duty drawback. Under 19 U.S.C. § 1313, you can recover up to 99% of duties paid on imported goods that are later exported, either as finished products or as components of exported goods. This is not a loophole; it is a statutory right that most importers never use. In FY2024, only $1.1 billion in drawback claims were paid out, while the CBP estimates that $10–15 billion in duties were eligible for drawback. If you import goods, add value, and then export, you are leaving money on the table.

Finally, the Section 232 exclusion process is still active for steel and aluminum. You must demonstrate that no US producer can supply the product in sufficient quantity or quality. The approval rate is around 25%, and the exclusion lasts for one year. The key is to apply before you start importing, not after the fact.

Country-of-Origin Enforcement: The AI Trap

The 2026 rules tighten rules-of-origin enforcement with new CBP AI-based verification. This is not a paper tiger. CBP has deployed machine learning algorithms that flag shipment patterns indicative of transshipment—e.g., a Vietnamese exporter with no manufacturing capacity, or a Mexican factory that imports more Chinese components than its output justifies.

The consequence is retroactive duty assessment. If CBP determines that your goods were actually of Chinese origin, you will owe the difference between the rate you paid (e.g., 2.5% for Vietnam) and the correct Chinese rate (e.g., 27.4%), plus penalties that can be up to two times the value of the merchandise. In Q1 2026, CBP issued $340 million in penalty notices related to origin misdeclaration. The era of "origin gaming" is over.

Decision Framework: Reshore, Near-Shore, or Stay?

With tariffs shifting so rapidly, you need a decision framework. Here is a five-step process to evaluate your sourcing strategy for 2026.

  1. Calculate your total tariff stack. Do not use the MFN rate. Use the combined rate (MFN + Section 301 + Section 232 + Reciprocal + AD/CVD). Our tariff calculator at tariffcalculator2026.com can do this for you in 30 seconds.
  2. Model the total landed cost. Add freight (which has increased 15% year-over-year), insurance, customs broker fees (typically 0.5% of value), and the new de minimis processing fees if applicable.
  3. Compare to reshoring. US manufacturing labor costs are 3-5x higher than Vietnam, but automation is closing the gap. If your tariff stack exceeds 30%, reshoring becomes viable for products with high labor intensity.
  4. Evaluate near-shoring to Mexico. USMCA gives you 0% tariffs, but you must prove origin. The cost of compliance (certificate of origin, record-keeping) is roughly $2,000-$5,000 per year per product line. This is a rounding error compared to a 15% tariff.
  5. Run a break-even analysis. If you are paying 17.5% to import from Vietnam, you can absorb a 12% increase in US production costs and still break even.

FAQ: Your Tariff Questions Answered

Q: What is the actual tariff rate I'll pay on goods from China in 2026?

A: It depends on your HS code, but the effective average is 27.4%. For steel-containing products, the rate can reach 53.5% once Section 232 and Section 301 stack. For EVs, the rate is 100%. Use the CBP's HTSUS lookup tool or our calculator to find your exact product rate.

Q: How do I find my product's HS code and corresponding tariff rate?

A: The US uses the 10-digit HTSUS code. Start with the general product category (e.g., Chapter 85 for electronics), then narrow by function. The USITC's HTS Search is free and authoritative. Once you have your 10-digit code, check for any Section 301 or 232 subheadings that apply additional duties.

Q: What's the difference between MFN, Section 301, and Section 232 tariffs?

A: MFN is the base World Trade Organization rate applied to all members. Section 301 is a trade remedy for unfair trade practices, currently targeting China specifically. Section 232 is a national security tariff on steel and aluminum. These stack—you pay MFN + 301 + 232 on the same product. They are not alternatives.

Q: Will the 2026 reciprocal tariff framework change rates for Vietnam and India?

A: Yes. Vietnam faces a 15% reciprocal tariff, bringing the effective rate to 17.5% for most goods. India faces 10%, but a negotiated trade deal could reduce this to 5% by Q3 2026. Monitor USTR announcements monthly, as these rates are still in flux.

Q: Can I still claim the de minimis exemption in 2026?

A: Only for packages valued under $200, and only if the goods are not from China. The threshold was lowered from $800 in February 2026. For packages from China, the exemption is completely eliminated. Plan for $2.50 per-package processing fees plus duties on all commercial shipments.

Q: How do I apply for a Section 301 exclusion or duty drawback?

A: Section 301 exclusion applications are filed through the USTR portal. You must show that the product is not available from a US source. Duty drawback is claimed via CBP Form 7551, and you have up to five years from the date of import to file. The drawback can recover up to 99% of duties paid on goods that are later exported. We recommend hiring a licensed customs broker for both processes.
