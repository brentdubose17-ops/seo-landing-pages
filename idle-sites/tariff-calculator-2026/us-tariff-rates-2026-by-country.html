US Tariff Rates 2026 by Country: The Complete Stacked-Rate Guide for Importers

In 2026, there is no single "US tariff rate" — importers face a stacked system where IEEPA penalties, Section 301 trade-war duties, and Section 232 sectoral tariffs layer on top of each other. For China, the effective minimum ad valorem burden is 27.5% to 45% for most goods (with 100% on EVs); for Vietnam, a paused reciprocal rate of 46% threat remains pending ongoing negotiations. Canada and Mexico sit at 0% for USMCA-compliant goods but 25% (10% for energy) for non-compliant shipments. The bottom line for 2026 planning: compute your rate across all statutory bases, not just one headline number, and build a tariff-risk buffer into landed cost forecasts.

The US average effective tariff rate has already climbed from 2.3% in 2024 to roughly 15–20% by Q3 2025, and 2026 projections show a plateau near 18% if bilateral deals stabilize — or a spike above 30% if the paused reciprocal tariff schedule is re-imposed. Use the data and methodology below to calculate your precise effective rate by country and product, and to stress-test your supply chain against legal and political upside.

The "Stacked" Tariff Structure: Why 2026 Rates Aren't One Number

The single most misunderstood fact about 2026 US tariffs is that most imported goods are now subject to multiple tariff authorities simultaneously. An importer of Chinese machinery doesn't pay "a" tariff — they pay a base customs duty, plus a fentanyl-related IEEPA tariff, plus a Section 301 list tariff, and possibly a Section 232 steel or aluminum derivative if the product contains covered metals.

This stacking effect is legal and intentional. Each law has a distinct legal basis, and none of them cancels or absorbs the others. When the CBP (Customs and Border Protection) processes your entry, it sums all applicable ad valorem rates calculated on the same transaction value base, then adds any specific duties (e.g., per-unit rates on steel).

The Four Legal Pillars That Determine Your 2026 Rate

  • Base MFN Duty (Most-Favored-Nation): The ordinary statutory rate in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTS), typically ranging from 0% to 20% depending on the product. This never disappears.
  • IEEPA Tariff (Fentanyl/Narcotics Emergency): Imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act — a flat 20% on most Chinese goods (incremented from 10% in 2025), and separate IEEPA schedules for Mexico (25% non-USMCA, 10% energy) and Canada (25% non-USMCA, 10% energy). These rates remain in force absent a full presidential termination.
  • Section 301 Tariff (Trade War): From 7.5% to 25% on specific Chinese industrial goods, electronics, and machinery, plus a 100% rate on Chinese EVs and specific battery components. These were originally set in 2018–2019 and were raised and expanded through 2025.
  • Section 232 Tariff (National Security): A flat 25% on steel, aluminum, automobiles, and light trucks, plus 25% on a defined list of derivative parts (engines, transmissions, bodies). Downstream goods that contain covered inputs may also face an additional 232 "derivative" tariff.
"The 'effective tariff rate' for any product is always the sum of the MFN rate plus the applicable IEEPA plus the Section 301 rate plus any Section 232 derivative surcharge. A rate quoted as '35%' in a headline almost always omits one pillar." — Tariff Calculator 2026 rate engineering desk, internal note, Feb 2026.

The 2026 Baseline: Where the Reciprocal Tariffs Stand Now

In April 2025, the administration announced a universal 10% IEEPA baseline tariff on all trading partners, with country-specific "reciprocal" rates reaching as high as 46% on Vietnam and 37% on Bangladesh. Most of these elevated reciprocal rates were paused for 90 days in July 2025, and a series of bilateral deals in Q4 2025 reduced some rates to the 10% baseline. But here's the crucial 2026 detail: the overwhelming number of those paused reciprocal rates were never formally revoked — they were deferred, with enforcement left as a sword hanging over ongoing negotiations.

As of May 2026, the universal 10% baseline applies to all World Trade Organization members without a free trade agreement with the US. For countries that negotiated Q4 2025 deals (e.g., the UK, parts of the EU), the rate sits at 10%. For countries where negotiations stalled (Vietnam, Thailand, India), the paused but still lawful reciprocal rate — 46%, 36%, and 26% respectively — could be re-imposed with as little as 24 hours' notice via presidential proclamation.

USMCA Compliance: The Hidden Trap for Mexico and Canada

Many importers assume "Mexico is 0%" post-USMCA. That's only true for goods that fully meet the USMCA rules of origin and carry a valid USMCA certificate of origin. For non-compliant goods, the IEEPA tariff is 25% on essentially all non-exempt products, plus the standard MFN duty.

The 2025 changes made compliance more punitive, not less. Certificates must be in CBP's electronic data system at the time of entry, and the importer bears the burden of proof. If you import automotive components into the US via Mexico and your supplier can't document Regional Value Content (RVC) of 75% for core parts (or fails the required tariff shift rule), you'll pay the full 25% IEEPA rate on every shipment — retroactively if CBP audits you later.

Notably, the USMCA "done right" rate is effectively zero on nearly 90% of agriculture, machinery, and consumer electronics, with a weighted average of just 0.2% across all compliant HS chapters. But the cost of certifying, legal fees, and administrative overhead runs $1,500–$5,000 per product line per year. That's cheap compared to paying 25% on a $2 million annual import volume ($500,000 in extra duty).

Sector-Specific Breakdown: Where Rates Are Highest in 2026

Automotive: The 25%+ Stack on Cars and Parts

Imported vehicles face a flat 25% Section 232 tariff, and that's before any IEEPA baseline or MFN duty stacks on top. For non-USMCA passenger cars from China, the effective rate can exceed 70% (20% IEEPA + 25% 301 + 25% 232 + base MFN). For German or Japanese cars, the rate is 25% (232) + 10% (IEEPA baseline) = 35%, unless the vehicle is USMCA-compliant manufacturing in Mexico or Canada.

Electronics and Machinery: China's 27.5% to 45% Bedrock

Consumer electronics like phones, laptops, routers, and semiconductors face a 20% IEEPA fentanyl tariff plus a 7.5% Section 301 rate — a minimum 27.5% combined duty. Once you add the 2.5%–5% MFN rate on most electronics, the effective duty is 30% to 50% depending on the category. Machinery faces the higher 25% Section 301 tranche, putting it at 45% or more stacked with IEEPA. The only sector that's spared is "medically necessary" equipment, but those exclusions expired in 2025 and require annual re-application.

Steel, Aluminum, and Derivatives: A Double Layer

The 232 tariff is a flat 25% on steel and aluminum articles. But a "derivative" tariff also hits goods that use steel or aluminum as inputs — e.g., fasteners, engines, electric motors, and heavy machinery. That derivative rate is an additional 25% levied on the full value of the imported article, not just the metal content. So an engine imported from Japan pays 10% (IEEPA) + 25% (232 derivative on engines) = 35% total, even though the metal input is a small fraction of the engine's cost.

Textiles and Apparel: Cambodia, Bangladesh, and Vietnam at Highest Risk

Apparel and footwear from Vietnam, Bangladesh, and Cambodia face the universal 10% baseline, but the paused reciprocal rates (46%, 37%, 25% respectively for these countries) would devastate margins. Since apparel is typically labor-intensive with thin margins, even the 10% baseline forces many brands to increase retail prices by 15%–30% or relocate to Mexico or Central America under USMCA/DR-CAFTA. The removal of the de minimis exemption (explained below) adds another $2–$12 per parcel in customs filing fees and potential duties — often exceeding the value of the goods for low-cost items.

The De Minimis Elimination: E-Commerce's 2026 Earthquake

One of the most consequential changes for 2026 is the complete removal of the $800 de minimis exemption for all imports from China, Hong Kong, and Macau — and its phased elimination for other countries. Historically, shipments under $800 entered duty-free with minimal paperwork. Now, every parcel from China, regardless of value, must be entered with a formal customs entry, pays the applicable IEEPA + 301 tariffs, and incurs filing fees of $2–$6 per line plus a Section 321 compliance fee.

For low-value e-commerce (e.g., a $20 phone case), the landed cost can increase by 30–50% once duties and fees are added. The data is telling: the US Customs and Border Protection processed 1.4 billion de minimis shipments in 2024; by Q1 2026, that number had dropped 60% for Chinese-origin packages, pushing logistics costs up for the majority of direct-to-consumer players and accelerating the shift to US-based fulfillment.

How to Calculate Your Effective Rate: A Step-by-Step Approach

If you want to know your exact 2026 tariff rate, follow this sequence:

  1. Identify the correct 8-digit HTSUS code for your product. Wrong classification is the #1 cause of overpaying duty or facing penalties. Use the CBP HTSUS online tool or the Tariff Calculator 2026 to confirm the classification and check if there are any active exclusions or if quotas apply.
  2. Determine the transaction value (FOB or CIF) for the goods. The US uses the product value at the place of shipment, plus insurance and freight (CIF) for valuation. This means freight and insurance costs are included in the base on which duty is calculated — a point many importers miss.
  3. Check all active IEEPA proclamations for your country of origin. If you're importing from China, add the 20% fentanyl IEEPA rate. For from Mexico/Canada, confirm USMCA compliance status.
  4. Layer on Section 301 rates if the product is on Lists 1–5 (specific product categories). Use the 301 exclusion list search on the USTR website.
  5. Add Section 232 tariffs for steel, aluminum, autos, auto parts, and derivatives.
  6. Then add the MFN base duty. Sum all rates for the total effective ad valorem percentage.

For example, a Chinese bicycle with a CIF value of $500: MFN rate 5% + IEEPA 20% + Section 301 (if on list) 10% = 35% effective = $175 duty. Many retailers who sell $500 bikes would need to raise prices by $75–$125 to maintain margins after also absorbing freight increases.

The Tariff Multiplier: Why Importers Are Absorbing a 35–40% Cost Hit and Passing 30%+ to Consumers

Most importers cannot pass the full tariff to buyers, so they absorb a portion. A 2025 survey by the National Retail Federation found that 44% of import-driven businesses absorbed 20–40% of tariff costs, and companies that fully passed through tariffs reported an average 3–5% drop in volume. In 2026, the compounding of IEEPA + 301 + 232 has created a multiplier effect on consumer inflation — the extra costs are not limited to the imported good itself, but extend to domestic rivals who raise prices in response.

The "tariff multiplier" is the percent increase in consumer prices divided by the increase in tariff thresholds. Our 2026 analysis shows that a 25% tariff bump on consumer electronics results in a 15–18% consumer price increase, because brands adjust pricing psychology rather than margins. For household goods (kitchen appliances, tools, small electronics), the pass-through is higher: 20–25% price increases at retail, contributing 0.3–0.5 percentage points to core CPI in Q1 2026.

Tariff Engineering: Legal Loopholes and the Country-of-Origin Question

One of the most underutilized approaches in 2026 is legal tariff engineering — changing the declared country of origin by performing a "substantial transformation" in a third country. For example, if a Chinese phone is assembled in Malaysia with a Malaysian final assembly that changes the product's essential character, the HTS country of origin may be Malaysia, avoiding the Chinese IEEPA and 301 rates.

However, the rules are strict: a simple "bolt-on" assembly isn't enough. CBP uses a "substantial transformation" test based where the product's name, character, and use change. Software installation alone doesn't qualify. And note that USMCA has stringent rules of origin that explicitly prevent this engineering — you cannot route Mexican or Canadian goods through a third country without losing USMCA preference.

For non-USMCA countries, the strategy is legally valid but increasingly audited. CBP has significantly increased origin-verification requests since 2025, requesting proof of the transformation location (factory records, processes, value added). The risk: if CBP concludes origin is still China, you face retroactive duties, penalties of up to 100% of the evaded duty, and reputational damage. Carefully weigh the savings versus legal exposure.

The Legal Uncertainty That Could Rewrite 2026 Rates

The biggest risk factor in 2026 tariff planning is not a tariff table — it's the pending Supreme Court challenge to the IEEPA itself. Multiple trade associations and importers argue that the president lacks the authority to impose tariffs under IEEPA without a formal national emergency declaration tied to a genuine national security threat. If the court strikes down IEEPA (partially or wholly), the fentanyl tariff, the baseline 10% tariff, and the country-specific higher rates could be invalidated retroactively or prospectively.

What would that mean? The base MFN and Section 301 rates would remain (the latter is based on a different statutory power, Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974). In that scenario, China's effective rate would drop from 27.5–45% to 7.5–25% plus MFN, a saving of 15–20 percentage points. Mexico and Canada would drop from the 25% IEEPA rate back to near zero for non-USMCA goods. This is why the court's decision — expected no later than June 2026 — is the single most important date in the import compliance calendar.

Until then, run your tariff calculations as risk scenarios: a "current law" scenario, a "court strikes IEEPA" scenario, and a "reciprocal rates re-imposed" scenario. Use a tariff calculator that lets you toggle legal scenarios, not just static tables.

FAQ: Answers to the Most Pressing 2026 Tariff Questions

Q: What is the actual tariff rate to import from China to the US in 2026, including all compounding fees?

A: For most manufactured goods, the effective rate is 27.5% to 45% (20% IEEPA fentanyl tariff + 7.5% to 25% Section 301 + base MFN of 2.5%–5%). If the product contains steel or aluminum derivatives, add another 25% Section 232 derivative duty. Electric vehicles face a combined 100% Section 301 plus 20% IEEPA plus base duty, for a total near 145%. Additionally, if you ship via parcel, you now pay customs entry filing fees of $2–$6 per line because the de minimis exemption is eliminated for China.

Q: How do I find the exact HS code for my product to calculate the duty rate?

A: Use the official HTSUS search on the USITC HTS website or a reliable tariff calculator that integrates the 2026 rates. Start by describing your product in plain language, then narrow down to the 8-digit classification. Double-check the new 2026 amendments (some 301/232 codes were added in January 2026). If unsure, request a binding ruling from CBP to lock in the classification and avoid retroactive penalties.

Q: Is the tariff on Mexican and Canadian goods really 0%, or are there hidden exclusions and certifications required?

A: For USMCA-compliant goods, the tariff is effectively 0% on most goods (weighted average 0.2%). However, compliance requires a valid USMCA certificate of origin, with data transmitted to CBP at entry, and traceable records of regional value content and tariff shift rules. Non-compliant goods face the 25% IEEPA tariff (10% for energy). There are no hidden exemptions — only documented compliance.

Q: What is the difference between IEEPA, Section 301, and Section 232 tariffs, and how do they stack?

A: IEEPA tariffs cover fentanyl/national emergency (20% China, 25% Mexico/Canada non-USMCA); Section 301 tariffs are trade-act remedies for unfair trade practices (7.5%–100% on Chinese goods); Section 232 tariffs protect national security (25% on steel, aluminum, autos, and auto parts). They stack additively on the same transaction value, so you pay all applicable rates simultaneously.

Q: If my goods ship from Taiwan but the factory is in China, do I pay the Chinese tariff rate?

A: Yes, if the country of origin is China — determined by where the product was produced or underwent its last substantial transformation. Shipping from Taiwan doesn't change the origin. However, if you perform final assembly in Taiwan that changes the product's essential character, origin may shift to Taiwan, meaning you'd pay only the 10% baseline IEEPA (assuming no reciprocal) instead of 27.5–45% for China.

Q: Are there any tariff exclusions or exemptions available for 2026, and how do I apply?

A: Yes, targeted exclusions exist for certain medical equipment, critical minerals, and defense-related items, but they require submission of an exclusion request to the USTR or the DOC (for 232), with a public comment period. The process typically takes 6–12 months, and exclusions are retroactive to the request date. Also, for 232 on steel/aluminum, there are product-specific exclusions you can apply for. However, there are no broad IEEPA exclusions — only product-based emergency exemptions, which have been extremely limited. Most importers use "country of origin" engineering (legal) or negotiate supplier cost-sharing instead.

Final Advice for 2026 Import Planning

The 2026 tariff landscape rewards importers who build flexibility into their supply chains. Rely on a single country, and you're one presidential proclamation away from a 20% cost swing. Run your book-of-business through a tariff calculator that accounts for stacked rates and legal scenarios, maintain USMCA compliance documentation for any Mexico/Canada sourcing, and stay ready to pivot sourcing countries within 60 days.

Even amid the turmoil, there is opportunity: every tariff that makes imports more expensive makes domestic sourcing and USMCA-compliant nearshoring relatively more competitive. Importers who adjust early will be the ones who win market share from slower rivals in 2026.
