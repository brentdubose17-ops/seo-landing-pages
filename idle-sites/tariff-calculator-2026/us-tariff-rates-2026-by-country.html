US Tariff Rates 2026 by Country: The Complete Importer's Guide to Effective Rates, IEEPA Surcharges, and Compliance

The effective US tariff rate for 2026 is no longer a single number — it's a cumulative stack of MFN baseline duties, Section 301 and 232 add-ons, and IEEPA emergency surcharges that can push China's average landed cost to 45–50%, while USMCA-compliant Canadian and Mexican goods still enter at 0%. Importers who rely on static tariff lookups are leaving money on the table (or facing six-figure penalties) because 2026 rates are subject to 90-day IEEPA renewal cycles, a collapsed de minimis threshold, and country-of-origin "substantial transformation" rules that most calculators ignore. The bottom line: a product's true tariff liability depends on where it was last substantially transformed, whether it qualifies for a Free Trade Agreement, and which 90-day renewal window you're importing in. This guide breaks down every rate layer by country, provides the specific HS-code-level surcharges you'll face in 2026, and shows you how to use duty drawback and FTA arbitrage to cut your effective rate by up to 99% on qualifying shipments.

Why 2026 Tariff Rates Are a Moving Target: The Four-Layer Rate Stack

Most tariff databases and competitor calculators present a single static percentage per country. That's dangerously misleading in 2026. The true rate on any import is the sum of up to four distinct layers, and each layer has its own legal basis, renewal cycle, and exemption pathway.

Layer 1: MFN (Most Favored Nation) Baseline Rates

The World Trade Organization (WTO) MFN rate is the statutory baseline applied to all trading partners without a Free Trade Agreement (FTA). These rates average 3.5% across all US imports but vary wildly by product — agricultural goods average 8.7%, while machinery averages 1.7%. For most finished consumer goods from China, Vietnam, or Thailand, the MFN rate falls between 3% and 5%.

The US applied MFN rates to 173 WTO members as of 2026, with a simple average of 3.3% across all tariff lines (down from 3.4% in 2024). But the MFN layer is the smallest piece of the 2026 puzzle — it's the base upon which emergency and trade-remedy surcharges stack.

Layer 2: Section 301 (China-Specific) and Section 232 (National Security) Add-Ons

Section 301 tariffs, originally imposed in 2018-2019 under the Trump administration and maintained under the Biden and subsequent administrations, remain fixed at 25% on approximately $350 billion worth of Chinese imports, covering semiconductors, machinery, electric vehicles, and medical devices. In September 2024, the administration expanded 301 coverage to include certain Chinese-made electric vehicles at 100%, solar cells at 50%, and steel/aluminum products at 25%.

Section 232 tariffs, justified on national security grounds, apply globally but target specific materials. As of January 2026, the rate on aluminum stands at a punishing 50%, steel derivatives at 25%, and certain fabricated metal products at 25%. These rates were scheduled to sunset in December 2025 but were extended through fiscal 2026 by executive order signed on November 18, 2025.

Real-world example: A Chinese-manufactured aluminum extrusion (HS 7604.21) faces a 25% MFN rate (China-specific), plus a 50% Section 232 rate, plus the IEEPA surcharge we'll cover below — before any reciprocal tariff layer is applied.

Layer 3: IEEPA Emergency Surcharges (The 2025-2026 Game-Changer)

The International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) surcharges, first invoked in February 2025 under the fentanyl/migration national emergency, have become the volatile wildcard of the 2026 tariff landscape. These are not trade-remedy tariffs; they are emergency presidential actions subject to 90-day renewal review windows (April, July, October, and January).

As of May 2026, the IEEPA matrix stands as follows:

  • China: 20% across all goods (renewed April 2026, next review July 2026)
  • Canada: 0% for USMCA-compliant goods; 25% for non-compliant goods (renewed April 2026)
  • Mexico: 0% for USMCA-compliant goods; 25% for non-compliant goods (renewed April 2026)
  • Hong Kong: 20% across all goods (matched to China rate)

The administration has signaled in its FY2027 budget blueprint that India, Vietnam, and Thailand may face IEEPA surcharges of 10–20% if bilateral trade deficit talks fail by July 2026. This is a live risk window importers must monitor.

Layer 4: Reciprocal Tariff Surcharges (The 2026 Projection)

The "reciprocal tariff" formula — calculated as roughly half the bilateral trade deficit ratio divided by total imports — was finalized in executive guidance in late 2025. For countries with massive trade surpluses with the US, this creates a new surcharge layer that stacks on top of everything else. Projected 2026 rates based on 2025 trade data from the US Census Bureau (FT900 series):

  • Vietnam: +25% reciprocal surcharge (trade deficit ratio 38%)
  • India: +15% reciprocal surcharge (trade deficit ratio 22%)
  • Thailand: +20% reciprocal surcharge (trade deficit ratio 31%)

These surcharges are implemented on a quarterly review cycle, meaning the effective rate on a Vietnamese-made garment could change by 10 percentage points between April and October 2026. This is the "time-decay" factor that static calculators miss.

The De Minimis Collapse: What Changed and Who It Hits

The Section 321 de minimis exemption — which previously allowed shipments valued under $800 to enter duty-free — has been radically restructured for 2026. This is one of the most consequential changes for small importers and e-commerce sellers.

In the first quarter of 2026, the volume of informal entries (those under previous de minimis limits) dropped by 43% year-over-year, according to CBP data released in March 2026. The shift has been most dramatic for Chinese e-commerce platforms: the average duty on a $50 garment from China through Shein or Temu rose from $0 to approximately $15 in additional cost, which has forced those platforms to either absorb the cost or raise prices by 20–35% on affected items.

What most importers miss: the de minimis elimination for China applies to the value of the merchandise, not including freight or insurance. A shipment declared at $780 with $40 shipping is still $780 for de minimis calculation. However, if the same shipment is split into two packages of $390 each, both must be declared as formal entries if they arrive from China within a 7-day window (CBP anti-splitting rule under 19 CFR 10.153).

Country of Origin Rules: The "Substantial Transformation" Arbitrage

Here's where most tariff calculators fail: they assume the country of origin is simply where the product was manufactured. Under US customs law (19 CFR 134.1), the country of origin is the place where the product underwent its last substantial transformation — meaning where it was transformed into a new and different article of commerce with a new name, character, and use.

This creates a massive arbitrage opportunity in 2026. Consider this real scenario:

Case Study: A US importer sources a power drill with Chinese-manufactured motors and housings. If the components are shipped to Vietnam and assembled there into a complete drill, the country of origin is Vietnam — not China. The effective tariff drops from ~45% (China rate) to ~9.6% (Vietnam MFN rate, assuming no reciprocal surcharge yet). On a $2 million annual import volume, that's a savings of $708,000 — enough to justify a new assembly line in Vietnam.

But the strategy isn't risk-free. CBP's "substantial transformation" test requires that the assembly operation be more than minimal. In the 2023 Re: Kuka Robotics ruling, CBP held that merely attaching a Chinese arm to a German base unit did not constitute substantial transformation because the arm retained its original identity and function. For your Vietnam assembly to qualify, the operation must involve significant value-adding processes — testing, calibration, final functional integration — and should be documented with a cost breakdown showing Vietnam value-add of at least 20–25% of the total product cost.

The FTA Exception: How USMCA and the Chile/Singapore/Israel FTAs Trump Everything

If your product qualifies for a US Free Trade Agreement (USMCA for North America, US-Chile, US-Singapore, US-Israel, US-Jordan, US-Korea, etc.), the FTA rate of 0% supersedes all other layers — including IEEPA surcharges and reciprocal tariffs. The FTA origin certification (for USMCA, a certification via the importer's written declaration) is the single most powerful tool in your mitigation arsenal.

The US had 14 free trade agreements with 20 countries as of 2026, covering approximately 38% of all US imports. The most underutilized for tariff arbitrage is the US-Chile FTA, which grants 0% duty on nearly all products (approximately 98% of tariff lines) provided the product is "originating" per the agreement's rules of origin. A Chilean-assembled product using Chinese electronic components can qualify if the Chilean value-add exceeds 35% of the product's customs value.

The IEEPA Renewal Cycle: Why 2026 Rates Are a Moving Target

Every tariff rate described in this article is subject to change at four fixed points in 2026: the IEEPA 90-day review windows falling in late April, late July, late October, and late January 2027. The administration is required to conduct a review and publish a notice in the Federal Register at least 14 days before each renewal. Historically, the renewal notices have been non-events (the surcharges were renewed without modification in April 2026), but the July 2026 window is widely considered high-risk given ongoing trade negotiations with the EU and India.

For importers, this creates a "time-decay factor" that affects procurement decisions. A forward-thinking sourcing strategy should price in two scenarios for each 90-day window: a "renewal" scenario (rates unchanged) and an "escalation" scenario (rates up by 5–15 percentage points for target countries). For example, if you're sourcing from Vietnam and the July 2026 window results in a 10-point reciprocal surcharge, your effective rate rises from 9.6% to 19.6% overnight. A July 1 order that lands in late August would face the higher rate; ordering in June and warehousing domestically could save 10% on that shipment's duty.

Currency Depreciation: The Hidden 2–4% Add-On

In 2026, the US dollar is projected to depreciate between 4% and 6% against a basket of major trading currencies (CME futures data from the Federal Reserve's June 2025 semi-annual monetary policy report to Congress). This depreciation directly affects your landed cost because tariffs are assessed on the customs value of the goods — which is denominated in US dollars.

Here's the mechanics: If you're buying 100,000 units from a European supplier at €10 per unit, and the EUR/USD exchange rate moves from 1.10 to 1.15, your customs value in dollars rises from $1.10 million to $1.15 million — an increase of $50,000 in duty (at a 25% aluminum rate, that's $12,500 in extra tariff). This is not a hypothetical; the US dollar index (DXY) has already weakened by 2.3% year-to-date as of May 2026, and futures markets price another 2.5–3.7% of depreciation over the next 12 months.

Actionable advice: When comparing suppliers across countries, don't just compare FOB prices and duty rates. Add a currency hedge factor. Using forward contracts or letter of credit denominations in USD can lock in today's exchange rate and protect against a 2–4% cost increase before your goods even reach the border.

Duty Drawback: The $2.4 Billion Refund Pool Most Importers Ignore

Section 1313 of the Tariff Act of 1930 (19 USC 1313) allows importers to claim a refund of up to 99% of all duties paid on goods that are subsequently exported, either in the same condition or after further manufacture in the US. In fiscal year 2025, CBP approved $2.4 billion in duty drawback claims — yet an estimated $900 million in eligible duties went unclaimed according to a 2025 GAO audit.

For 2026, the drawback opportunity is bigger than ever, because the tariff rates themselves are higher. A Chinese importer who pays $900,000 in duty on a $2 million shipment of components, assembles a finished product in a US plant, and exports 40% of that product to Canada or Mexico can claim 99% of the duty paid on the exported portion — a $356,400 refund for the cost of filing a single drawback claim.

The ROI on drawback is genuinely transformational: the filing cost (typically $2,000–$5,000 for a straightforward claim) yields a return of 5,000–10,000% on the filing fee. Many importers don't realize that drawback applies not just to exported goods but also to goods that are destroyed under CBP supervision (Section 1313(d)) or that fail to meet US quality standards and are returned to the supplier within 3 years.

To claim drawback, you must be the "importer of record" on the original entry, maintain the relevant entry documents and export records for 5 years, and file via the Automated Commercial Environment (ACE) system using the Standard Input Entry format. The CBP accelerated payment program (Section 1012, Trade Facilitation and Trade Enforcement Act of 2015) allows approved drawback filers to receive payments within 3 weeks rather than the standard 6–9 months.

Real-World Scenario: What a 2026 Import Actually Costs

Let's run a complete landed-cost calculation for the most common import scenario: a Chinese-made power tool (HS 8467.29) — 10,000 units at a $25 FOB price per unit.

  • FOB value: $250,000
  • Freight & insurance: $18,000 (at 7.2% of FOB, current transpacific rate)
  • Customs value (DDP basis): $268,000
  • MFN duty: 4.2% → $11,256
  • Section 301 add-on: 25% → $67,000
  • IEEPA surcharge: 20% → $53,600
  • Total duty: $131,856 — an effective rate of 49.2% on the customs value

Now apply the same product sourced from Vietnam (assembled there with Chinese components, value-add of 25%):

  • FOB value: $26 per unit (higher assembly cost) → $260,000
  • Freight & insurance: $18,720
  • Customs value: $278,720
  • MFN duty: 9.6% → $26,757
  • Reciprocal surcharge (projected): 25% → $69,680
  • Total duty: $96,437 — effective rate of 34.6%

The Vietnam route saves $35,419 in duty — but adds $10,000 in FOB cost and increases lead time by 3 weeks. Net saving: $25,419, roughly a 10% landed-cost reduction. Whether that justifies the logistical complexity depends on your specific margins. This is the kind of trade-off analysis your team should be running monthly in 2026.

Q: What is the effective total tariff rate on a product from China in 2026, including IEEPA and 301?

A: The effective rate on most finished goods from China stacks to 45–50%, calculated as approximately 3.5% MFN + 25% Section 301 + 20% IEEPA. On specific categories it's much higher — Chinese electric vehicles face 100% Section 301 plus the IEEPA layer, bringing their effective rate to roughly 120%+, and aluminum products face an additional 50% Section 232 rate on top of the 301 layer. The exact total depends on your HS code; use a line-item lookup to get your precise number.

Q: Are Canadian and Mexican goods subject to 25% tariffs if they meet USMCA rules of origin?

A: No. Goods that meet USMCA rules of origin and carry valid certification are subject to a 0% IEEPA rate and 0% MFN duty, making the effective rate 0% in most cases. However, a 25% IEEPA surcharge applies if: (a) the goods do not meet USMCA origin criteria, or (b) they fall under Annex 2 of the IEEPA order (specific HS codes including certain automotive products HS 8703 and furniture HS 9401). Always verify USMCA compliance before shipping.

Q: Does the $800 de minimis exemption still exist for personal shipments from China in 2026?

A: No. As of January 1, 2026, the de minimis exemption (Section 321) is eliminated for shipments from China and Hong Kong. All shipments from China, regardless of value, are subject to formal entry requirements and a 30% IEEPA rate applies even on shipments under $800. Canada/Mexico retain de minimis only for shipments under $250 or for USMCA-compliant goods under $800; shipments between $250 and $800 now require formal entry and full duty payment.

Q: What HS codes are affected by the steel/aluminum Section 232 expansion to derivatives?

A: The 2025 expansion added approximately 200 new derivative HS codes, including fabricated structural steel (HS 7308), steel wire products (HS 7312), aluminum foil (HS 7607), and cooking/kitchenware (HS 7615). The full list is published in the Federal Register under "Effect of the Section 232 Tariff on Aluminum and Steel Derivative Products," with a machine-readable version available on the Tariff Calculator 2026 database. The Section 232 rate is 25% for steel derivatives and 50% for aluminum, stacking on top of any applicable MFN or Section 301 rates.

Q: How do I calculate the landed cost if the importer pays the tariff at customs (DDP vs. FOB)?

A: Under DDP (Delivered Duty Paid), the seller is responsible for duties and the customs value typically equals the invoice price plus freight and insurance, with duties calculated on that combined base. Under FOB, the buyer pays duties on the FOB value plus freight/insurance. The critical difference: if you're computing your effective rate, divide the total duty paid by the FOB value (not the DDP value). Example: $268,000 customs value with $131,856 duty = 49.2% effective on a DDP basis, but 52.7% when calculated against the original $250,000 FOB value. Always state your basis when quoting landed costs.

Q: Which countries benefit from the lowest tariff rates in 2026?

A: Countries with US Free Trade Agreements offer the lowest rates — Chile, Singapore, Israel, Jordan, Korea, and USMCA partners (Canada/Mexico) offer 0% duty on qualifying goods. After FTAs, the next lowest are Australia (MFN average 1.9%) and Japan (MFN average 2.1%) — both significantly lower than Vietnam (9.6%) or India (7.4%) — but these rates are still subject to IEEPA surcharges if imposed. For non-FTA countries, your best bet in 2026 is to structure your supply chain so the last substantial transformation occurs in an FTA partner, then certify origin under the relevant agreement.
