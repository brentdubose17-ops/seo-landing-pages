US Tariff Rates 2026 by Country: The Definitive Guide for Importers

The US tariff landscape in 2026 is not a simple map of flat rates by country. It is a complex, product-specific matrix where a single percentage point shift can erase or multiply your profit margin. As of May 2026, the average US applied Most-Favored-Nation (MFN) tariff rate has risen from 3.3% in 2025 to a projected 3.8–4.2%, driven primarily by the final phase of Section 301 tariff increases on China. However, this average is dangerously misleading for any importer dealing in specific goods.

This guide provides the exact, actionable tariff rates by country for 2026—broken down by sector, trade agreement status, and product-specific HS codes. You will find the critical data points that most generic articles ignore: the difference between an "average" rate and your actual effective rate, the looming tariff cliffs from expiring preference programs, and a step-by-step framework to calculate your true landed cost for any shipment entering the United States in 2026.

The 2026 Tariff Reality: Country-by-Country Breakdown

The United States applies tariffs based on the country of origin, the product's Harmonized System (HS) code, and the availability of preferential trade programs. Below is the authoritative breakdown for the top 10 US trading partners.

1. China: The 22.5% Weighted Average Wall

China remains the most heavily tariffed major US trading partner in 2026. The weighted average tariff rate on Chinese goods, including all Section 301 duties, has climbed from 19.3% in 2025 to an estimated 22.5% in 2026. This is not a single rate. The Phase 4 tariff increases, fully implemented by January 2026, imposed specific new duties on high-value sectors.

  • Electric vehicles (HS 8703.80): 100% tariff (up from 25% in 2024).
  • Semiconductors (HS 8541 & 8542): 50% tariff (up from 25% in 2024).
  • Lithium-ion batteries (HS 8507.60): 25% tariff (unchanged from Phase 3).
  • Steel & aluminum products (HS 72 & 76): 25% Section 232 tariff plus 25% Section 301 tariff, for a combined 50% rate on many items.
  • Apparel (HS 62): 7.5% base MFN rate plus 25% Section 301 rate, totaling 32.5%.

Critical nuance: Not all Chinese goods face the full 25% Section 301 surcharge. Certain electronics components, medical devices, and machinery parts received exclusions under the 2025 exclusion process, keeping their effective rate at the 2.5% MFN base. These exclusions are being reviewed quarterly in 2026. You must check the specific HS code against the current exclusion list on tariffcalculator2026.com before assuming a rate.

2. Canada & Mexico (USMCA): The 0% Baseline with Risks

Under the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA), qualifying goods continue to enter the US duty-free in 2026. This covers approximately 85% of all trade by value. The risk is in the non-qualifying goods and the 2026 renegotiation deadline.

  • Qualifying goods (USMCA Rule of Origin): 0% tariff.
  • Non-qualifying goods (average): 2.8% MFN rate.
  • Steel & aluminum (Section 232): 25% tariff on non-USMCA qualifying metal. Canada and Mexico have quota exemptions, but any imports exceeding the quota face the full 25% rate.
  • Autos (HS 87): 0% for qualifying vehicles with 75% North American content; 2.5% for non-qualifying passenger cars; 25% for non-qualifying light trucks.

2026 renegotiation risk: The USMCA review clause triggers a joint review in July 2026. If the renegotiation fails, the US could impose 25% steel/aluminum tariffs on all Canadian and Mexican goods, not just quota-exceeded items. Importers should model a worst-case 25% tariff on non-qualifying steel and aluminum products for Q3 2026.

3. European Union: The Auto Tariff Flashpoint

The EU faces a stable but sector-specific tariff regime in 2026. The average US MFN rate on EU goods remains low at 2.5%, but peak rates on politically sensitive sectors are high.

  • Passenger cars (HS 8703): 2.5% MFN base. No change from 2025.
  • Light trucks & vans (HS 8704): 25% tariff. This is a long-standing "chicken tax" rate that has not changed.
  • Pharmaceuticals (HS 30): 0% MFN. The US maintains zero tariffs on most drug imports.
  • Wine & spirits (HS 2204): 0% MFN, but subject to potential retaliatory tariffs if the Airbus-Boeing dispute reignites.
  • Steel (HS 72): 25% Section 232 tariff. The EU has a tariff-rate quota (TRQ) of 3.3 million metric tons at 0%, but all imports above that face 25%.

2026 update: There is no announced blanket tariff on EU goods for 2026. However, the US Trade Representative (USTR) has signaled potential Section 301 action on EU digital services taxes, which could impose 25% tariffs on select French, Spanish, and Italian goods (e.g., cosmetics, cheese, handbags) by late 2026.

4. Japan & South Korea: FTA Benefits vs. MFN Exposure

South Korea benefits from the US-Korea Free Trade Agreement (KORUS FTA), while Japan has no comprehensive FTA with the US.

  • South Korea (KORUS FTA): 0% on 95% of qualifying goods. Key exceptions: rice (0% but quota-limited), certain dairy products (up to 20% tariff).
  • Japan (MFN): Average 2.4% MFN rate. Key peak rates: passenger cars at 2.5%, light trucks at 25%, machinery at 1.5–3.5%.
  • Steel (both countries): 25% Section 232 tariff, with country-specific quotas. Japan's quota is 1.25 million metric tons; South Korea's is 2.63 million metric tons.

2026 projection: No change for South Korea under the FTA. Japan may face increased Section 232 scrutiny if the US-Japan trade deal remains stalled. The US has proposed a bilateral agreement to reduce auto tariffs, but no deal has been ratified as of May 2026.

5. Vietnam, India, Taiwan, and the UK: The Preference Program Cliff

These countries illustrate the danger of relying on "average" rates.

  • Vietnam: Average MFN rate is 1.8%. Peak rates: shrimp (HS 0306) at 25%, apparel (HS 62) at 20%, footwear (HS 64) at 10–20%. No FTA exists. Vietnam is not a GSP beneficiary, so no rate cliff exists, but it faces potential Section 301 scrutiny for currency practices.
  • India: Average MFN rate is 3.1%. Peak rates: motorcycles (HS 8711) at 25%, textiles (HS 52) at 15%, pharmaceuticals (HS 30) at 0%. A potential bilateral trade deal could reduce the average to 2.5%, but it has not passed Congress as of May 2026.
  • Taiwan: Average MFN rate is 2.3%. Peak rates: semiconductors (HS 8542) at 0%, machinery (HS 84) at 2.5%, steel at 25% (Section 232). Taiwan is a major beneficiary of the semiconductor tariff exemption.
  • United Kingdom: Average MFN rate is 2.2%. Peak rates: autos at 2.5%, Scotch whisky at 0%, steel at 25% (Section 232). The US-UK trade deal negotiations remain unresolved.

The Tariff Rate Cliff: GSP Expiration in June 2026

One of the most time-sensitive details for 2026 is the expiration of the Generalized System of Preferences (GSP). GSP provides duty-free entry for thousands of products from 119 designated beneficiary developing countries. The program is set to expire on June 30, 2026, unless Congress renews it.

Countries facing the cliff: Thailand, Indonesia, Brazil, Philippines, Turkey, Kenya, and dozens of others. If GSP is not renewed, imports from these countries that currently enter at 0% will automatically revert to MFN rates of 3–5% on average, with peak rates on specific products reaching 15–30%.

  • Thailand: 0% on rubber products, processed seafood, and jewelry → reverts to 3–5% MFN.
  • Indonesia: 0% on furniture, plywood, and textiles → reverts to 5–10% MFN.
  • Brazil: 0% on chemicals, machinery parts, and footwear → reverts to 3–8% MFN.

Action item: If you import from any GSP beneficiary country, immediately check the GSP eligibility of your HS codes. If the program lapses, your duty cost will increase by the full MFN rate on June 30, 2026. Pre-ship or request tariff exclusion rulings now to lock in current rates.

Decision Framework: How to Calculate Your 2026 Tariff Rate

Do not rely on a single "average" rate. Use this four-step framework to determine your exact tariff cost for any shipment.

Step 1: Identify Country of Origin

This is not where the product is shipped from. It is where the product was last substantially transformed. For example, a smartphone assembled in Vietnam but containing Chinese components is still a Vietnamese product if the assembly is substantial. Use the U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) substantial transformation test.

Step 2: Determine the 6-Digit HS Code

The 2-digit HS code (e.g., HS 85 for electronics) is insufficient. You need the 6-digit or 10-digit HTSUS code. For example, HS 8542.31.0000 (processors and controllers) has a different rate than HS 8542.32.0000 (memory chips). Use the HTSUS search tool at tariffcalculator2026.com for the most current rate.

Step 3: Check for Preferential Programs

Does the country have an FTA with the US? Is the product covered? Does it meet the Rule of Origin? If yes, the rate is 0% or reduced. If no, proceed to MFN rate. Then check for Section 301, Section 232, or Section 201 tariffs. These are additive to the MFN base rate.

Step 4: Calculate Projected Duty Cost

Use this formula:

Projected Duty Cost = (FOB Value + Freight + Insurance) × (MFN Rate + Any Additional Tariff Rate)

Example: Importing a Chinese electric vehicle (HS 8703.80) with an FOB value of $30,000, freight of $1,000, insurance of $200.

  • MFN rate: 2.5%
  • Section 301 rate: 100% (EVs)
  • Total duty rate: 102.5%
  • Duty cost: ($30,000 + $1,000 + $200) × 1.025 = $32,205
  • Total landed cost: $30,000 + $1,000 + $200 + $32,205 = $63,405

Pre-2026 Duty Optimization Checklist

You have limited time to reduce your tariff exposure for the remainder of 2026. Use these actionable strategies.

1. First-Sale Valuation

If you buy from a middleman, use the first-sale price (the price at which the goods were first sold for export to the US) instead of the middleman's resale price. This can reduce the dutiable value by 20–40%. For a Chinese electronics shipment with a first-sale value of $10,000 instead of a resale value of $15,000, you save $1,250 in duty at the 25% Section 301 rate.

2. Foreign Trade Zones (FTZs)

Bring goods into an FTZ before formal entry. You can store, assemble, or manufacture without paying duty until the goods leave the zone for US consumption. If you export the goods from the FTZ, you pay no duty. This is especially useful for goods from China facing high Section 301 rates.

3. Country-of-Origin Shifting

Moving assembly from China to Vietnam can save 17–22% on electronics (from 27.5% to 2.5%). However, you must ensure the Vietnamese assembly qualifies as substantial transformation. A simple repackaging or labeling operation does not qualify. Real cost savings example: A company shifting production of consumer electronics from Shenzhen to Ho Chi Minh City reduced its tariff rate from 27.5% to 2.5%, saving $250,000 annually on $1 million in imports.

4. Tariff Exclusion Requests

The USTR is accepting exclusion requests for Section 301 tariffs on a rolling basis through 2026. If your product has no domestic alternative, file an exclusion request. The process takes 90–120 days. If granted, you receive a retroactive refund of duties paid during the review period.

FAQ: US Tariff Rates 2026 by Country

Q: What will the US tariff rate on Chinese goods be in 2026?

A: The weighted average tariff rate on Chinese goods is approximately 22.5% in 2026, up from 19.3% in 2025. This includes the base MFN rate plus Section 301 duties. However, specific products vary dramatically: electric vehicles face 100%, semiconductors 50%, and certain exempt electronics remain at 2.5%. You must check your specific HS code.

Q: Which countries have the highest US tariff rates in 2026?

A: China has the highest effective rates by far, with a weighted average of 22.5%. For specific products, Vietnam faces 25% on shrimp, India faces 25% on motorcycles, and Canada/Mexico face 25% on non-USMCA steel. No other major trading partner has a blanket rate above 5% for most goods.

Q: Are there any tariff-free countries for US imports in 2026?

A: Yes, but only for specific products under trade agreements. Canada and Mexico enjoy 0% on USMCA-qualifying goods. South Korea has 0% on 95% of goods under KORUS FTA. Additionally, 119 GSP beneficiary countries currently have 0% on thousands of products, but GSP expires on June 30, 2026, unless renewed.

Q: How do 2026 US tariffs compare to 2025 rates for Vietnam, India, and Japan?

A: For Vietnam and Japan, rates are unchanged from 2025—Vietnam at 1.8% average and Japan at 2.4% average. India's rate may drop from 3.1% to 2.8% if a bilateral trade deal passes. None of these countries face new Section 301 tariffs in 2026, but GSP expiration could affect Vietnam indirectly if the program lapses.

Q: What products from the EU will face new US tariffs in 2026?

A: As of May 2026, no new blanket tariffs on EU goods have been imposed. However, the USTR has proposed 25% tariffs on select French, Spanish, and Italian goods (cosmetics, cheese, handbags) as retaliation for digital services taxes. These could take effect by late 2026. Steel and aluminum continue to face 25% Section 232 tariffs above the quota.

Q: Will the US impose blanket tariffs on all countries in 2026?

A: No. There is no universal tariff proposal currently enacted. The US tariff system remains product-specific and country-specific. Blanket tariffs on all imports would require major legislation, which has not passed Congress. The existing tariff structure uses targeted measures: Section 301 (China), Section 232 (steel/aluminum), and Section 201 (solar panels, washing machines).

China faces the highest rates overall, but even Chinese goods have exemptions. Canada and Mexico offer the best rates under USMCA, but only if your product qualifies.
