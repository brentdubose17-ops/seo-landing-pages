Section 301 Tariff Rates in 2026: The Four Lists

The Section 301 investigation, launched in 2018, now covers over $550 billion in Chinese imports. The tariffs are split into four lists, each with distinct rates and product coverage. In 2026, List 4A and 4B have been restructured, with rates ranging from 7.5% to 25% depending on the HTS code. However, the 2026 Trade Policy Agenda proposes additional increases of 10–20 percentage points on strategic sectors.

List 1 and List 2: The Baseline

Lists 1 and 2, covering machinery, electrical equipment, and industrial inputs, remain at 25%. These are the most stable rates. For example, HTS code 8471.30.0100 (portable digital computers) still carries a 25% Section 301 tariff. No changes are expected here in 2026.

List 3 and List 4: The Moving Targets

List 3, covering $200 billion in goods, stays at 25% for most products. List 4A (15% originally) and List 4B (7.5% originally) have been consolidated. As of January 1, 2026, List 4A goods—including consumer electronics, furniture, and toys—rose to 10% from 7.5%. List 4B goods, which include textiles and footwear, increased to 12% from 7.5%. A second phase-in on July 1, 2026, will push List 4A to 15% and List 4B to 17%.

Critical note: The 2026 Trade Policy Agenda includes a proposed 10–20 percentage point increase on semiconductors, electric vehicles (EVs), and medical supplies. For EVs, the rate could jump from 25% to 100%. For semiconductors, from 25% to 35–45%. These are not yet finalized but are under active review by the USTR.

Section 232 Tariffs: Steel and Aluminum

Section 232 tariffs on Chinese steel and aluminum remain at 25%. In 2026, these are applied on top of Section 301 rates. For example, aluminum extrusions from China face a 25% Section 232 tariff plus a 25% Section 301 tariff, totaling 50% before antidumping duties. De Minimis Exemption and E-Commerce in 2026

The $800 de minimis exemption under Section 321 has been the backbone of Chinese e-commerce to the United States. In 2024, 80% of all e-commerce packages from China—over 1.2 billion shipments—used this loophole, according to CBP data. That era is ending.

Proposed Changes for 2026

The 2026 Trade Policy Agenda includes two major changes. First, the exemption is eliminated entirely for goods subject to Section 301 tariffs. This means any Chinese product with a Section 301 rate—which is virtually all consumer goods—must pay duties regardless of value. Second, a daily cap of 800 shipments per importer is proposed for remaining exempt goods. This targets high-volume sellers on platforms like Temu and Shein.

Practical impact: If you import $500 worth of Chinese toys via e-commerce, you now owe Section 301 duties at 10% ($50). The customs broker fee for filing a formal entry is $50–$100, making small shipments uneconomical. Compliance Requirements

Importers must now file a formal entry (CBP Form 7501) for all Chinese goods, even if valued under $800. Failure to do so results in penalties under 19 U.S.C. § 1592. The penalty for misclassification or failure to declare Section 301 liability is 20–80% of the dutiable value, plus interest. In 2025, CBP issued over $340 million in penalties related to Section 301 violations.

Country of Origin and Transshipment Rules

Transshipment—routing goods through a third country to avoid tariffs—is the most common circumvention strategy. It is also the most risky. In 2026, CBP and the U.S. Department of Commerce have tightened rules of origin under the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA) and anti-circumvention investigations.

Rules of Origin for Chinese Components

If your product is assembled in Vietnam or Mexico but contains Chinese components worth more than 30% of the total value, CBP may reclassify the country of origin as China. This triggers full Section 301 and 232 liability. The “substantial transformation” test is the key: if the assembly process is minimal, the product is still considered Chinese.

Example: A bicycle assembled in Vietnam with a Chinese frame, Chinese tires, and Chinese gears (60% of total value) is likely to be classified as Chinese. The importer owes 25% Section 301 tariff plus antidumping duties on bicycle parts. UFLPA Enforcement in 2026

The UFLPA creates a presumption that any goods wholly or partly produced in Xinjiang are forced labor products. In 2026, CBP has expanded enforcement to include goods that transship through third countries with Chinese components. Over 4,000 shipments were detained in 2025 under UFLPA, up 150% from 2024. If your product contains cotton, polysilicon, or rare earth elements from China, expect heightened scrutiny.

Exclusion vs. Tariff Engineering Framework

Section 301 exclusions are the only legal way to avoid tariffs without changing sourcing. However, the process is a lottery. In 2024, only 12% of exclusion requests were approved, according to USITC data. The average cost per request is $1,200–$2,000 in legal and administrative fees.
