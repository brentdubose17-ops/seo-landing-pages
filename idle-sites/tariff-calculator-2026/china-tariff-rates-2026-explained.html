China Tariff Rates 2026 Explained: The Complete Importer's Guide to the Stack, the Math, and the Traps

In 2026, the effective US tariff rate on Chinese imports typically lands between roughly 30% and 130% depending on product and HS code — and the headline number you see in the news is almost never the rate you actually pay at the port. The stack combines MFN base rates (0–16.5% for most consumer goods), Section 301 (7.5–25% broadly, with 50–100% on EVs, solar, and semiconductors), Section 232 (25% on steel, aluminum, and thousands of derivative products), IEEPA fentanyl and reciprocal tariffs (30% combined under the 2025 Geneva framework), plus antidumping and countervailing duties that can exceed 300% on specific products.

The $800 de minimis exemption no longer applies to China — it ended May 2, 2025 — and the global suspension on August 29, 2025 means virtually every commercial shipment now requires formal or informal entry with duty, Merchandise Processing Fee, and Harbor Maintenance Fee. Because these duties apply additively to the same customs value, a Chinese lithium-ion battery can legitimately clear at 58%–80% all-in while a Chinese electric vehicle clears above 130%.

Bottom line: budget a 30%–50% landed-cost uplift on most Chinese goods, verify your exact HS code and exclusion status before you place a purchase order, and never assume the country of origin on a commercial invoice is accurate. The One-Number Answer (And Why It's Misleading)

Ask ten customs brokers "what's the tariff on China?" and you'll get ten answers: 10%, 30%, 55%, 100%. All of them are correct and all of them are wrong, because there is no single China tariff rate.

The US applies roughly 19.3% average duty on Chinese goods in 2023, before the 2024–2025 escalation cycle. By 2025, the Peterson Institute for International Economics estimated the average effective rate on Chinese imports peaked near 39.8%. By 2026, that average has settled into a band — but the variance around the average is enormous.

A cotton t-shirt and an electric vehicle both come from China. One clears at roughly 54%. The other clears above 130%. Same country, same trade relationship, radically different outcomes determined entirely by HS classification and statutory program.

The Tariff Stack: How China Rates Actually Compound (They Don't — They Add)

The single biggest analytical error importers make is treating "the China tariff" as one number. It isn't. It's a stack of independent programs, each with its own legal authority, its own exclusion process, and its own compliance risk.

Critically, these duties are generally additive on the same customs value, not compounding on top of each other. A 25% Section 301 duty plus a 25% Section 232 duty plus 30% IEEPA plus 3.4% MFN equals 83.4% — not 1.25 × 1.25 × 1.30 × 1.034. Most duties are assessed against the entered value, so you simply sum them. (AD/CVD is the main exception worth scrutinizing, since case-specific instructions sometimes define the base differently.)

Order of Application

  1. Determine customs value — typically the transaction value (price paid) plus certain additions like assists and royalties.
  2. Classify the goods — 10-digit HTSUS code. Everything downstream depends on this.
  3. Determine country of origin — where the good was wholly obtained or substantially transformed. Not where it was shipped from.
  4. Apply MFN/Column 1 General duty — the base rate for PNTR countries including China.
  5. Layer Section 301 — if the HTS code appears on Lists 1, 2, 3, 4A, or a subsequent tranche.
  6. Layer Section 232 — if the good or a derivative is steel, aluminum, copper, or auto-related.
  7. Layer IEEPA tariffs — fentanyl-related and reciprocal baseline, subject to ongoing litigation.
  8. Layer AD/CVD — only if a case order covers the product from China.
  9. Add fees — MPF, HMF, and any Section 301-specific processing.

Section 301: The Backbone of China Tariffs

Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 is the legal engine behind most China-specific duties. It covers roughly $350 billion in annual Chinese imports at rates of 7.5% to 25% across four original lists.

Lists 1–4A at a Glance

  • List 1 ($34B, effective July 2018): 25% — industrial machinery, aerospace parts, medical devices.
  • List 2 ($16B, effective August 2018): 25% — semiconductors, chemicals, plastics.
  • List 3 ($200B, effective September 2018): initially 10%, raised to 25% in May 2019 — the broad consumer goods list: furniture, handbags, textiles, seafood.
  • List 4A ($120B, effective September 2019): initially 15%, reduced to 7.5% under the Phase One agreement in February 2020 — apparel, footwear, consumer electronics, toys.
  • List 4B ($160B): suspended indefinitely, never took effect.

Exclusions: Narrow, Time-Limited, and Usually Retroactive

Section 301 exclusions are product-specific and time-limited. When USTR grants an exclusion, it typically applies retroactively to the date the request was published — meaning importers who paid the duty can file for a refund via post-summary correction or protest.

Two practical rules: first, check CBP's CROSS rulings database for a binding classification ruling before you assume your product is excluded; second, most economic exclusions from the 2018–2020 wave expired years ago, so any exclusion list you find online that isn't linked to an active Federal Register notice is probably dead.

IEEPA Tariffs: The 2025 Escalation and the 2026 Geneva Framework

In 2025, the US invoked the International Emergency Economic Powers Act to impose tariffs outside the Section 301 architecture. Two layers emerged:

  • Fentanyl-related tariff: 20% on all Chinese imports, citing narcotics trafficking flows.
  • Reciprocal baseline: 10% on Chinese imports, initially calibrated to trade deficits.

In April 2025, escalation pushed combined additional IEEPA duties to a headline of 145%, with China retaliating at 125%. The May 2025 Geneva negotiations produced a mutual de-escalation: the US settled at 30% additional (20% fentanyl + 10% reciprocal) while China reduced its retaliatory rate to 10%. That framework was extended repeatedly through 2025 and into 2026.

Importer takeaway: The Geneva framework is a political agreement, not a treaty. It has been extended through executive action, not legislation. Model your 2026 forecasts with a 30% IEEPA assumption and a scenario in which it drops to 10% or reverts to 145% — the swing is larger than most companies' entire net margin.

The Litigation Risk Nobody Is Pricing In

IEEPA tariffs have faced ongoing federal litigation over whether the statute authorizes tariffs at all. If courts were to strike them down, the 30% IEEPA layer would vanish overnight — and refund claims would likely run into the tens of billions. If Congress instead codifies tariffs through legislation, the rates become far harder to reverse and far more durable.

Either outcome changes your landed cost by 20–30 percentage points. Treat IEEPA as a distinct line item in every model, not blended into a single "China rate."

Section 232: Steel, Aluminum, and Derivatives

Section 232 national security tariffs apply at 25% on steel and 25% on aluminum, and — this is the part importers miss — they extend to a sprawling list of derivative products. That includes fabricated structural steel, aluminum extrusions, certain fasteners, and downstream goods where the metal content is deemed subject.

Derivative status is determined by the aluminum or steel content of the article, not the article's primary function. A furniture component with aluminum framing can carry Section 232 exposure even though it's classified as furniture.

This is where stacking bites hardest. A steel derivative from China can carry MFN + Section 301 + Section 232 + IEEPA simultaneously — an effective rate above 80% on the full entered value, including the non-metal components.

AD/CVD: Where Rates Go Above 300%

Antidumping and countervailing duty orders are the most dangerous layer because they are both enormous and administratively complex. They apply only to products covered by a specific order — but when they apply, they apply hard.

  • Chinese solar cells: antidumping duties around 238.95%, countervailing duties around 15.24%.
  • Aluminum extrusions: rates up to 376.85%.
  • Common other targets: steel nails, plywood, quartz surfaces, mattresses, ceramic tile, truck tires, corrugated paper.

Unlike Section 301, AD/CVD liability often depends on your supplier's specific rate. Chinese exporters that participated in the original investigation have their own company-specific rate; exporters that didn't get the "all-others" rate or, worse, the China-wide rate — which is frequently punitive.

Actionable advice: before placing a large order, search CBP's AD/CVD module for the HTS code and check the case number. Then ask your supplier for their specific cash deposit rate. If they can't answer, that's a red flag.

De Minimis Is Dead: What Changed in 2025 and 2026

For decades, Section 321 allowed shipments under $800 to enter duty-free. That era is over.

  • May 2, 2025: de minimis ended for China and Hong Kong origin goods.
  • August 29, 2025: the suspension was extended globally to all countries.

Context matters: CBP processed roughly 1.36 billion de minimis packages in FY2024, the vast majority originating from Chinese e-commerce platforms. The policy change was aimed squarely at that volume.

Non-Tariff Retaliation Is Often the Bigger Threat

Beijing's most effective levers in 2026 aren't tariffs — they're export controls. China has restricted gallium, germanium, graphite, antimony, and rare earth element exports, and it controls the majority of global processing capacity for several of these materials.

For a US manufacturer dependent on Chinese rare earth magnets, a 25% tariff is a cost problem. An export license denial is an existential one. Also watch the entity list, UFLPA forced-labor enforcement, and slower Chinese customs clearance for US-bound shipments.

Real Numbers on a $100,000 Shipment

Assume a $100,000 CIF value, Chinese-origin consumer electronics subject to MFN 0%, Section 301 List 3 at 25%, and IEEPA at 30%.

  • Duty: $100,000 × 55% = $55,000
  • MPF: $100,000 × 0.3464% = $346.40 (within limits)
  • HMF (ocean): $100,000 × 0.125% = $125.00
  • Broker fee: ~$150–$300 per entry
  • Total landed cost: approximately $155,600

That's a 55.6% uplift. If the same product originated in Vietnam at 20% MFN with no 301 and no IEEPA, duty would be $20,000 — a $35,000 difference on a single container. That gap, multiplied across a year of purchasing, is why sourcing decisions in 2026 are tariff decisions.

2026 Cliff Risks: Five Things to Watch

  1. Section 301 four-year review cycles. Additional tranches and rate adjustments have moved through notice-and-comment repeatedly. Watch the Federal Register weekly.
  2. IEEPA litigation. A judicial ruling either way would swing rates by 20–30 points.
  3. Congressional tariff powers. If Congress codifies tariffs statutorily, reversibility drops sharply.
  4. USMCA joint review (2026). Changes to rules of origin affect how attractive Mexico looks as a China alternative.
  5. China's 15th Five-Year Plan. Beijing's industrial policy signals which export controls and subsidies will shape supply availability through 2030.

Compliance: Where Importers Actually Get Burned

The penalties are asymmetric. Saving 5% on duty through misclassification can cost you 100%+ of the unpaid duty in penalties, plus lost import privileges.

  • Misclassification: Section 592 penalties reach the domestic value of the merchandise for fraud; negligence penalties are typically 1x–2x unpaid duty.
  • Transshipment and origin fraud: EAPA investigations and False Claims Act suits have produced eight-figure settlements. CBP uses trade data analytics to flag anomalies.
  • UFLPA: A withhold release order can stop your goods at the port indefinitely with a rebuttable presumption against you. Document your supply chain to the raw material level.
  • First sale and tariff engineering: Legal strategies exist — buying from the manufacturer's first sale to a middleman can reduce the dutiable value, and re-engineering a product to move it to a lower-duty HTS subheading is legitimate. Both require advance CBP rulings, not hindsight.
  • Classification rulings: File for a CBP binding ruling before the first shipment. It takes 30–90 days and eliminates classification risk entirely.

FAQ: China Tariff Rates in 2026

Q: What is the total tariff rate on Chinese imports in 2026?

A: There is no single rate. Most Chinese consumer goods clear at roughly 30%–55% effective duty, combining MFN base rates, Section 301 at 7.5–25%, and IEEPA tariffs at 30% (20% fentanyl-related plus 10% reciprocal). Strategic categories run far higher — EVs at ~132%, solar at 80%–334% with AD/CVD, semiconductors at ~80%. Steel and aluminum derivatives can exceed 83%. Always calculate by 10-digit HTS code.

Q: Does the $800 de minimis exemption still apply to China?

A: No. The de minimis exemption ended for China and Hong Kong origin goods on May 2, 2025, and was suspended globally on August 29, 2025. Postal-channel shipments now face either 30% ad valorem duty or a flat $25 per item (goods valued at or under $200) and $50 per item (goods over $200). Formal entry requires a customs bond, MPF, and 5-year record retention.

Q: Who pays the tariff — the US importer or the Chinese seller?

A: The US importer of record is legally liable for all duties, taxes, and fees. That's the party named on the CBP Form 7501 entry summary. If your Chinese supplier verbally agrees to "absorb the tariff," that's a commercial arrangement between you and them — it does not shift legal liability to CBP. Many importers now write tariff-sharing clauses into purchase contracts, but the government will still collect from you first.

Q: How do Section 301 exclusions work and when do they expire?

A: USTR grants exclusions for specific products for defined windows, often retroactive to the Federal Register publication date. If you paid duty on an excluded product before the exclusion was granted, you can typically recover it via post-summary correction or protest within 180 days of liquidation. Most 2018–2020-era exclusions have expired. Verify any exclusion's current status directly against USTR notices, not third-party summary sites.

Q: Are Section 301 and IEEPA tariffs additive or compounding?

A: Additive. Each duty rate is applied to the customs value (typically the transaction value), and the resulting dollar amounts are summed. A 25% Section 301 duty plus 30% IEEPA plus 3.4% MFN yields 58.4%, not 67.7%. The common "compounding" claim is incorrect for most duty programs. AD/CVD is the exception to watch carefully, since case-specific instructions define the assessment base.

Q: Will China tariffs go up or down in 2026?

A: Genuinely uncertain, and you should plan for both. Upside risk: IEEPA litigation fails for importers and Congress codifies tariffs, or the Geneva framework lapses and rates revert toward the April 2025 peak. Downside risk: courts strike IEEPA tariffs and 20–30 points disappear, triggering mass refund claims. The practical answer is to model three scenarios — 10%, 30%, and 60% incremental — and keep pricing flexible enough to survive all three.

Your 2026 Action Plan

  1. Get a binding ruling. If your HTS classification is uncertain, file for a CBP ruling now. It's the cheapest risk mitigation available.
  2. Build a line-item tariff model. Separate MFN, 301, 232, IEEPA, and AD/CVD. Never blend them into one number.
  3. Audit your supply chain for origin. Document substantial transformation evidence for any non-China assembly. Assume CBP will ask.
  4. Check exclusion and refund eligibility. Post-summary corrections have deadlines. Unclaimed refunds are free money you're leaving behind.
  5. Re-run landed cost monthly. Rates moved materially in 2024, 2025, and 2026. A quote from last quarter is stale.
  6. Stress-test sourcing scenarios. Compare China, Vietnam, Mexico, and India on total landed cost — not unit price. The tariff gap routinely exceeds the labor cost gap.
