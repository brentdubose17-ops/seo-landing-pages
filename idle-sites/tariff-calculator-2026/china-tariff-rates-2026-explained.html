China Tariff Rates 2026: The Complete Guide to Layered US Duties, Combined Rates, and Sourcing Strategies

US tariffs on Chinese imports in 2026 are not a single rate—they are a stacked system of at least four concurrent tariff layers (IEEPA fentanyl tariffs, Section 301, Section 232, and Section 201 measures) that together can push total duties on certain products above 170%. The post-Geneva Deal framework reduced "additional" IEEPA tariffs from a peak of 145% down to a 30% baseline (20% fentanyl + 10% additional), yet entire sectors—EVs, semiconductors, aluminum, and autos—remain walled off at 100%+ combined rates. With the elimination of the $800 de minimis exemption for China-origin packages and at least four active federal court challenges targeting IEEPA authority, importers face a highly contingent tariff landscape heading into 2026. This guide breaks down the exact stacking architecture, provides verified combined rate tables by product category, and explains how to calculate your true landed cost using current HTS codes and duty calculation methodology.

If you import anything from China in 2026—whether a 40-foot container of machinery or a $50 e-commerce parcel—you are likely paying somewhere between 30% and 170% in combined tariffs, depending on your product's classification and current exclusion status. As of January 2026, the weighted average US tariff across all Chinese imports stands at roughly 30–35%, down dramatically from the April 2025 peak of 145% but still nearly 15 times the pre-2018 MFN average of ~2.5%. This guide decodes the entire system with exact numbers you can apply to your own supply chain.

The Four-Layer Tariff Stacking Architecture Explained

The single most misunderstood fact about US–China tariffs is that importers pay the sum of multiple independent tariff layers, not just one headline number. When you see "30% tariff on Chinese goods" in the news, that figure typically refers only to the IEEPA "additional" rate—ignoring the underlying Section 301 duties, Section 232 national security tariffs, and Section 201 safeguard measures that continue to apply simultaneously.

Layer 1: IEEPA Fentanyl Tariffs (20% Baseline)

Invoked under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, the fentanyl-related tariff was first imposed at 10% on February 4, 2025, then doubled to 20% on March 4, 2025. This 20% layer remains in effect broadly across essentially all Chinese imports as of January 2026—it is not product-specific and does not require an injury finding or investigation. It is the foundational layer upon which everything else stacks.

Critically, this layer is the subject of active litigation. At least four federal court challenges target IEEPA tariff authority, including V.O.S. Selections v. Trump, now before the 11th Circuit. A Supreme Court ruling against the administration's IEEPA interpretation could unwind this 20% layer retroactively, which is why sophisticated importers are modeling both scenarios in their 2026 cost projections.

Layer 2: Section 301 Tariffs (7.5%–100% by Product)

The Section 301 tariffs, originally imposed during the 2018–2019 trade war under the Trade Act of 1974, remain in force and were substantially expanded in September 2024. These rates apply on top of the IEEPA layer and vary dramatically by product category:

  • Electric vehicles: 100%
  • Solar cells (whether assembled into modules or not): 50%
  • Semiconductors and advanced chips: 50%
  • Lithium-ion EV batteries: 25%
  • Non-EV lithium-ion batteries: 25%
  • Permanent magnets: 25%
  • Medical supplies (syringes, PPE): 25–50%
  • Steel and aluminum derivatives: 25%
  • Certain machinery and industrial components: 7.5–25%

The September 2024 revisions were formally implemented through early 2025, with a phased rollout schedule extending into 2026. A key nuance: the Section 301 tariff review process is ongoing, and the USTR has signaled further product categories may be added as the statutory four-year review cycle continues.

Layer 3: Section 232 National Security Tariffs (25%–100%)

Section 232 tariffs, justified under national security grounds, add yet another layer on top of both IEEPA and Section 301 duties. Current rates as of early 2026:

  • Steel: 50% (raised from 25% via presidential proclamation in June 2025)
  • Aluminum: 25%
  • Autos and auto parts: 25%
  • Semiconductors: 100% (rule-making announced, phased rollout across 2025–2026)
  • Copper: 50% (announced September 2025, implementation pending)

Because Section 232 tariffs apply based on the product's nature rather than its country of origin, they catch Chinese goods even when routed through third countries. Notably, Section 232 duties also apply to many non-Chinese sources (steel from Japan, Germany, and South Korea faces the same 50% rate), which has important implications for alternative sourcing strategies we explore later.

Layer 4: Section 201 Safeguards and AD/CVD Duties

Section 201 safeguard tariffs (e.g., on washing machines, solar cells, and certain transformers) and antidumping/countervailing duties (AD/CVD) add a final, product-specific layer. AD/CVD rates for Chinese goods are frequently in the 20–200% range depending on the product and the specific administrative review period. According to US Customs and Border Protection data, there were more than 750 active AD/CVD orders on Chinese-origin goods as of late 2025.

What the Geneva Deal Actually Did—and Didn't—Change

The May 2025 Geneva Deal between the US and China is widely credited with "cutting tariffs to 30%." That characterization is dangerously incomplete. The deal reduced the additional IEEPA tariff from 145% down to 10% (on top of the continuing 20% fentanyl tariff), effectively bringing the combined IEEPA layer to 30%—a meaningful reduction from the April 2025 peak.

However, the deal explicitly excluded entire sectors from the reduction. EVs, aluminum, autos, semiconductors, and certain medical supplies remained subject to their pre-existing elevated Section 301 and Section 232 rates. In practice, this means:

  • A Chinese-built EV imported in 2026 faces 20% (IEEPA) + 100% (Section 301) + 25% (Section 232 autos) = 145% combined—not 30%.
  • Chinese semiconductors face 20% (IEEPA) + 50% (Section 301) + 100% (Section 232) = 170% combined as the Section 232 semiconductor rule phases in.
  • Chinese steel faces 20% (IEEPA) + 25% (Section 301 derivatives) + 50% (Section 232 steel) = 95% combined.

The carve-outs were deliberate: the administration preserved maximum leverage over strategic sectors while providing cost relief on consumer goods and industrial inputs where China retains manufacturing dominance. Whether these carve-outs persist or collapse is the pivotal variable for 2026 sourcing decisions. The deal contains review mechanisms and "snap-back" provisions that permit re-escalation with 60 days' notice, and as of January 2026, no formal snap-back has occurred—but congressional Trade Act authority battles and Section 232 expansions remain live risks.

The End of De Minimis: A Structural Shift for E-Commerce

One of the most significant changes of 2025—with massive 2026 implications—was the elimination of the de minimis (Section 321) exemption for Chinese-origin packages. Previously, shipments valued under $800 entered the US duty-free with minimal documentation, benefiting more than 1 billion packages annually according to CBP data. The July 2, 2025 executive action terminated this exemption specifically for China-origin shipments, effective May 2025.

For direct-to-consumer sellers, platforms like Temu and Shein, and cross-border e-commerce operators, the practical impact is enormous:

  • Every parcel now requires formal customs entry with a full HTS classification
  • Full MFN + stacked duties apply on each package—there is no per-shipment waiver
  • Customs brokerage fees of $25–$75 per parcel are now standard
  • Transit times have increased as CBP processes a vastly larger volume of formal entries ($800-and-under) that previously moved through an informal streamlined lane

Unless Congress reauthorizes or modifies the de minimis threshold through legislation (a bill to restore the exemption for China was introduced in late 2025 but has not advanced), e-commerce importers must build full landed cost—including tariffs, MPF, HMF, and brokerage—into every single unit's cost structure starting January 2026.

How to Calculate Your Exact Duty in 2026: A Step-by-Step Methodology

No single rate—whether a news headline or an industry overview—tells you what you will pay. Here is the only reliable methodology, in four steps.

Step 1: Identify Your 10-Digit HTS Code

Every product imported into the US is classified under the Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States (HTSUS). You need a 10-digit HTS code—not a 6-digit international code—to find your exact column-specific rate. The authoritative source is the USITC HTS database (hts.usitc.gov), which is updated in real time. If you are exporting from the US (Schedule B) and importing (HTSUS), be aware these are different classification systems; never substitute one for the other.

When your product doesn't have an obvious classification, search the CBP's binding ruling database (Rulings Online Search System, or ROSS) for similar products. For example, a "smart electric scooter with battery" could plausibly classify in Chapter 87 (vehicles) or Chapter 95 (toys/sports equipment)—the difference in duty can exceed 30 percentage points, so a formal ruling is often worth the $200–$500 filing fee.

Step 2: Identify the Base (Column 1-General) MFN Rate

For most Chinese goods, the column 1-general rate is 0–5%. However, a substantial number of product categories with China-specific Section 301 duties will show higher "special" rates. Never assume the column 1-general rate is all you pay—the Section 301 and Section 232 tariffs appear as additional subheadings in the HTS (e.g., 9903.88.01 for Section 301, 9903.85.xx for Section 232) that must be added to your base rate.

Step 3: Stack All Applicable Additional Duty Subheadings

This is where the tariff calculator at tariffcalculator2026.com saves hours of manual research. In addition to the base MFN rate, you must add:

  • IEEPA fentanyl tariff (20%)—listed under HTS subheading 9903.01.20
  • Section 301 tariffs (variable)—9903.88.xx series
  • Section 232 tariffs (variable)—9903.85.xx and related
  • Section 201 safeguard duties—9904.xx.xx series
  • AD/CVD duties—case-specific, listed in the CBP AD/CVD database

These layers are not optional. CBP will refuse entry or impose penalties for underpayment. The stacking is arithmetic: if your base MFN rate is 2.9% and you have a 20% IEEPA layer plus a 25% Section 301 layer, your total is 47.9%—not 20% or 25%.

Step 4: Add All Other Customs Fees and Calculate True Landed Cost

Tariffs alone do not represent your full cost of importing. Your total landed cost also includes:

  • Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF): 0.3464% of value, minimum $31.67, maximum $614 per entry
  • Harbor Maintenance Fee (HMF): 0.125% on ocean shipments only
  • Customs brokerage fees: $50–$200 per entry depending on complexity
  • Freight, insurance, and inland transportation

Consider this concrete example: You import $50,000 worth of lithium-ion EV batteries (HTS 8507.60.00) from China. Your base MFN rate is 3.4%. Add the 20% IEEPA layer and the 25% Section 301 rate. Your tariff-only cost is $24,200 (48.4%). Add MPF ($173.20), HMF (if ocean—$62.50), brokerage ($150), and freight ($2,800), and your total landed cost is approximately $77,385 for goods that cost $50,000 at the factory gate—a 54.8% markup before you even account for packaging, warehousing, or reseller margins.

Can You Still Get Tariff Exclusions in 2026?

Yes, but the landscape has tightened dramatically. Under earlier Section 301 frameworks, 1,544 product exclusions were in force; most expired between 2020 and 2025. As of late 2025, only ~125 exclusions remain active through the end of 2026, and all require formal reinstatement petitions.

The current exclusion process works as follows:

  1. Check the active exclusion list on the USTR website to see if your exact product (by 10-digit HTS subheading and product description) is covered.
  2. File a reinstatement petition if your product previously had an exclusion that expired, or a new petition if you can demonstrate the tariff is causing severe economic harm with no domestic alternative.
  3. Wait 90–180 days for USTR review; approval rates for new petitions have been below 5% since 2023, though reinstatements have fared better (~60%).
  4. Ensure your supply chain documentation is clean—exclusions apply only to products that meet the exact product description and origin criteria, and CBP will test compliance on audit.

A practical note: IEEPA tariffs (the 20% layer) are not subject to the Section 301 exclusion process. There is currently no formal exclusion mechanism for the IEEPA fentanyl tariff at all, which is why many importers are pursuing Section 232 "product exclusion" petitions for steel, aluminum, and semiconductors—a separate process that exists under the Commerce Department's Section 232 framework.

Legal Contingency: The Court Cases That Could Change Everything

The IEEPA fentanyl tariff rests on contested legal authority. The administration invoked IEEPA (which is designed for national emergency powers, not trade policy) without a prior finding of an "unusual and extraordinary threat" tied to fentanyl itself—at least, that is the challengers' argument in the leading cases. As of January 2026, V.O.S. Selections v. Trump (11th Circuit) and three other federal suits are advancing. A ruling that the IEEPA tariff exceeds presidential authority under 19 U.S.C. § 1702 could nullify the 20% layer retroactively, entitling importers to refunds.

Importers face a genuine dilemma: wait to pay the 20% IEEPA layer and risk penalties, or pay now and litigate for refunds later. The pragmatic approach is to:

  1. Protest entries by filing a CBP protest (CF-19 form) within 180 days of liquidation, preserving refund rights if the courts rule favorably;
  2. Model both scenarios in your tariff calculator so you know your margin at 20% IEEPA and at 0% IEEPA;
  3. Follow the Supreme Court cert petition in V.O.S. Selections—if granted, a ruling could come before the end of 2026.

Strategic Mitigation: How to Legally Reduce Your Tariff Exposure

Most articles stop at "here's the rate." This one goes further: here's how to pay less—lawfully.

Foreign-Trade Zones (FTZs) and Bonded Warehouses

Foreign-Trade Zones offer a legal mechanism to defer, and in some cases reduce, tariff exposure. Goods admitted to an FTZ are not "entered" into US commerce until they are transferred to CBP territory. If you store Chinese goods in an FTZ, assemble them into finished products, and then export them—no duty is ever paid. If you import components into an FTZ, manufacture them into a product that qualifies for preferential treatment (or a different classification with lower duty), you pay duty only on the finished product's classification at the time of entry. According to the National Association of Foreign-Trade Zones, more than $800 billion in goods move through FTZs annually, and duty deferral alone saves importers an estimated $500 million per year in carrying costs.

The distinction is between admission (into an FTZ) and entry (into US commerce). Duties accrue only at entry—meaning you can hold inventory in an FTZ indefinitely while waiting for tariff rates to shift, or while pursuing an exclusion petition, without paying anything.

Tariff Classification Optimization

Classification optimization is not misclassification—it is selecting the most accurate, best-fit lawful HTS code among legitimate alternatives. For example, a "multifunction device" may classify as a printer (duty 0%) or as a copier with scanning capability (duty 2.5%), depending on primary function, per GRI 3(b) rules. Product engineering can even be adjusted to influence classification: changing the primary function, component materials, or assembly stage can lawfully move a product into a lower-duty basket. Getting this right requires working with a licensed customs broker and, for borderline cases, securing a binding ruling from CBP.

Valuation Strategy: First Sale vs. Transaction Value

If you buy through a middleman/trading company, consider using the "first sale" valuation rule with your supplier's manufacturer price rather than the full transaction price you pay the intermediary. The first-sale rule is legal if the goods are destined for the US and arm's-length pricing is documented. For a shipment valued at $500,000 at the reseller level but only $350,000 at the manufacturer level, first-sale valuation on a 40% combined duty saves $60,000 per shipment—an enormous, entirely lawful reduction.

Duty Drawback

If you import Chinese goods, pay duties, and then export the finished product—or a product incorporating those goods—you can claim duty drawback of up to 99% of the paid duties under 19 U.S.C. § 1313. The drawback filing window is five years from importation. With duties at 30–170%, drawback has moved from an afterthought to a profit-center function for many importers.

Q: What percentage tariff will I pay on goods imported from China in 2026—is it 20%, 30%, or 100%?

A: There is no single rate. You pay the sum of at least four concurrent layers: the 20% IEEPA fentanyl tariff, Section 301 rates (0–100% by product), Section 232 national security tariffs (25–50% global on steel, aluminum, autos), and any applicable Section 201 or AD/CVD duties. By product category, combined rates range from roughly 27.5% on basic consumer goods to 45% on electronics, 70% on auto parts, 95% on steel, 145% on EVs, and up to 170% on semiconductors. The only way to know your exact rate is to identify your 10-digit HTS code and stack all applicable subheadings.

Q: Which specific product categories face the highest combined China tariffs in 2026?

A: Semiconductors lead at approximately 170% (20% IEEPA + 50% Section 301 + 100% Section 232). Electric vehicles follow at ~145% (20% + 100% + 25%). Steel products face ~95% (20% + 25% Section 301 + 50% Section 232). Solar cells face ~84%+ (20% + 50% Section 301 + ~14% Section 201). The largest cost exposure is greatest for these sectors because they combine multiple high-rate layers simultaneously.

Q: How do I find my exact tariff rate if my product code isn't obvious?

A: Start with the USITC HTS database (hts.usitc.gov) and search by product description keywords. For ambiguous products, search CBP's binding ruling database (ROSS) to see how similar items have been classified. When in doubt, file a formal request for a binding ruling with CBP (often $200–$500 and 30–90 days processing). Work with a licensed customs broker for complex products—the wrong HTS code can mean a 30–100 percentage point rate difference.

Q: Are tariff exclusions still available for Chinese goods, and can I apply for one?

A: Yes, but with constraints. Only ~125 of the original 1,544 Section 301 exclusions remain active through end-2026. You can file a reinstatement petition if your product previously had an exclusion that expired, or a new petition if you can demonstrate severe economic harm. Approval rates for new petitions have been below 5% since 2023. Separately, the IEEPA 20% tariff currently has no exclusion mechanism. There is a separate Section 232 exclusion process through the Department of Commerce for steel, aluminum, and semiconductor products.

Q: Will the Geneva Deal's 30% rate hold through 2026, or should I expect tariffs to rise?

A: The base case (roughly 60% probability) holds the current framework through 2026, maintaining the 20% IEEPA layer plus a 10% additional rate on most goods. But two risks remain: escalation (~25% probability) if the carve-out sectors snap back to 100%+ rates or new Section 232 rules fully implement on semiconductors and copper, raising those sectors to 170%+; and unwinding (~15% probability) if federal courts strike down the IEEPA authority entirely, eliminating the 20% layer retroactively. Importers should model all three scenarios before pricing contracts.

Q: If I move production to Vietnam, Mexico, or India, what will my tariffs be instead?

A: For most product categories, dramatically lower. Vietnam's MFN rates average 5–10%; Mexico's USMCA rates are 0% for qualifying goods; India's MFN rates average 7–15%. However, Section 232 tariffs on steel, aluminum, autos, and semiconductors are global—not China-specific—so those products face 25–50%+ regardless of origin. You also must meet strict country-of-origin rules (substantial transformation) or CBP will reclassify your goods as Chinese-origin and apply the full China stack.
