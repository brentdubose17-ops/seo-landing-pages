If you import goods from China into the United States, 2026 is shaping up to be the most volatile tariff year since the trade war began in 2018. The average MFN tariff on Chinese goods now sits at 19.3%, but that headline number hides massive swings depending on your product category. Some electronics face combined rates approaching 40%, while a narrow window of auto parts may see temporary relief.

This guide breaks down exactly what changed in 2026, how to calculate your total landed cost, and where the hidden opportunities—and traps—lie. 2026 Tariff Rate Schedule Changes: What Actually Changed

The U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) implemented several adjustments effective January 1, 2026, following the statutory four-year review of Section 301 tariffs initiated in 2024. Two major changes dominate the landscape: targeted increases on electronics and machinery, and a phased reduction on certain electric vehicle (EV) battery components.

Section 301 List 4A Electronics Tariff Increase

The most impactful change for 2026 is the increase of Section 301 tariffs on List 4A electronics from 7.5% to 25%. This applies to HTS chapters 8471 (computers), 8473 (computer parts), 8525 (telecommunications equipment), and 8542 (semiconductors). The increase took effect January 1, 2026, and is not subject to any exclusion process as of May 2026.

Real-world impact: A laptop imported from China with an MFN rate of 0% (duty-free under WTO agreements) now faces a 25% Section 301 tariff. Combined with the 0.3464% Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF), the total duty on a $1,000 laptop jumps from $3.46 in 2025 to $253.46 in 2026. That’s a 7,200% increase in tariff cost on that single item.

Section 232 Steel and Aluminum Continuation

The additional 25% tariff on steel and aluminum products from China under Section 232 remains in full effect with no exemptions in 2026. This applies to HTS chapters 72, 73, and 76. For Chinese-origin steel, the effective rate is the MFN rate (typically 0% to 3%) plus 25% Section 232 plus applicable Section 301 rates (7.5% to 25%).

Example: Chinese steel wire rod (HTS 7213.91.30) with an MFN rate of 0% now carries a 25% Section 232 tariff plus a 25% Section 301 tariff, for a total of 50% plus MPF. On a $50,000 shipment, that’s $25,000 in tariffs alone.

The Hidden 2026 Tariff Trap: EV Battery Phase-Out Window

Most articles focus on increases, but 2026 also includes a temporary reduction opportunity. As part of the 2024 Section 301 review, certain EV battery components (HTS 8507.60) saw a phased reduction from 25% to 7.5% effective April 1, 2026, through September 30, 2026. This six-month window applies specifically to lithium-ion battery cells and modules used in electric vehicles, provided the importer can certify the components are not produced by entities on the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA) entity list.

Why competitors miss this: Most tariff analysis focuses on rate increases. The USTR quarterly updates are rarely tracked by general importers. If you import EV batteries or components, you have until September 30, 2026, to lock in a 17.5% rate reduction. After that, rates revert to 25% through 2027.

To take advantage, file a Section 301 exclusion request using the USTR online portal and ensure your supply chain documentation is UFLPA-compliant. This is not automatic—you must apply and receive approval.

Calculation Methodology: How to Compute Total Landed Cost in 2026

Most importers only calculate the tariff rate. That’s a mistake. Your total landed cost includes at least five components: the MFN duty, Section 301 tariff, Section 232 tariff (if applicable), antidumping/countervailing duties (AD/CVD), and the MPF. Here is the exact formula for 2026.

Step 1: Determine the Correct HTS Code

Use the Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTS) published by the U.S. International Trade Commission (USITC). Do not rely on old codes from 2025. The 2026 HTS revisions include new subheadings for semiconductor devices and EV batteries. Misclassification can result in penalties of up to 20% of the value of the imported merchandise under 19 U.S.C. § 1592.

Actionable advice: Use the CBP’s HTS search tool or tariffcalculator2026.com’s built-in lookup. Verify your code against the 2026 HTSUS PDF update published January 1, 2026.

Step 2: Identify All Applicable Duty Rates

For Chinese imports, you need to layer up to four rates:

  • MFN rate: The base column 1 rate for China. Average 19.3% but ranges from 0% to 37.5% for certain textiles.
  • Section 301 rate: 25% on List 4A items (most electronics, machinery, apparel, toys) or 7.5% on List 4B items (some chemicals, plastics). Check the USTR annex for your specific HTS.
  • Section 232 rate: 25% on steel and aluminum products, regardless of MFN.
  • AD/CVD rates: Product-specific. For Chinese solar panels, the antidumping rate is 24.1% (unchanged from 2025). For Chinese tires, the rate is 44.6%.

Step 3: Add the Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF)

The MPF is 0.3464% of the declared value, with a minimum of $27.23 and a maximum of $528.33 per shipment (2026 rates, unchanged from 2025). This applies to all formal entries valued over $2,500. For informal entries ($800 to $2,500), the MPF is capped at $2.00.

Step 4: Compute Total Duty Paid

Formula: Total Duty = (Declared Value × MFN Rate) + (Declared Value × Section 301 Rate) + (Declared Value × Section 232 Rate) + (Declared Value × AD/CVD Rate) + MPF

Example calculation for a Chinese laptop (HTS 8471.30.01):

  • Declared value: $1,200
  • MFN rate: 0%
  • Section 301 rate: 25%
  • Section 232: 0% (not steel/aluminum)
  • AD/CVD: 0% (computers not subject)
  • MPF: 0.3464% of $1,200 = $4.16
  • Total duty: ($1,200 × 25%) + $4.16 = $300 + $4.16 = $304.16

Compare to 2025: ($1,200 × 7.5%) + $4.16 = $90 + $4.16 = $94.16. That’s a 223% increase in duty cost.

Step 5: Include Duty Drawback Potential

If you export the imported Chinese goods or use them to manufacture products for export, you can recover 99% of the duties paid under the 2026 duty drawback program. This is unchanged from 2025. File Form 7551 with CBP within three years of importation.

De Minimis vs. Formal Entry: Cost Impact in 2026

The de minimis threshold remains unchanged at $800 per shipment in 2026. Shipments valued at $800 or less enter duty-free and without formal entry requirements. Shipments over $800 require a formal entry and are subject to all applicable tariffs and fees.

Shipment Value Entry Type Tariffs & Fees Example Cost (Laptop, $1,200)

Actionable advice: If you import small quantities, splitting shipments into sub-$800 values can save significant tariff costs. However, CBP scrutinizes “split shipments” from the same supplier to the same recipient within a short timeframe as potential fraud. Keep shipments separated by at least 30 days or use different importers.

What Happens if You Misdeclare the HTS Code in 2026?

Misclassification is the number one cause of customs penalties. Under 19 U.S.C. § 1592, if CBP determines you negligently misclassified goods to avoid higher tariffs, you face a penalty of up to 20% of the dutiable value. If the misclassification is deemed fraudulent, the penalty can reach 80% of the value.

Real case: In 2025, a major electronics importer was fined $2.3 million for classifying laptops under a 0% MFN code for computer parts (HTS 8473) instead of the correct 8471 code with 25% Section 301 tariffs. CBP caught the discrepancy during a post-entry audit.

To avoid this, use binding rulings from CBP or consult a licensed customs broker. The cost of a ruling (approximately $500 to $2,000) is negligible compared to a potential penalty.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How do I calculate the exact tariff for my Chinese product in 2026?

A: First, find the correct 10-digit HTS code using the 2026 HTSUS. Then identify the MFN rate, any Section 301 rate (25% for List 4A, 7.5% for List 4B), and any Section 232 or AD/CVD rates. Multiply each rate by the declared value, add the MPF (0.3464% with caps), and sum them. Use tariffcalculator2026.com for an automated calculation.

Q: Are there any new tariff exclusions or exceptions for small businesses in 2026?

A: No blanket exclusions exist for small businesses. The only exclusion opportunity in 2026 is the temporary reduction on EV battery components (HTS 8507.60) from April 1 to September 30, 2026, which requires a USTR exclusion request. Small businesses can also benefit from the de minimis threshold ($800 per shipment) for duty-free entry of small orders.

Q: Will tariffs on Chinese electronics increase or decrease in 2026?

A: They increased significantly. Section 301 tariffs on List 4A electronics (computers, semiconductors, telecom equipment) rose from 7.5% to 25% effective January 1, 2026. No decreases are scheduled for the remainder of 2026, except the temporary EV battery reduction. Expect rates to remain at 25% through at least 2027.

Q: What is the difference between MFN and Section 301 tariffs for Chinese goods in 2026?

A: MFN (Most Favored Nation) is the base tariff rate that applies to all WTO members, including China. The average MFN rate for Chinese goods is 19.3%. Section 301 tariffs are additional duties imposed specifically on Chinese goods due to the 2018 trade investigation. In 2026, most Section 301 rates are 25% on List 4A items and 7.5% on List 4B items. You must pay both MFN and Section 301 rates on the same import.

Q: How does the 2026 tariff rate impact my total landed cost vs. 2025?

A: For most Chinese imports on Section 301 List 4A, your total landed cost increased by 17.5 percentage points in tariff rate alone. For a $100,000 shipment of electronics, that’s an additional $17,500 in duty. For apparel with a 19.3% MFN rate, the combined rate jumped from 26.8% in 2025 to 44.3% in 2026, adding $17,500 per $100,000. Factor in potential AD/CVD rates and MPF for the full picture.

Q: Can I use a tariff classification code to avoid higher rates in 2026?

A: Deliberately using an incorrect HTS code to avoid tariffs is illegal and carries penalties of up to 80% of the goods’ value for fraud. However, legitimate tariff engineering—choosing a product design that falls under a different, lower-rate HTS code—is legal. For example, importing a “smartwatch” under HTS 9102 (watches, 0% MFN) instead of 8471 (computers, 25% Section 301) may save tariffs if the product meets the legal definition. Consult a customs expert before making design or classification changes.
