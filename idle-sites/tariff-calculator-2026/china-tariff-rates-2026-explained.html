China Tariff Rates 2026 Explained: The Complete Stacking Guide for Importers

There is no single "China tariff rate" in 2026. The duty owed on a Chinese-origin product is calculated from a stack of authorities — base MFN duty under the HTSUS, Section 301, Section 232, IEEPA-based fentanyl and reciprocal tariffs, and antidumping/countervailing duties — and those layers are additive on the same customs value, not compounded. As a result, an ordinary $100 import from China can carry anywhere from roughly $10 to more than $170 in total duties depending entirely on its 10-digit HTSUS code and product category. The 2026 escalations hit non-EV lithium batteries, natural graphite, and medical gloves, which moved from 7.5% or 0% up to 25% on January 1, 2026. The $800 Section 321 de minimis exemption is gone for China-origin goods — and was suspended globally in 2025 — meaning every commercial parcel now requires formal or informal entry, duty, and Merchandise Processing Fee.

Trump, No. 24-1287): IEEPA does not authorise the President to impose tariffs. Section 232, Section 301 and Section 338 remain in force. A fifth mechanism is pending in Congress: H.R. 5334, passed by the Senate 86–11 and awaiting a House vote, would add up to 500 percent duties on Russian goods and up to 100 percent secondary duties, and it is not law as of September 15, 2026. Why "The China Tariff Rate" Is the Wrong Question in 2026

Most tariff trackers publish a country average. That number is analytically interesting and operationally useless. An importer of knit cotton T-shirts and an importer of lithium-ion battery cells may both source from Shenzhen, but in 2026 they face radically different landed cost structures.

The correct unit of analysis is the 10-digit HTSUS subheading plus country of origin plus product-specific trade actions. Everything else — the headline averages, the diplomatic headlines, the "47.5% average effective tariff" estimates from think tanks like the Peterson Institute for International Economics — is context, not a calculation.

The 2026 Tariff Stack: Five Layers, Additive Math

Duties from separate legal authorities are applied to the same entered customs value. They do not compound — you do not pay 25% and then 50% on the result. You pay 25% plus 50%, both computed against the base value. This distinction matters enormously at high rates.

Layer Legal Authority Typical Rate Range Scope Stacking Position
Base duty HTSUS Column 1 General (MFN) 0% – 35%+ All imports Always applies first
Section 301 Trade Act of 1974, as amended 7.5% – 100% ~$300B+ of Chinese imports across Lists 1–4A Adds to base duty
Section 232 Trade Expansion Act of 1962 25% – 50% Steel, aluminum, copper and derivative articles Adds; applies globally, not just China
IEEPA (fentanyl) International Emergency Economic Powers Act 10% (reduced from 20% in Nov 2025) China-origin goods broadly Adds; legal status subject to litigation — verify
IEEPA (reciprocal) International Emergency Economic Powers Act 10% baseline; 24% suspended through Nov 2026 Nearly all origins Adds; carved out for Section 232 goods
AD/CVD Tariff Act of 1930 Frequently 50% – 250%+ Product-specific (solar, nails, plywood, furniture) Adds; case-by-case by scope order

The stacking math that changes buying decisions

Take a $100 customs value shipment of a steel derivative article from China, entered in 2026: Section 301 List 3 at 25%, Section 232 steel derivative at 50%, IEEPA fentanyl at 10%, plus 10% reciprocal (assuming the suspension on the higher tier holds). Effective duty load: 95% — $95 on a $100 item. Add MPF and brokerage and the landed cost is more than double the invoice.

Now swap the product for an electric vehicle. Section 301 on Chinese EVs rose from 25% to 100% in 2024. Stacked with the IEEPA layers, that is a duty load above 120% before any AD/CVD. On a $30,000 vehicle, that is north of $36,000 in duty — which is precisely why almost no Chinese EVs are commercially imported into the United States today.

Section 301 in 2026: What Actually Changed on January 1

Section 301 is the single largest China-specific tariff program, and it operates on a list system. The four-year statutory review that concluded in 2024 produced a phased escalation schedule, with the final tranche landing on January 1, 2026.

The baseline structure remains: List 1, 2, and 3 at 25%; List 4A at 7.5%. List 4B — announced in 2019 — never took effect.

The 2024–2026 escalations are the part most importers get wrong, because the effective dates are staggered across three calendar years. Here is the actual schedule:

Critical minerals 0% 25% 25% 25%

The practical consequence: if you modeled your 2025 landed costs on non-EV lithium batteries, natural graphite, or nitrile exam gloves, your 2026 model is understated by 17.5 percentage points on the Section 301 layer alone. On graphite anode material — where the base MFN rate was zero — the increase is a full 25 points of net new cost.

Section 232: the global layer that catches Chinese goods twice

Section 232 steel and aluminum tariffs were raised to 50% in June 2025, and copper followed at 50% in August 2025. Unlike Section 301, Section 232 is origin-neutral — it applies to steel and aluminum content regardless of where it was melted or poured, though China-origin material typically triggers additional Section 301 layers on top.

Critically, Section 232 derivative rules can apply duty to the value of the metal content in a finished article — not the full customs value. A steel-framed office chair may owe 50% on the declared steel content rather than 50% on the invoice price. Getting the content value wrong in either direction is one of the most common causes of CBP post-entry adjustments.

IEEPA Fentanyl and Reciprocal Tariffs: Verify Before You Model

The IEEPA-based tariffs have moved repeatedly and been subject to active litigation. The fentanyl-related tariff on China-origin goods began at 20% in March 2025 and was reduced to 10% in November 2025. The reciprocal tariff carries a 10% baseline, with the higher 24% tier suspended through November 2026 under the current truce framework.

Because these authorities rest on emergency powers rather than statutory trade law, their rates and even their validity can change faster than any annual tariff schedule. Build your landed cost model with the IEEPA layers as variables, not constants, and re-verify against the Federal Register before committing to a landed-cost quote or a long-term purchase order.

AD/CVD: The Layer That Ends Businesses

Antidumping and countervailing duty orders are the most under-modeled risk in Chinese sourcing. These are not percentage points — they are multiples. Chinese solar cells have carried combined AD/CVD exposure above 250%. Steel nails from China have exceeded 200%. Hardwood plywood from China has exceeded 200%.

AD/CVD is administered by case and by scope. A product can be pulled into an order because it is "merely" a minor modification of a covered article. And unlike Section 301, AD/CVD liability can be retroactive — CBP can suspend liquidation and later assess the difference, plus interest and penalties, years after entry.

Practical rule: before placing any order from China above roughly $25,000, run the HTS code against the ITC and Commerce scope databases. If there is an active order, get a written scope opinion or a scope ruling. This is the single highest-ROI diligence step available.

De Minimis Is Gone: Every Parcel Now Needs an Entry

The Section 321 de minimis exemption — the $800 per-shipment duty-free threshold — ended for China and Hong Kong-origin goods on May 2, 2025. The suspension was then extended globally on August 29, 2025.

For any importer who built a business on direct-to-consumer parcel shipping, this is the structural change of the decade. Every commercial shipment now requires:

  • A formal or informal customs entry, filed by a licensed broker or the carrier
  • Applicable duty at the full stacked rate
  • Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF), currently 0.3464% ad valorem subject to annual minimum and maximum caps
  • Harbor Maintenance Fee (HMF) at 0.125% on ocean shipments
  • Carrier or postal handling surcharges for clearance

The MPF minimum and maximum are adjusted annually for inflation; confirm the current-year figures in CBP's Federal Register notice before finalizing unit economics. On a small parcel, the MPF minimum alone can exceed the duty on a low-value item — which is why sub-$20 unit economics broke for many China-direct sellers.

How to Calculate Landed Cost Correctly: The Decision Path

Use this sequence. Skipping a step is how importers end up with surprise liquidations.

  1. Classify to 10 digits. Not 6, not 8. Chapter 99 special headings for Section 301 and 232 are separate classifications layered onto the base code.
  2. Establish country of origin. Origin is determined by substantial transformation, not by port of export or invoice address.
  3. Check Section 301 lists. Identify the applicable list and the current rate for your subheading.
  4. Check Section 232. Are you importing a covered metal, or a derivative article containing one? Determine metal content value.
  5. Check IEEPA layers. Fentanyl and reciprocal tariffs, with current rates and any product carve-outs.
  6. Check AD/CVD. Active order? Scope match? Case number and cash deposit rate?
  7. Apply exclusions. Confirm the exclusion is still valid and the HTSUS heading is correctly cited on the entry.
  8. Compute duty on customs value. Sum all layers, then add MPF, HMF, brokerage, and freight.

The formula:

Landed Cost = Customs Value + (Customs Value × Sum of All Duty Rates) + MPF + HMF + Brokerage + Freight + Insurance

Worked example: $10,000 customs value, 25% Section 301 + 10% IEEPA + 10% reciprocal = 45% duty = $4,500. MPF at 0.3464% = $34.64 (if above the annual minimum). HMF on ocean freight = $12.50. Brokerage $125. Total landed cost ≈ $14,672 — a 46.7% premium over invoice before freight.

Exclusions: What Remains and How to Track It

Section 301 exclusions have narrowed considerably. The 352 reinstated exclusions that had been extended through May 31, 2025 have largely lapsed, and the 2024 additions — including 164 solar manufacturing equipment exclusions — are product-specific with defined expiration dates.

Exclusion Type Typical Term Key Requirement
Section 301 product exclusions 1 year or specified end date Exact HTSUS 10-digit match; exclusion number cited on entry
Solar manufacturing equipment Through specified 2025–2026 windows End-use certification may be required
Section 232 derivative carve-outs Per proclamation Metal content value documentation
First sale valuation Ongoing Bona fide sale for export; full documentation trail
AD/CVD scope exclusion Case-specific Scope ruling or certification

First sale valuation deserves separate attention. If you buy from a trading company that buys from a Chinese factory, you may be able to declare the factory-to-trader price rather than the price you paid. On a 95% duty stack, reducing customs value by 20% cuts duty by nearly as much. The documentation burden is real — you need the full transaction chain — but the savings are proportional to your rate stack, which in 2026 is unusually high.

Country-of-Origin Shifting: Why Vietnam and Mexico Often Fail

The most expensive mistake in 2026 sourcing strategy is assuming that moving final assembly to Vietnam or Mexico changes origin. It usually does not.

Under U.S. substantial transformation doctrine, origin changes only if the article emerges from processing with a new name, character, or use. Screwing together Chinese-manufactured battery modules in Vietnam does not create a Vietnamese battery. Cutting Chinese fabric and sewing it in Mexico may create a Mexican garment — but if the fabric is Chinese, the textile rules may still pull you back, and USMCA rules of origin are stricter than the substantial transformation test.

Transshipment — shipping through a third country without genuine processing — is not an origin strategy at all. It is customs fraud under 19 U.S.C. § 1592, exposing importers to penalties equal to the domestic value of the merchandise, plus possible criminal referral. CBP's trade enforcement posture in 2026 includes substantially expanded use of trade-data analytics to detect exactly this pattern.

Trade Context: Why This Matters More Than the Headlines Suggest

China's share of U.S. goods imports fell from 21.6% in 2017 to roughly 13.3% in 2024, and U.S. goods imports from China totaled approximately $438 billion in 2024 against a goods trade deficit of about $295 billion. Section 301 tariffs originally covered roughly $350 billion of Chinese imports; after exclusions and shifting trade patterns, the actively covered base is closer to $300 billion.

Those numbers describe a structurally decoupled supply chain. For an individual importer, the operative fact is simpler: the products still coming from China in 2026 are, disproportionately, the ones that are hardest to move — batteries, graphite, critical minerals, medical consumables, and specialized industrial inputs. Those are also, by design, the categories with the highest 2026 rates.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is the total tariff rate on Chinese goods in 2026?

A: There is no single rate. A Chinese-origin product's total duty is the sum of its MFN base rate, the applicable Section 301 rate (7.5%–100%), any Section 232 rate (25%–50%), IEEPA fentanyl and reciprocal layers (currently around 20% combined), and any AD/CVD cash deposit rate. Real-world combined loads range from roughly 30% on low-list consumer goods to over 120% on EVs and syringes, with AD/CVD cases pushing above 250%. Always calculate at the 10-digit HTSUS level.

Q: Do Section 301, Section 232, IEEPA, and reciprocal tariffs stack?

A: Yes. They are separate legal authorities, and each is assessed on the same entered customs value. They are additive, not compounded — a 25% duty plus a 50% duty equals 75% of value, not 87.5%. Section 232 goods are generally carved out of the reciprocal tariff, so the reciprocal layer usually does not apply on top of a metal-specific 232 rate, but Section 301 and IEEPA fentanyl layers do.

Q: Did the 2026 Section 301 increases take effect for batteries, graphite, and gloves?

A: Yes. Effective January 1, 2026, the Section 301 rate rose to 25% for non-EV lithium-ion batteries (from 7.5%), natural graphite (from 0%), and medical gloves including surgical and examination gloves (from 7.5%). These were the final scheduled tranche of the four-year review. EV and EV-battery increases, solar at 50%, semiconductors at 50%, and syringes and needles at 100% had already taken effect in 2024 and 2025.

Q: Is the $800 de minimis exemption still available for China?

A: No. The Section 321 de minimis exemption ended for China and Hong Kong-origin shipments on May 2, 2025, and was suspended globally on August 29, 2025. Every commercial shipment now requires a customs entry, duty at the full stacked rate, MPF, and — for ocean freight — HMF. Gifts and samples are not blanket-exempt; they still require entry and duty unless a specific provision applies.

Q: How do I calculate landed cost for a product made in China?

A: Classify to the 10-digit HTSUS code, confirm country of origin under substantial transformation rules, then sum every applicable duty layer against the same customs value. Add MPF (0.3464% ad valorem, subject to annual minimum and maximum caps), HMF (0.125% on ocean), brokerage, freight, and insurance. Landed Cost = Customs Value + (Customs Value × Total Duty Rate) + Fees. If your calculation produces a single percentage rather than a rate stack, you are missing layers.

Q: Can I avoid China tariffs by transshipping through Vietnam or Mexico?

A: Not legitimately, unless the goods undergo substantial transformation — meaning they emerge with a new name, character, or use. Simple repackaging, minor assembly, or relabeling does not change origin. Transshipment without genuine processing is customs fraud under 19 U.S.C. § 1592, carrying penalties up to the domestic value of the merchandise. CBP actively uses trade-data analytics to identify these patterns, and civil penalties plus interest can exceed the tariff savings many times over.
