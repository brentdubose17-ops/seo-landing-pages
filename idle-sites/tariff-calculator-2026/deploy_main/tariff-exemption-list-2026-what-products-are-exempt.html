Tariff Exemption List 2026: What Products Still Enter the U.S. Duty-Free

The 2026 tariff exemption landscape is not a static list — it is a conditional logic puzzle driven by five variables: country of origin, 10-digit HS code, free trade agreement eligibility, end-use, and shipment value. As of May 2026, the U.S. maintains zero new general Section 301 exclusions for China-origin goods, only 34 targeted exclusions remain active, and the $800 de minimis exemption no longer applies to Chinese imports. However, genuine duty-free entry still exists through USMCA, Section 232 quota-based country exceptions, Foreign-Trade Zone re-exports, and narrow end-use provisions. An estimated 68% of U.S. imports from Mexico still enter duty-free, while roughly 3,500 product lines once covered by the lapsed Generalized System of Preferences (GSP) remain fully dutiable. The bottom line: importers who treat exemptions as a five-step verification process — not a lookup table — can legally save 5% to 100% in landed costs, while those who guess risk liquidated damages of two times the value of the goods.

The 2026 Reality Check: "Exempt" Is Conditional, Not a Static List

Most tariff content published in 2026 tells importers one of two incomplete stories: either "all China exclusions are dead" or "here is a PDF-style list of duty-free products." Both are misleading. The truth is that every exemption currently in force depends on how a product moves through a five-variable decision framework: origin, HS code, FTA status, end-use, and value threshold. Change one variable and a duty-free product becomes dutiable — and vice versa.

This is why the smartest importers no longer ask "what is exempt?" They ask "under what conditions is my specific shipment exempt?" The difference is worth real money. A Section 232 steel exclusion, for example, only applies when the steel was melted and poured in a qualifying country. Change the melt source and your "exempt" steel suddenly carries a 25% duty. Similarly, a product imported from Mexico under USMCA is duty-free only if it meets the Regional Value Content (RVC) threshold — typically 50% to 60% of the value must originate within the trade bloc.

Throughout this article, we will treat the 2026 tariff exemption landscape as a decision tree, not a list. By the end, you will know exactly which variables to verify — and which "exemptions" will trigger penalties if claimed incorrectly.

Section 301 China Tariffs: What Exclusions Survive in 2026?

For importers of Chinese goods, 2026 is the year the exclusion pipeline ran dry. The tariff structure itself remains massive: List 1, 2, and 3 continue to apply a 25% tariff on roughly $250 billion of Chinese goods, while List 4A carries a 100% tariff on approximately $180 billion of additional products. List 4B, originally scheduled for tariffs of 7.5% to 25%, has been partially rescinded and repriced — selected electric vehicles, solar panels, rare earths, and semiconductors now face 25% to 50% duties.

What changed is the exclusion process. During the three original Section 301 exclusion rounds, the USTR granted exclusions at steadily declining rates: 57% of requests in Round 1 (Lists 1–2, 2018–2019), 38% in Round 2 (List 3), and just 28% in Round 3 (List 4A). In 2026, that relief mechanism is effectively closed. Zero new general exclusions are being issued, and only 34 targeted exclusions continue past late 2025 — almost exclusively trace amounts of medical inputs and specialty industrial chemicals where no U.S. or third-country alternative exists.

Can importers still apply for a Section 301 exclusion in 2026? Technically, the USTR Section 301 Exclusions Portal remains open for comments and status checks, and the agency has not formally abolished the mechanism. But the practical answer is no: the USTR has not granted a new general exclusion since 2021, and the 2026 trade policy environment is explicitly hostile to new carve-outs for Chinese goods. The only active exclusions are the 34 legacy items, each tied to a specific 10-digit HTS code and a specific approved U.S. importer.

The Circumvention Enforcement Blowback

The bigger 2026 story is what happens when importers try to route around Section 301 tariffs through third countries. U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) and the USTR have ramped up circumvention investigations targeting transshipment through Mexico and Vietnam. If CBP determines that a product shipped from Mexico is merely a pass-through of Chinese-origin content, the Section 301 tariff applies retroactively — and the importer faces penalties regardless of the Mexican certificate of origin.

The enforcement signal is clear: in the first quarter of 2026 alone, CBP issued more than 200 penalty notices tied to Section 301 circumvention, a 300% increase over 2024 levels. The lesson for importers is to document the substantial transformation that occurs in each country of the supply chain — because a Mexican invoice and a made-in-Mexico label no longer mean anything without verifiable production records.

Section 232 Steel and Aluminum: The "Melted and Poured" De Facto Exemptions

Unlike Section 301, the Section 232 program still offers a genuine, if narrow, path to exemption. Steel carries a 25% tariff and aluminum carries a 25% tariff (raised from 10% in January 2020). But the program has always been more of a quota-to-tariff system than a blanket duty. Countries that negotiated tariff-rate quotas (TRQs) — notably Argentina, Australia, and Brazil — can still ship specific volumes of steel and aluminum into the United States duty-free or at reduced rates.

For importers, the practical implication is this: a steel product that would normally face a 25% Section 232 duty is technically exempt if it originates from a TRQ country within its quota allocation. This is one of the most under-used "exemption list" items in 2026, precisely because it is origin-specific rather than product-specific. The same HS code from China or Japan faces the full 25%; the same product from Australia enters duty-free within quota limits.

The Melted and Poured Standard

The critical conditionality for Section 232 exemptions is the "melted and poured" rule. Effective 2025–2026, CBP requires importers to document the actual melt and pour location of the steel or aluminum — not the location of the finishing work. If Chinese steel is shipped to Vietnam, rolled into coils, and then exported to the United States, it is still Chinese-origin steel under Section 232 and faces the full tariff plus potential circumvention penalties. Only steel actually melted and poured in a qualifying country can claim the exemption.

The exclusion request process itself remains a statistical long shot. Since 2018, importers have filed roughly 180,000 Section 232 exclusion requests. The approval rate is approximately 30%, and the average USTR decision time is 204 days — with a realistic range of 100 to 300 days. For a product with annual duty exposure under $50,000, the 20 to 60 hours of internal staff time required to file an exclusion simply does not make financial sense. The rule of thumb in 2026: pursue a Section 232 exclusion only if your annual duty savings exceed $50,000.

The $800 De Minimis Exemption in 2026: Still Alive — With a China-Sized Hole

The de minimis exemption — the rule allowing shipments valued at $800 or less to enter the United States duty-free and without formal entry — was the workhorse of e-commerce trade for a decade. In 2024, approximately 1.4 billion de minimis parcels entered the United States duty-free, with an estimated 75% of that volume originating in China. Then came the May 2025 executive order closing the de minimis loophole for China-origin goods.

That change was codified in the new HS code 9808.00.40, which now covers Chinese-origin de minimis shipments. These goods are subject to full Section 301 tariffs, Section 301 processing requirements, and formal entry procedures — no exceptions for low value. The practical effect: if you are importing a $50 parcel of Chinese-made goods, you now pay 25% to 100% tariff plus a processing fee, and the shipment requires formal customs entry.

For non-China origins, the $800 rule still works exactly as before. The ceiling remains $800 per person per day, and it is calculated on the value of the goods alone — shipping costs are excluded from the de minimis valuation threshold (though they are included in the transaction value once the goods cross above the threshold). U.S. importers can still receive up to $800 per day in duty-free goods from Mexico, Canada, the EU, Japan, and other non-covered countries.

Which Countries Are Next on the De Minimis Closure List?

The 2026 policy projection is unambiguous: China is not the only target. Trade analysts expect the de minimis closure to extend to additional "covered countries" — with Mexico and Vietnam at the top of the list — by late 2026 or early 2027. Both countries are significant transshipment hubs for Chinese goods, and CBP's shared KYC data initiatives are already flagging e-commerce importers who cycle de minimis entries through these origins. If your supply chain relies on sub-$800 shipments from Mexico or Vietnam, build a compliance plan that assumes the de minimis route will disappear.

USMCA and Free Trade Agreements: The Duty-Free Highway That Still Works in 2026

For importers willing to do the documentation work, free trade agreements remain the most reliable route to a genuine tariff exemption. The United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA) is the heavyweight champion of this category: approximately 68% of U.S. imports from Mexico enter duty-free in 2026. The key test is not where the product is shipped from — it is where the product's value is created.

The USMCA "substantial transformation" and RVC tests are strict. For autos, the RVC requirement is 60%; for most other goods, it is 50% to 60% of the product's value must originate within the USMCA region. Importers must maintain a Certificate of Origin and, in many cases, supporting production records. A product that merely passes through Mexico from China with light assembly will not qualify — and claiming USMCA preference without meeting the RVC test is one of the most common sources of CBP penalty actions.

The GSP Lapse: 3,500 Product Lines Still Waiting

On the down side, the Generalized System of Preferences remains lapsed — it expired on December 31, 2020, and every renewal bill introduced in 2024 and 2025 has failed. The GSP lapse affects roughly 3,500 HS lines covering chemicals, jewelry, hand tools, and select manufactured goods from qualifying developing countries. If GSP were renewed in 2026, the average duty savings for qualifying beneficiaries would be 5.6% — a meaningful margin for chemical importers and jewelry brands that have been paying full Most-Favored-Nation (MFN) duties for five consecutive years.

Until renewal passes, the practical advice is straightforward: do not price your 2026 landed costs assuming GSP will return. If a GSP revival bill gains traction, your tariff calculator will be your best friend for repricing — but ship your Q3 inventory under the assumption that duties remain in force.

The #1 Exemption Blocker: Your HS Code Is Wrong

Here is the most expensive mistake in the entire tariff system: an incorrect 10-digit HS code makes a legitimately exempt product look dutiable — and an incorrectly classified product can make a dutiable product look exempt. The second error is far worse, because it triggers penalties, interest, and liquidation damages. The first error simply costs you money every single shipment.

Consider the frozen chicken example. Frozen chicken nuggets — breaded and pre-cooked — fall under a different HTS chapter than raw frozen chicken parts. One classification is "prepared food" (Chapter 16), while the other is "meat" (Chapter 2). The tariff rates differ, the quota treatment differs, and the exemption availability differs. A U.S. importer who trusts a supplier's vague "it's chicken, it's duty-free" claim without verifying the 10-digit code is the classic 2026 penalty case.

The same logic applies to the 34 remaining Section 301 exclusions. Each exclusion is tied to a precise 10-digit HTS number. If your product is truly exempt but you classify it under a similar-but-wrong 10-digit code, you will pay duties you do not owe. If you classify under a code that matches an exclusion you do not qualify for, you face a false exemption claim — with penalties under 19 U.S.C. 1592 that start at two times the value of the goods. Getting the HS code right is not paperwork. It is the single highest-ROI step in the entire exemption process.

The 2026 Exemption Decision Tree: Five Steps to a Defensible Answer

Stop searching for a master exemption list. Instead, run every product through this five-step decision framework. Each step eliminates or confirms a potential exemption, and the output is a defensible answer for your specific shipment.

Step 1: Determine True Country of Origin

Origin is not where the product is shipped from — it is where the product underwent substantial transformation. For Section 301 purposes, CBP examines the country where the last substantial transformation occurred. Document every production stage with supplier declarations and production records. If any material processing occurred in China, assume Section 301 scrutiny will follow the product, even if it transits Mexico or Vietnam.

Step 2: Check FTA Eligibility (USMCA First)

If your origin is Mexico or Canada, verify USMCA eligibility using the RVC test. For most goods, 50% to 60% of the net cost must originate in the USMCA region. Obtain a valid Certificate of Origin from the exporter — but remember that the certificate alone does not create eligibility. Your production records must support it.

Step 3: Check HS-Specific Exclusions (Section 301 and Section 232)

Pull your product's precise 10-digit HS code. Cross-reference it against the 34 remaining Section 301 targeted exclusions and the active Section 232 exclusion list. If your code does not match exactly, the exclusion does not apply. A "similar" code is not an exemption — it is a penalty risk.

Step 4: Check De Minimis ($800) Eligibility

If your shipment value is $800 or less per person per day, and the origin is NOT China (and not a future "covered country"), the de minimis exemption still applies. Verify the shipment qualifies under customs valuation rules — remember that shipping costs are excluded from the $800 threshold calculation. For China-origin goods, do not attempt de minimis entry under HS 9808.00.40 unless you are fully prepared for formal entry and full duty payment.

Step 5: Check End-Use Provisions (Notes 4 and 5)

Finally, check whether your product qualifies for special end-use provisions. Aircraft parts imported for installation at U.S. airports, chemical feedstocks used in qualified manufacturing processes, and certain medical devices on the FDA shortage list can qualify for duty relief under HTS Chapter 98 end-use provisions. These are not well-publicized, but they are legitimate, fully enforceable exemptions that mainstream tariff calculators routinely ignore.

Run all five steps and you will arrive at one of two outcomes: a duty-free determination backed by documentation, or a dutiable determination with a known, calculable tariff rate. Both outcomes are useful. The first saves money; the second prevents penalties.

The Untapped Loophole: Foreign-Trade Zones and Re-Exports

One of the most powerful tariff exemptions in 2026 is also the least understood. Goods entering a U.S. Foreign-Trade Zone (FTZ) and then being re-exported to another country are 100% exempt from U.S. tariffs — no duty, no processing fee, no formal consumption entry. The goods are legally outside U.S. customs territory for tariff purposes while inside the FTZ.

This is not a niche technicality. The data is striking: only about 2% of U.S. importers use FTZs, despite roughly 25% of all U.S. imports being eligible for FTZ treatment. If your company imports components, performs light assembly or kitting in the United States, and re-exports a portion of the finished goods, an FTZ can eliminate U.S. tariffs on the re-exported share entirely — and defer tariffs on the portion that enters U.S. commerce.

The FTZ strategy is particularly powerful for Section 301 exposure. Chinese-origin components entering an FTZ are not subject to Section 301 tariffs at the time of entry; the tariff only applies if and when the goods are formally entered into U.S. commerce. If the assembled product is exported, the Section 301 duty is never paid. This is a legal, documented, and CBP-recognized exemption structure that most tariff calculators cannot model — but your landed-cost decisions should.

The Downside List: Products That Look Exempt but Will Trigger Penalties

Every tariff landscape has a "trap list" — products that were previously exempt but had their exemptions reversed with retroactive enforcement. In 2025–2026, the most dangerous items are:

  • EV battery components: Section 301 exclusions on certain battery cells and modules were revoked in 2025. CBP is actively auditing entries made under the expired exclusions and collecting back duties.
  • Certain semiconductor chips: Exclusion coverage narrowed sharply in late 2025. Importers who relied on prior exclusion IDs without verifying the 10-digit HTS code now face duty assessments plus interest.
  • Rare-earth magnets: Exclusions reversed with retroactive effect; CBP has issued penalty claims under 19 U.S.C. 1592 for importer claims of exclusion
