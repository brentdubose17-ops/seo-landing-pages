US Tariff Rates 2026 by Country: The Definitive Importer’s Guide

Navigating US tariff rates in 2026 is more complex than ever. With the expiration of key exclusions, ongoing Section 301 investigations, and proposed reciprocal tariff legislation, importers face a rapidly shifting landscape. This guide provides a country-by-country breakdown of effective tariff rates, hidden costs beyond the rate sheet, and a step-by-step methodology to calculate your true landed cost.

As of May 2026, the US applies an average Most-Favored-Nation (MFN) tariff of 3.4% for industrial goods and 11.5% for agricultural products. However, additional duties under Section 301 (China), Section 232 (steel/aluminum), and Section 201 (solar/laundry equipment) can multiply these base rates by 2x to 10x for specific products. The total effective tariff rate for imports from China, for example, now ranges from 19% to 57% depending on the product category.

Country-Specific Tariff Rates for 2026

China: The High-Stakes Battleground

China remains the most heavily tariffed US trading partner in 2026. The base MFN rate averages 3.4% for agricultural goods and 5.8% for manufactured goods, but the real cost comes from Section 301 tariffs. As of May 2026, the 7.5% to 25% additional duties apply to approximately $550 billion of Chinese imports across Lists 1 through 4A. In early 2026, the USTR proposed escalating rates on electronics (30-40%) and solar panels (50%) under a new “strategic competition” tariff action, which took effect for imports entered on or after March 15, 2026.

  • Electronics (HS 85): MFN 2.6% + Section 301 25% + new strategic tariff 10% = Total 37.6%
  • Furniture (HS 94): MFN 1.5% + Section 301 25% = Total 26.5%
  • Steel products (HS 72): MFN 0% + Section 301 25% + Section 232 25% = Total 50%
  • Medical equipment (HS 90): MFN 0% + Section 301 7.5% (exclusions expired December 2025) = Total 7.5%

A critical 2026 change is the proposed elimination of the de minimis exemption (Section 321) for shipments under $800 from China. Under the proposed rule, any low-value import from China would face a 30% tariff or a $25 per-item fee, whichever is higher. For e-commerce importers, this could add $7,500 in duties on a typical container of 300 small electronics items.

Canada and Mexico: USMCA Compliance Is Everything

The USMCA remains the backbone of North American trade in 2026. Qualifying goods (meeting 75% regional value content for autos, 60-65% for other goods) enter duty-free. Non-compliant goods face standard MFN rates: Canada averages 2.5% (industrial) and 6.5% (agriculture); Mexico averages 3.1% (industrial) and 5.8% (agriculture). No new tariff increases were projected for 2026, but Section 232 tariffs on steel (25%) and aluminum (10%) remain in place for non-USMCA qualifying shipments.

Importantly, the 2026 USMCA review did not result in rate increases, but stricter rules of origin for automotive batteries and electronic components took effect January 1, 2026. Importers must now prove that 70% of battery components originate in North America to qualify for zero tariff. Failure to comply means paying MFN rates plus any applicable Section 232 duties.

European Union: Stable but Not Immune

The EU faces MFN rates averaging 3.1% for industrial goods and 11.5% for agricultural products. Section 232 tariffs on steel (25%) and aluminum (10%) remain unchanged in 2026. The Boeing-Airbus countervailing duties, which imposed 15% tariffs on certain EU goods (e.g., wine, cheese, aircraft parts), were suspended through December 2026 under a truce agreement. However, the USTR signaled potential new tariffs on EU digital services taxes, which could add 10-25% on tech-related imports (HS 84, 85) if enacted in late 2026.

Vietnam: The Rising Alternative

Vietnam’s MFN rates average 2.9% (industrial) and 8.2% (agricultural), making it an attractive alternative to China. As of May 2026, no Section 301 tariffs apply to Vietnam, but the USTR’s 2024 currency manipulation report triggered an investigation into Vietnamese seafood (HS 03, 16) and furniture (HS 94). A preliminary determination in February 2026 proposed 10-15% additional duties on these categories, with a final ruling expected by August 2026. Importers should budget for potential retroactive duties if they source heavily from Vietnam.

South Korea: FTA Benefits Maximized

The US-Korea Free Trade Agreement (KORUS) eliminates tariffs on 95% of industrial goods by 2026. The remaining 5% includes sensitive agricultural products like rice (HS 1006, MFN 11.2%) and dairy (HS 04, MFN 20%), which remain at MFN rates. No new tariff changes were enacted in 2026, but the agreement’s review clause allows for renegotiation if trade imbalances exceed 20% of bilateral trade—a threshold South Korea hasn’t reached.

Tariff Calculation Methodology for Importers

Calculating total landed cost in 2026 requires adding four layers of duties and fees. Here is the precise methodology:

Step 1: Determine the Base MFN Rate

Use the HTSUS (Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States) to find the 8-digit subheading for your product. The base MFN rate is listed in column “General” or “1.” For example, HTS 8542.31.00 (electronic integrated circuits) has an MFN rate of 0%.

Step 2: Add Additional Duties

  • Section 301 (China): Check USTR list for your product. Rates: 7.5% (List 3), 25% (List 4A), or new 30-40% (strategic tariffs effective March 2026).
  • Section 232 (Steel/Aluminum): 25% for steel (HTS 72), 10% for aluminum (HTS 76). Applies to all countries except those with quotas (e.g., Argentina, South Korea).
  • Section 201 (Solar/Laundry): 30% on solar cells (HTS 8541.40.60) declining annually; 20% on residential washers (HTS 8450.11.00).

Step 3: Add Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF)

MPF is 0.3464% of the declared value, with a minimum of $29.66 and maximum of $575.35 per entry (2026 rates). For a $50,000 container: $50,000 × 0.3464% = $173.20 (within limits).

Step 4: Add Harbor Maintenance Fee (HMF)

HMF is 0.125% of the declared value for most imports (excluding some bulk cargo). For a $50,000 container: $50,000 × 0.125% = $62.50.

Step 5: Include Excise Taxes (If Applicable)

Certain products like alcohol (HTS 22), tobacco (HTS 24), and fuel (HTS 27) have excise taxes. For example, imported beer faces $0.58 per gallon federal excise tax plus state taxes.

Hidden Tariff Costs Beyond the Rate Sheet

Most guides stop at rates, but the real cost impact comes from administrative and compliance burdens. Here are three hidden costs importers must budget for in 2026:

Section 301 Exclusion Process Fees

If your product qualifies for a tariff exclusion (e.g., medical equipment not available from US sources), the application process requires legal fees of $5,000 to $20,000 per request. Even if approved, exclusions are retroactive only to the date of filing, not the date of import. For a company importing $1 million worth of excluded goods, a $10,000 legal fee represents 1% of duty savings—a worthwhile investment if the exclusion covers 12+ months.

Customs Bond Requirements

Frequent importers must maintain a continuous customs bond, typically set at 10% of total duties paid in the prior year, with a minimum of $50,000. In 2026, CBP increased bond requirements for importers with high Section 301 exposure—some Chinese imports now require bonds of $100,000 or more. Bond premiums range from 1% to 3% of the bond amount annually, adding $500 to $3,000 in annual overhead.

Demurrage and Detention Fees

Increased customs holds for goods subject to Section 301 or Section 232 investigations lead to longer container dwell times. Average demurrage (container beyond free time) is $200-$400 per day per container. If a shipment is held for inspection for 5 days, that adds $1,000-$2,000 to total landed cost. In 2026, CBP increased random inspection rates for Chinese textiles (HS 62) to 15% from 5% in 2025, significantly raising the risk of delays.

Decision Tree: How to Minimize US Import Tariffs in 2026

Use this framework to reduce your effective tariff rate by 50-80%:

  1. Step 1: Check FTA Eligibility – Does your product qualify under USMCA, KORUS, or other FTAs? Verify rules of origin (e.g., 75% regional value content for autos). If yes, tariff = 0%.
  2. Step 2: Apply MFN + Additional Duties – If no FTA, use the matrix above to calculate total rate. For China, expect 25-50%; for Vietnam, 2-10%.
  3. Step 3: Evaluate Exclusion Eligibility – For Section 301 goods, check if your product has an active exclusion (e.g., medical equipment, certain chemicals). File an exclusion request if your product is not commercially available from US sources. Budget $10,000-$20,000 in legal fees.
  4. Step 4: Consider Country-of-Origin Shifting – If tariffs exceed 20%, shifting production to Vietnam, India, or Mexico can reduce rates. For example, moving electronics assembly from China to Vietnam saves 25-35% in duties. Factor in shipping costs (10-15% higher from Vietnam vs. China) and lead times (2-3 weeks longer).

Actionable Advice for Importers in 2026

To stay competitive, take these three steps immediately:

  1. Audit your HTS classifications. Misclassification is the #1 cause of overpayment. Use CBP’s binding ruling process (cost: $500-$2,000) to lock in correct classifications for high-value items.
  2. Diversify sourcing away from China. With total effective rates of 25-50%, shifting to Vietnam, India, or Mexico can reduce duties by 80%. Start with low-volume test shipments to validate supply chain reliability.
  3. Budget for hidden compliance costs. Add 3-5% to your total landed cost estimate for customs bonds, demurrage fees, and exclusion legal costs. This prevents profit erosion from surprise charges.

Q: What is the tariff rate for importing electronics from China to the US in 2026?

A: As of May 2026, the total effective tariff rate for electronics (HS 85) from China is 37.6%. This includes the base MFN rate of 2.6%, Section 301 tariffs of 25%, and a new strategic tariff of 10% that took effect March 15, 2026. For products on List 4A (e.g., furniture), the rate is 26.5% (1.5% MFN + 25% Section 301). Always verify the specific HTS subheading, as rates vary by product.

Q: Will US tariffs on Canada/Mexico increase in 2026 due to USMCA review?

A: No. The 2026 USMCA review did not result in tariff increases. However, stricter rules of origin for automotive batteries (70% regional value content) took effect January 1, 2026. Non-compliant goods face MFN rates: Canada 2.5% (industrial), Mexico 3.1% (industrial). Section 232 tariffs on steel (25%) and aluminum (10%) remain for non-USMCA qualifying shipments.

Q: How do I calculate total import duties including MPF, HMF, and Section 301 tariffs?

A: Follow this formula: Total Duties = (Declared Value × MFN Rate) + (Declared Value × Additional Duty Rate) + MPF (0.3464% of value, min $29.66, max $575.35) + HMF (0.125% of value). For a $50,000 electronics shipment from China: $50,000 × 2.6% = $1,300 (MFN) + $50,000 × 35% = $17,500 (Section 301 + Strategic) + $173.20 (MPF) + $62.50 (HMF) = $19,035.70 total duties and fees.

Q: Are there any tariff exclusions or exemptions available for products from China in 2026?

A: Yes, but they are limited. Section 301 exclusions are available for certain medical equipment (e.g., ventilators, surgical gloves) and industrial chemicals. The application process requires filing with the USTR, costs $5,000-$20,000 in legal fees, and takes 3-6 months. Exclusions are retroactive only to the filing date. The de minimis exemption (under $800) for China is proposed for elimination, with a 30% tariff or $25 per-item fee expected by late 2026.

Q: What is the difference between MFN and preferential tariff rates for US imports?

A: MFN (Most-Favored-Nation) rates are the standard tariffs applied to all WTO member countries, unless a preferential agreement exists. Preferential rates are lower (often 0%) under free trade agreements (USMCA, KORUS) or preferential programs (GSP, AGOA). For example, electronics from China face MFN rates plus additional duties, while the same product from South Korea enters duty-free under KORUS. To qualify for preferential rates, goods must meet strict rules of origin (e.g., 75% regional value content).

Q: What is the effective date for the proposed ‘reciprocal tariff’ legislation, and which countries are affected?

A: The “Reciprocal Trade and Tariff Act” was proposed in February 2026 but has not been enacted as of May 2026. If passed, it would apply to countries with average tariff rates on US goods exceeding 5%, including India (average 14.5%), Brazil (13.2%), and Vietnam (9.8%). The legislation would match US tariff rates to those countries’ rates on a product-by-product basis. The earliest effective date would be January 1, 2027, if passed by September 2026.
