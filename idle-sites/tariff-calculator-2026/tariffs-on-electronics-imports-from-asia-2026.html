Scope and Timing of 2026 Tariff Increases

The most significant tariff action affecting electronics in 2026 is the continuation and hardening of Section 301 tariffs on Chinese imports. Originally imposed under the Trump administration and largely maintained by the Biden administration, these tariffs were scheduled for a statutory review in 2025. That review concluded with a determination to keep rates at 25% for most electronics categories under List 3 and List 4A.

Specifically, the following timelines apply:

  • January 1, 2026: Section 301 tariffs on Chinese-origin electronics under List 3 (HS 8471, 8517, 8542, etc.) remained at 25%. No new product exclusions were granted. All previous COVID-era exclusions expired by September 30, 2024, and were not renewed.
  • Proposed Section 232 on Semiconductors: The USTR published a notice of proposed rulemaking in late 2025 for a 25% ad valorem tariff on all imported semiconductors, memory chips, and integrated circuits, regardless of country of origin. As of May 2026, this tariff has not been enacted, but it is modeled as a high-probability event for Q3 2026.
  • Section 201 Safeguard on Solar Electronics: The existing 30% tariff on imported solar cells and modules (with a 2.5 GW tariff rate quota) continues through 2026, with a slight reduction to 25% expected in August 2026.

The practical effect is that any electronics product with a Chinese bill of material or final assembly is now subject to a minimum 25% tariff. For products using semiconductors sourced from China, the import duty can compound, pushing effective rates above 35% in some cases.

Which Product Categories Are Hit Hardest?

Not all electronics are created equal under the 2026 tariff regime. The highest exposure falls on finished consumer goods and key components with high Chinese market share. Here is the breakdown by HS code and product segment:

  • HS 8471 (Laptops, PCs, Tablets): Nearly 65% of all laptops imported into the U.S. are assembled in China. The 25% Section 301 tariff applies directly. Average import price in 2025 was $450–$600 per unit; the tariff adds $112–$150 per unit at full cost.
  • HS 8517 (Smartphones, Telecom Equipment): Over 80% of smartphones are assembled in China, with key components from Vietnam and Taiwan. The 25% tariff applies to the finished product. For a $800 smartphone, the tariff adds $200 per unit before any passthrough adjustments.
  • HS 8542 (Semiconductors, Integrated Circuits): Currently at 25% under List 3 if imported directly from China. If the Section 232 tariff is enacted, all imported chips—including those from Taiwan (TSMC), South Korea (Samsung), and Malaysia—will face a 25% duty. This would raise the cost of a $50 chip to $62.50 instantly.
  • HS 8534 (Printed Circuit Boards, PCBs): China accounts for 50% of global PCB production. The 25% tariff applies. Average PCB import price is $0.10–$0.50 per unit; the tariff adds $0.025–$0.125 per board, which compounds across millions of units.
  • HS 8507 (Lithium-Ion Batteries): Subject to both Section 301 (25%) and potential Section 232 actions. Battery packs for laptops and EVs face effective rates of 25–30% in 2026.

Country-of-Origin Shifts and Exclusion Risks

The conventional wisdom in 2024 and 2025 was to shift sourcing from China to ASEAN countries—Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia, and Indonesia—to avoid Section 301 tariffs. That strategy worked, to a degree. In 2024, U.S. electronics imports from Vietnam grew 18% year-over-year, while imports from China declined 6%. But 2026 has introduced new variables that make this shift riskier.

The ASEAN Tariff Trap

Here is the contrarian angle most competitors miss: shifting to Vietnam or Thailand does not guarantee tariff-free entry in 2026. There are three specific threats:

  • Anti-Dumping Duties: The U.S. Department of Commerce has initiated anti-dumping investigations on certain electronics components from Vietnam and Thailand, particularly solar-related electronics and battery cells. Preliminary duties of 10–20% have been proposed for Vietnamese-origin solar panels. If these expand to other electronics categories, the tariff savings vanish.
  • UFLPA Seizure Risks: The Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA) applies to goods produced wholly or in part in China’s Xinjiang region. However, U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) has expanded enforcement to include goods transshipped through Vietnam or Malaysia that contain Chinese-origin components. In 2025, CBP issued over 200 detention orders for electronics imported from ASEAN countries where the bill of materials included Chinese inputs. The cost of detention—storage, legal fees, lost sales—can exceed 15% of the shipment value.
  • Section 301 Expansion: The USTR has signaled that if transshipment of Chinese electronics through ASEAN is detected at scale, Section 301 tariffs could be extended to cover ASEAN-origin products with high Chinese content. This would eliminate the tariff arbitrage entirely.

In short, the “China + 1” strategy is not a permanent solution. Importers must verify not just the country of final assembly, but the entire supply chain for forced labor risk and anti-dumping exposure.

Exclusion Lists: What Is Still Available?

As of May 2026, there are no general exclusions for electronics under Section 301. The last batch of product exclusions expired in September 2024. However, there are narrow pathways:

  • Section 301 Exclusions for Medical Electronics: Some medical devices (e.g., ECG machines, ultrasound equipment) still have case-by-case exclusions if imported for humanitarian use. This is not applicable to consumer electronics.
  • Section 232 Exclusion Requests: If the semiconductor tariff is enacted, companies can file for product-specific exclusions with the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS). Historically, only 30–40% of such requests are granted, and the process takes 6–9 months.
  • UFLPA Waivers: Limited waivers exist for electronics components that cannot be sourced outside Xinjiang. These are granted on a per-company basis and require extensive supply chain tracing.

For the vast majority of consumer electronics—laptops, phones, tablets, wearables—there is no exclusion available in 2026. Importers must plan for the full 25% tariff as a fixed cost.

Mitigation Strategies for Importers

The good news is that tariff costs are not immutable. There are legal, strategic ways to reduce your effective duty rate. The key is to act now, before the Section 232 tariff is enacted and before anti-dumping duties expand.

Strategy 1: Tariff Engineering via HS Code Reclassification

Not all electronics products are classified under the same HS code. Minor modifications to a product’s design or function can shift it to a lower-duty HS code. For example, a device classified as a “computer” (HS 8471, 25% tariff) might be reclassifiable as a “telecommunications apparatus” (HS 8517, 25% tariff) or even as a “consumer electronic device” (HS 8528, 15% tariff) if it includes a display function. This requires engineering and legal review, but the savings can be 10 percentage points or more.

  • Feasibility: Medium. Requires product redesign and CBP ruling requests.
  • Savings Potential: 10–15% reduction in effective tariff rate.

Strategy 2: Duty Drawback and Bonded Warehousing

If you import electronics components, assemble them in the U.S., and then export the finished product, you may qualify for duty drawback—a refund of 99% of the tariff paid on the imported components. Bonded warehousing allows you to store imported electronics without paying duty until they are withdrawn for U.S. consumption. If you re-export from the warehouse, no duty is owed.

  • Feasibility: High for companies with export operations.
  • Savings Potential: Up to 99% of tariff cost on re-exported goods.

Strategy 3: Free Trade Agreement (FTA) Utilization

The USMCA (US-Mexico-Canada Agreement) and the US-Korea FTA offer zero-tariff entry for electronics that meet strict rules of origin. For example, a laptop assembled in Mexico using U.S. or Mexican components can enter duty-free under USMCA. Similarly, semiconductors from South Korea qualify for 0% tariff under the US-Korea FTA if they meet the regional value content requirement (typically 55% or higher).

  • Feasibility: High for companies already operating in FTA partner countries.
  • Savings Potential: Full 25% tariff avoidance.

Strategy 4: Sourcing Shifts (with Caution)

Shifting final assembly from China to Taiwan, South Korea, or Mexico can reduce tariff exposure from 25% to 0–3.9% (MFN rate). However, as discussed, the ASEAN Tariff Trap means you must verify the entire supply chain.   • Feasibility: Medium to High, depending on supplier relationships.
  • Savings Potential: 20–25% reduction in tariff cost.

FAQ: Tariffs on Electronics Imports from Asia in 2026

Q: Will tariffs on electronics from China increase in 2026?

A: Yes. Section 301 tariffs on Chinese-origin electronics under List 3 and List 4A are at 25% as of January 1, 2026. No new exclusions have been granted. Additionally, a proposed 25% Section 232 tariff on all foreign-made semiconductors is under active consideration, which would affect chips from Taiwan, South Korea, and Malaysia.

Q: Which specific electronics products will be hit hardest?

A: Laptops (HS 8471), smartphones (HS 8517), semiconductors (HS 8542), PCBs (HS 8534), and lithium-ion batteries (HS 8507) face the highest exposure. Finished consumer goods with high Chinese assembly content are affected most, with effective tariff rates of 25% or more.

Q: Are there any exclusions or exemptions for electronics from Vietnam or Malaysia?

A: Not reliably. While Vietnam and Malaysia are not subject to Section 301 tariffs, they face potential anti-dumping duties on certain electronics (e.g., solar panels, batteries) and seizure risks under the UFLPA if their supply chains include Chinese components. The “ASEAN Tariff Trap” means shifting to these countries is not a guaranteed safe harbor.

Q: How much will a new iPhone or laptop cost in 2026 due to tariffs?

A: Based on a 60–85% passthrough rate, a $800 smartphone will cost $80–$120 more at retail. A $600 laptop will increase by $72–$120. These estimates assume the Section 232 semiconductor tariff is not enacted; if it is, add another $10–$15 per device.

Q: Can I avoid tariffs by importing from Taiwan or South Korea instead of China?

A: Partially. Taiwan and South Korea are not subject to Section 301 tariffs, so you avoid the 25% rate. However, they are subject to MFN rates (0–3.9% for most electronics) and potentially the Section 232 semiconductor tariff if enacted. The US-Korea FTA provides a 0% rate for qualifying electronics, making South Korea an attractive alternative.
