De Minimis Rule Changes 2026: The $25 Tripwire That Reshapes Ecommerce Importing

The de minimis exemption — the Section 321 rule that allows packages valued under $800 to enter the United States duty-free and largely undocumented — is ending in 2026. Under the bipartisan End the De Minimis Exemption Act of 2025 and the 2025 executive order on China-origin goods, the threshold drops to $0 for shipments from China and $200 for all other countries, with a proposed $25 per-package processing fee attached to every formal entry. For a $50 product, the combined tariff and fee stack raises landed cost from roughly $50 to $110–$130, a 120–160% increase that makes low-cost dropshipping economically unviable. The bottom line: every ecommerce merchant importing into the U.S. must re-engineer sourcing, fulfillment, pricing, and checkout display before the rules take full effect.

The De Minimis Rule Change: What Exactly Happens in 2026

The Section 321 de minimis exemption has been a cornerstone of cross-border ecommerce since the threshold was raised to $800 in March 2016 under the Trade Facilitation and Trade Enforcement Act. For nearly a decade, any package valued under $800 arriving in the United States entered without formal customs declarations, without duties, and without meaningful inspection. That era ends in 2026.

Two parallel legal tracks are collapsing the exemption. First, the America Works Act and the End the De Minimis Exemption Act of 2025, reintroduced in the 119th Congress, propose a dual-track threshold: $0 for China-origin goods and $200 for all other countries. Second, the 2025 executive order on IEEPA tariffs already terminated de minimis treatment for China-origin goods, subjecting them to the full formal-entry process plus escalating tariffs that reached 40% by mid-2025.

The Dual-Track Threshold: China vs. the Rest of the World

The single most important distinction for merchants is origin country, not product category. China-origin shipments face a $0 threshold — meaning every package, regardless of value, requires formal entry, pays applicable tariffs, and incurs the proposed $25 processing fee. Non-China shipments retain a $200 threshold, which still eliminates de minimis relief for most consumer ecommerce parcels, since the average cross-border order value sits between $40 and $80.

This is not a hypothetical policy debate. Over 1.3 billion packages entered the United States via Section 321 in 2024, according to U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP), and approximately 60% of those shipments originated in China. In categories like direct-to-consumer apparel, phone cases, and consumer electronics, the China share exceeds 90%. The 2026 rule change effectively removes the majority of global ecommerce parcel traffic from the informal-entry system at once.

Who Pays, and How Much: The Real Cost Stack

The common mistake in coverage of this rule change is focusing on tariff percentages. Tariffs matter, but the structural shock comes from fixed fees that punish low-value shipments disproportionately. Understanding the full cost stack requires separating four distinct charges: IEEPA tariffs, Section 301 tariffs, the per-package processing fee, and carrier-imposed brokerage surcharges.

The Tariff Layers: IEEPA Plus Section 301

China-origin goods now carry an IEEPA tariff that escalated through 2025: 10% in February, 20% in March, 30% shortly after, and 40% by mid-year. On top of that, Section 301 tariffs apply to specific product categories — up to 100% on electric vehicles, 50% on semiconductors and solar cells, and 25% on a broad range of machinery, electronics, and consumer goods. For a merchant importing a $50 electronics accessory from China, the tariff stack alone can reach 65% of declared value.

The government has a strong revenue motive for closing the loophole. CBP testimony revealed that over $1 billion in IEEPA and Section 301 tariff revenue was evaded through de minimis shipments in 2023–2024 alone. The agency currently screens less than 1% of the 3.6 million daily de minimis packages, meaning tariff avoidance was not just common but structurally guaranteed.

The $25 Per-Package Tripwire

Here is the number most coverage gets wrong: the tariff percentage is not the killer — the fixed fee is. CBP estimates it spends roughly $0.40 to clear a Section 321 entry today versus $20 or more to process a formal entry. To recover that cost, the proposed 2026 legislation includes a $25 per-package processing fee, paid by the importer of record.

Run the math on a $30 item. A 40% IEEPA tariff adds $12. A 25% Section 301 tariff adds another $7.50. But the $25 processing fee plus a carrier brokerage surcharge of $10–$15 adds $35–$40 in fixed costs. The combined landed cost jumps from $30 to roughly $70–$80 — a 130–170% increase — before you even account for shipping. For low-cost products, the fee is the tripwire that makes the entire shipment economically unviable.

How Carriers Will Weaponize the Change

Independent of government policy, UPS, FedEx, DHL, and USPS are rewriting their ancillary surcharge schedules in response. Carriers already charge $10–$15 per package for limited de minimis brokerage at 2025 rates, and they have stated publicly that 2026 rates will rise 50–100% as formal-entry paperwork volume increases. New surcharge line items are appearing across carrier rate sheets: "Section 321 Processing Fees," "Formal Entry Brokerage Fees" ranging from $40 to $80, and "Disbursement Fees" for duties paid on the importer's behalf.

This is a secondary cost shock that no policy analysis fully captures. The government collects its $25 fee, the carrier collects its brokerage fee, and the merchant collects the bill. For a merchant shipping 1,000 packages per month, the combined fee stack — government plus carrier — can easily exceed $50,000 per month in new costs that did not exist in 2024.

Operational Disruption: Dropshipping, Clearance Times, and Compliance

The drop-shipping business model — where a merchant sells a product online and a supplier in China ships it directly to the U.S. customer — was built on the $800 de minimis exemption. The 2026 change dismantles that model's economics in one stroke. Every China-origin package now requires formal entry, which means a customs broker, a bond, a documented Importer of Record, and a tariff classification for each SKU.

Clearance Times Will Multiply

Today, a de minimis package typically clears in 0–2 hours because no customs declaration is filed at all. Under the new formal-entry regime, CBP must review documentation, validate HS codes, assess duties, and potentially inspect the physical package. Industry planning estimates assume 48–72 hours of additional clearance time under normal conditions — and significantly longer during peak season, when CBP processes millions of formal entries that were previously informal.

For merchants promising 7–10 day delivery from China, the realistic timeline becomes 14–21 days. For merchants using the U.S. Postal Service's inbound international service, the delay compounds because USPS must coordinate with CBP's formal entry process rather than relying on the mail-import shortcut that governed de minimis parcels for years.

The DDP-to-Formal-Entry Shift

Most cross-border ecommerce currently ships under Delivered Duty Paid (DDP) terms, where the seller handles duties and clearance at destination. Under the new regime, DDP becomes DDP-with-mandatory-bonded-entry. The seller must either establish its own Importer of Record status with a surety bond (typically $50,000 for continuous coverage) or pay a licensed customs broker $75–$150 per entry to act as agent. That bond requirement alone eliminates the "no-touch" drop-shipping workflow that operators have relied on for a decade.

Delivered Duty Unpaid (DDU) models face an even worse outcome: the customer becomes the Importer of Record, must provide a Social Security number or EIN for customs clearance, and faces the $25 fee plus carrier brokerage charges at the door. Industry data from 2025 pilot programs shows refusal rates above 30% when customers are handed surprise import bills at delivery, making DDU shipping effectively unusable for low-value goods.

Mitigation Strategies: What Smart Merchants Are Doing Now

The merchants who thrive in 2026 are not waiting for legislative clarity. They are restructuring their supply chains around four proven strategies: bulk pre-importing, 3PL warehousing, shipment bundling, and HS-code compliance remediation.

Strategy 1: Shift From Dropshipping to U.S.-Based 3PL Warehousing

The most direct mitigation is to stop shipping individual orders from overseas and instead pre-import inventory in bulk to a U.S. warehouse. Bulk shipments arrive as formal commercial entries — but the $25 per-package fee does not apply because the fee is assessed per parcel, not per container. A 1,000-unit shipment cleared as a single formal entry incurs one brokerage fee, not 1,000.

The breakeven calculation is stark. A merchant shipping more than 500 packages per month from China will pay $12,500 per month in processing fees alone under the per-package regime — plus $40,000–$80,000 in carrier brokerage fees. A bonded 3PL with pre-imported inventory, by contrast, pays duties on the wholesale value (not retail), amortizes one brokerage fee across the entire container, and ships domestically with no customs involvement whatsoever.

Strategy 2: The Bundling Loophole That Still Works

For non-China suppliers, the proposed $200 threshold creates a strategic consolidation opportunity that most merchants are missing. Multiple items consolidated into a single shipment stay under the threshold as one entry, amortizing the $25 fee across several products. A merchant selling $60 bundles can pair complementary SKUs into a $150–$180 combined shipment, pay one $25 fee, and preserve margin.

This changes product architecture itself. Sellers should redesign pricing, packaging, and SKU architecture so that maximum order value lands between $150 and $195 — above average order value, below the $200 threshold, and optimized for the fee structure. Merchants who ship four separate $40 items from a non-China supplier pay $100 in processing fees; merchants who bundle the same four items into one $160 package pay $25. The bundling advantage is a 75% reduction in per-order customs cost.

Strategy 3: Renegotiate Supplier Incoterms

Current supplier agreements written on EXW (Ex Works) or FOB (Free On Board) terms shift all import cost and risk to the buyer. In 2026, merchants should renegotiate toward DAP (Delivered at Place) terms where the supplier retains ownership and compliance responsibility until the goods reach U.S. soil. Better yet, push suppliers to adopt DDP with the supplier registered as Importer of Record — moving the compliance burden and the bond requirement entirely off the merchant's books.

Chinese suppliers who primarily sell to U.S. consumers are already adapting. The most sophisticated factories are opening U.S. distribution subsidiaries that pre-clear inventory at wholesale value, effectively converting themselves from foreign suppliers into domestic wholesalers. Merchants who source from these suppliers bypass the 2026 problem entirely because the goods are already in the country and duty-paid.

Strategy 4: Fix Your HS Codes Before CBP Audits You

Here is the compliance time bomb no one is talking about: many sellers are underpaying today by using vague or incorrect HS codes. A phone case declared as "plastic article" rather than the specific electronics-accessory code can slash assessed duty by 50% or more. Under the informal de minimis system, CBP never reviewed these declarations. Under the formal-entry regime, every entry is documented and auditable.

CBP has both the authority and the incentive to back-assess duties on prior shipments, and liquidated damages for false or inaccurate entries can reach $100,000 per violation. Every merchant importing goods in 2026 should run a full HS-code audit of all active SKUs against the 6-digit Harmonized System harmonization schedule before the rule takes effect. The cost of the audit — typically $500–$2,000 per 100 SKUs from a licensed customs broker — is trivial compared to the liability exposure.

The Checkout Experience: How Fees Destroy Conversions

The 2026 rule change does not just alter landed cost; it alters consumer psychology at the exact moment of purchase. Merchants face a fundamental decision with no clean answer: show the new import fees at checkout and risk cart abandonment, or absorb them into pricing and risk margin collapse.

Data from 2025 cross-border pilot programs shows a 10–25% cart abandonment spike when surprise import fees are displayed at checkout. Consumers who were trained for a decade to expect free, fee-free shipping from overseas do not accept a $40 customs surcharge on a $50 product gracefully — they abandon the cart and shop domestic alternatives.

The Winning Play: Built-In Absorption Above $75

The emerging best practice among profitable cross-border merchants is built-in price absorption for orders over $75 combined with a "free shipping + duties included" guarantee. Instead of itemizing a $25 processing fee and $12 tariff as separate line items, successful merchants raise the product price by 15–25% and absorb the fees invisibly into margin. The conversion economics favor this approach: losing 20% of your margin on a converted sale beats losing 20% of your entire cart volume to abandonment.

For orders under $75, the math collapses. A $40 product with 40% tariffs and $35 in fixed fees needs a 140% price increase to maintain margin, and no absorption strategy survives that. The hard truth is that low-value products sourced from China become effectively unviable for direct-to-consumer shipment in 2026. The rational response is to stop selling those SKUs, bundle them into higher-value orders, or shift production to non-China origins.

Goods from all other countries face a proposed $200 threshold under the End the De Minimis Exemption Act of 2025.
