De Minimis Rule Changes 2026 Impact on Ecommerce

Published August 20, 2026By ABD Legacy LLC

The 2026 De Minimis Overhaul: What Ecommerce Sellers Face When the $800 Loophole Closes

As of May 2026, the $800 Section 321 de minimis exemption that let low-value ecommerce packages enter the United States duty-free is no longer a reliable cost assumption — it is a regulatory battleground. The proposed "Stop Online Shopping Loopholes Act" would cut the threshold from $800 to $50, CBP's pending surety bond rule would impose new bonding and advance-data costs on every Section 321 entry, and H.R. 3522 threatens to revoke China's Permanent Normal Trade Relations (PNTR) status, which would push average tariffs on Chinese goods from roughly 3 percent to between 40 and 60 percent. For a $50 sweater shipped from China, the fully loaded landed cost under the proposed framework rises from $50.00 to $72.06 — a 44 percent increase driven by a 20 percent tariff, a $2.06 Merchandise Processing Fee, and a $10 broker fee. The bottom line: cross-border ecommerce is shifting from a $0-customs-cost model to a per-package compliance economy, and merchants who do not rebuild their landed-cost models by Q3 2026 will watch margin evaporate on every order.

The de minimis rule has been called the "backbone of fast-fashion ecommerce," and for good reason. CBP recorded 1.1 billion de minimis entries in fiscal year 2023, valued at $68.3 billion — a fifteen-fold increase from $4.5 billion in 2015. That works out to roughly 3 million packages per day crossing the border with zero duty, zero Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF), and zero broker involvement. The system was designed for personal gifts and occasional purchases; it is now the primary import lane for Temu, Shein, AliExpress, and thousands of US-based ecommerce sellers.

That era is ending. This article breaks down exactly what changed between 2025 and 2026, what each change costs on a per-package basis, which carriers and entry types are affected, and — most importantly — which products still make financial sense to ship direct-to-consumer. If you sell goods online, source from Asia, or buy from marketplaces on a regular basis, the math below applies directly to you.

From Executive Order to Statute: How the 2026 Rules Actually Changed

To understand where the de minimis rule stands in May 2026, you need the full timeline. The policy did not change overnight — it lurched through an executive order, a temporary suspension, a reinstatement, and now a legislative overhaul that is still in motion.

The February 2025 Executive Order: A Dress Rehearsal

On February 1, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order imposing an additional 30 percent ad valorem tariff on Chinese-origin goods, or $25 per item, whichever was greater, and simultaneously suspended the de minimis exemption for those goods. The suspension took effect February 4, 2025, and was terminated on May 2, 2025, after the underlying IEEPA tariff structure was revised. During those roughly 90 days, the ecommerce industry got a brutal preview of a world without Section 321: USPS stopped accepting certain China-bound and China-origin packages, customs brokers were overwhelmed with informal entries that previously never touched a filing system, and consumers saw delivery times stretch from days to weeks.

That turbulence was not an anomaly — it was the stress test for 2026. The executive order proved the administrative machinery could flip the de minimis switch quickly. The question now is whether Congress and CBP will make that flip permanent and universal.

H.R. 3522 and the PNTR Revocation Threat

The most consequential legislative threat is H.R. 3522, the Restore Trade Fairness Act, which would revoke China's Permanent Normal Trade Relations (PNTR) status. PNTR, granted in 2000 when China joined the WTO, locks in low "normal" tariff rates. Revoking it would reset Chinese goods to the much higher "Column 2" tariff schedule.

The numbers are stark. According to estimates cited by the US-China Business Council, the average US tariff on Chinese goods would climb from roughly 3 percent to between 40 and 60 percent under full PNTR revocation. When stacked on top of existing Section 301 tariffs — which currently average 20 to 25 percent on consumer goods — a single product could face combined tariffs exceeding 80 percent. That is not a rounding error; that is the difference between a profitable SKU and a loss leader.

As of this writing, H.R. 3522 remains pending, but the threat alone is reshaping sourcing decisions. Merchants are already modeling a worst-case scenario where "made in China" carries a tariff burden heavier than any other major origin country.

The CBP Surety Bond Rule: The Hidden Cost Multiplier

Separate from legislation, CBP published a proposed rule in mid-2025 requiring a continuous surety bond and advance electronic data for all Section 321 entries. Today, de minimis shipments require no bond — postal shipments often have no filing at all, and express carrier T86 filings are transmitted without a customs bond. The proposed rule would change that fundamentally.

The estimated compliance cost is $100 to $500 per company per year for the bond itself, plus per-entry filing fees imposed by the carrier or broker. That sounds modest until you spread it across millions of packages. More importantly, the bond requirement creates a legal importer of record — someone with skin in the game who can be held liable for misdeclarations, prohibited goods, and unpaid duties. In the current no-bond system, liability is diffuse; in the proposed system, it is concrete and enforceable.

The Per-Package Cost Explosion: Doing the Math on Duty, MPF, and Broker Fees

The single biggest misconception about the 2026 de minimis changes is that they only affect "big" shipments. In reality, the cost impact is inverse to package value — the cheapest items take the biggest percentage hit. Here is the entry-type framework you need to understand before any calculation.

The Four Entry Tiers Every Merchant Must Know

  • De minimis (Section 321, under $800): $0 duty, $0 MPF, $0 broker fee, no bond. Clearance typically under 1 day.
  • Informal entry (under $2,500): MPF equals the lesser of $2.06 or 2% of value; broker fees typically $5–$15 per line; duty applies based on HTS classification.
  • Formal entry (over $2,500): MPF is 0.3464% ad valorem, with a $32 minimum and $634 maximum; broker fees $20–$50 per entry; a continuous customs bond is required.
  • Formal entry with AD/CVD or Section 301 exposure: Adds countervailing and anti-dumping duties, plus higher bond requirements and longer examination times.

Under current law, a $10 phone case from China crosses the border with zero customs cost. Under the proposed $50 threshold, that same phone case becomes a dutiable informal entry. The shift is not linear — it is a cliff.

The $50 Sweater Test: A 44% Price Increase

Here is the benchmark example every merchant should memorize. A $50 sweater from China, with $0 shipping, currently lands on a US doorstep for $50. Under the proposed 2026 rules, the breakdown is:

  • Tariff (20% Section 301 average): $10.00
  • Merchandise Processing Fee (lesser of $2.06 or 2%): $2.06
  • Broker or carrier entry fee: $10.00
  • Total landed cost: $72.06 — a 44% increase

If PNTR revocation passes on top of everything else, that 20 percent tariff could jump to 40–60 percent. The same sweater would land at $102–$122. At that price, the consumer is no longer buying an "impulse item" — they are buying a considered purchase, and price comparison against US-retailed alternatives becomes brutal.

Carrier and Entry-Type Gridlock: USPS vs. FedEx, UPS, and DHL

Not all packages enter the United States the same way, and the 2026 reforms do not treat carriers equally. The distinction between postal and express shipments will determine your compliance burden, your cost per package, and your delivery timelines.

T86 vs. T11 vs. T01: Which Entry Your Package Actually Files

There are three entry types that matter for ecommerce:

  • T86 (Section 321, formalized): The ACE electronic filing used by express carriers (FedEx, UPS, DHL) for de minimis packages. It transmits advance data, but requires no bond today.
  • T11 (informal, postal): The USPS pathway, which historically involved no electronic filing at all for low-value packages. CBP relies on paper manifests and post-hoc sampling.
  • T01 (formal): The full customs entry for shipments over $2,500, requiring ISF filing, a bond, and classification under the HTS.

The proposed CBP rule would effectively kill the USPS T86/T11 pathway because USPS lacks the infrastructure to transmit the required advance electronic data for import safety screening. The express carriers already transmit this data; USPS does not.
