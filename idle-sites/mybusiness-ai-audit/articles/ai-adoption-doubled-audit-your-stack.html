AI Adoption Just Doubled in 8 Months. Is Your Business Ready?

Published August 19, 2026 · Updated September 1, 2026My Business AI Audit
AI industry

OpenAI's annualized revenue run rate passed $40 billion in July 2026 — roughly double what it was at the end of 2025, per Bloomberg reporting corroborated by five independent outlets. That is a projection of the company's current pace, not audited revenue — a distinction worth keeping straight, because it is exactly the kind of number that becomes a headline before it becomes a plan.

For a small or mid-size business, the number that matters is not OpenAI's money. It is the speed of the market behind it. AI demand just doubled in about eight months. The vendors selling AI are sprinting. Most businesses, meanwhile, cannot answer the simplest audit question: what AI tools are we running right now? That gap between adoption and readiness is widening — and it is the gap an audit closes.

What the $40B run rate is — and is not

OpenAI's annualized run rate passed $40 billion in July 2026, roughly double its end-of-2025 run rate of about $20 billion, a figure CFO Sarah Friar confirmed in a January business review. In July alone the run rate climbed more than 20% month-over-month, per company president Greg Brockman — driven by Codex, ChatGPT Work, subscriptions, and ads, with advertising at roughly a $1 billion annualized run rate as one component of the month's growth. Business customer count grew 32% in July.

All of that is run-rate framing — current pace annualized, not audited results. For context, OpenAI's audited FY2025 results were $13.07 billion in revenue against a $20.92 billion operating loss. The run rate tells you where demand is heading; the audited numbers tell you where the company has been. Both matter, and conflating them is how bad decisions get made.

What the adoption curve means for your business

Here is the pattern worth noticing: enterprise revenue exceeded consumer revenue at OpenAI for the first time — CFO Sarah Friar disclosed the crossover on August 14, roughly two quarters ahead of the company's own forecast, saying "the majority of our revenue is now enterprise." The company has also filed a confidential S-1 with the SEC (submitted May 2026, confirmed June 8) and is moving toward an IPO, with Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, and JPMorgan leading. No date has been claimed.

The takeaway for a small business is not "buy more AI." It is that the market has shifted decisively toward business buyers — which means vendors are now optimizing their pitch for companies like yours: more seats, more tools, more integrations, more subscriptions. When a market doubles in eight months, the selling accelerates faster than the controlling. Your job is to be on the controlling side.

Readiness is not the same thing as adoption. Adoption is a demand stat: it tells you what people are buying. Readiness is a control stat: it tells you whether what you bought is inventoried, scoped, and governed. The two have never been further apart, and the cost of that gap shows up in data exposure, permission sprawl, and spend nobody can explain.

The 5-point audit checklist

You do not need a consultant for this — you need one person and a checklist. Run these five points against your stack, and you will know where your business stands:

  1. Vendor inventory. List every AI tool in use — chat assistants, writing tools, CRM copilots, scheduling agents, free trials, shadow-IT installs. If you cannot fill in the list, that is your first finding. Our AI readiness assessment guide walks through the full inventory process.
  2. Data access. For each tool, what customer data, financial data, or credentials could it read or copy? Check integrations — an AI tool connected to your CRM, email, or bank feeds is a data-exfiltration path.
  3. Permissions. What can each tool do without a human? Anything irreversible — payments, account changes, outbound messages, code merges — should require approval. The AI agent permissions audit shows how to lock this down.
  4. Spend. What are you actually paying, per tool, per month, including per-seat upgrades and free trials that quietly became paid plans? AI spend creeps exactly the way the market does — fast and unattributed.
  5. Roadmap. What are you planning to adopt next quarter, and what will it connect to? Write the plan down before you buy, not after — the vendor will not do it for you.

Healthcare AI hit the EHR in September 2026 — audit these five things

September 1, 2026: OpenAI announced an Epic EHR integration for ChatGPT for Healthcare. Epic's electronic health record system holds data for more than 325 million patients, and the integration gives authorized clinicians read-only access to pull patient context — appointment notes, lab results, medications, specialist documentation — into ChatGPT to summarize and ask questions. It does not write back: the integration is explicitly read-only, ChatGPT cannot place orders or alter clinical documentation, and it is built to meet HIPAA requirements. San Francisco-based UCSF Health is the pilot partner, and OpenAI also launched a Healthcare Public Data plugin connecting nine official industry sources including PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, CMS Coverage, RxNorm, and DailyMed. OpenAI's own framing: “Healthcare organizations can now connect EHR and additional industry data to ChatGPT.” Read OpenAI's announcement.

Why this belongs on an AI adoption audit: EHR integration is the point where AI stops being a chat tool and becomes part of a regulated, patient-data workflow. If your business touches healthcare — a clinic, a practice, a med-spa, a health-adjacent software vendor, or any company whose AI tools could connect to patient records — the audit questions change. Add these five healthcare-specific checks to your stack review:

  1. Read-only access verification. Confirm in writing that any EHR-connected AI tool has read-only access — no writes, no orders, no chart edits. OpenAI specifies this for ChatGPT-Epic; your vendor should be able to do the same. Check the actual integration configuration, not the marketing page.
  2. Patient-data workflow compliance. Map exactly which patient data can flow into the AI tool, under what authorization, and whether that workflow meets HIPAA and your Business Associate Agreement obligations. Unauthorized patient-context import is a compliance finding even if the tool itself is compliant.
  3. Clinician authorization boundaries. Only authorized clinicians should be able to pull patient context. Audit who holds access, whether non-clinical staff can reach records through the tool, and whether role changes revoke access immediately.
  4. Safety and lawsuit scrutiny. AI health advice carries real liability — OpenAI faces a Florida pastor suit and a May 2026 wrongful-dosage suit, and even its own 4,300-response physician evaluation found some unsafe answers (99.1% safe). Document how your organization reviews AI output before it influences care decisions.
  5. Vendor and pilot validation. Pilot partnerships like UCSF Health are evidence, not proof. Validate the vendor's compliance claims, check who the pilot partners are, what the rollout scope is, and what happens to your data if the pilot ends.

The bottom line

AI demand doubling in eight months is a market signal, not a buying instruction. The vendors pushing AI have never had a bigger tailwind; the businesses auditing what they run have never been further behind. Adoption is happening on the vendors' schedule. Readiness happens on yours — but only if you actually run the checklist.

Audit the stack you already have before you add to it. Inventory the vendors, map the data access, check the permissions, total the spend, and write the roadmap. That is the five-point discipline that keeps a fast-moving market from making decisions for you.
