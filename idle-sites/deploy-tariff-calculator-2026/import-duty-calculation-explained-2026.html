As of May 2026, navigating import duties has become more complex yet more critical than ever. The Core Formula: How Import Duties Are Calculated in 2026

Every import duty calculation in 2026 follows a three-step framework: determine the correct HS code, establish the customs value, and apply the duty rate. The formula is straightforward: Duty = Customs Value × Duty Rate. However, the details within each step have evolved significantly this year.

First, the Harmonized System (HS) code determines the product classification. As of January 2026, the World Customs Organization implemented 46 amendments to HS 2022, affecting categories like electronics, textiles, and environmental goods. For example, lithium-ion batteries now fall under a new subheading (8507.60), which carries a lower duty rate of 2.5% in the EU compared to the previous 4.7% under the old classification. Using an outdated code can cost you thousands.

Second, customs value is not simply the invoice price. Under the WTO Valuation Agreement, it includes the cost of goods, insurance, and freight (CIF) to the port of entry. In 2026, customs authorities in the US, EU, and China are increasingly auditing related-party transactions and royalties. If you import from a subsidiary, you must prove that the transfer price reflects an arm's length transaction or face a 10–20% penalty on underpaid duties.

Key 2026 Updates Affecting Your Duty Calculation

Three major changes took effect in early 2026 that directly impact how you calculate import duties:

  • Revised De Minimis Thresholds: The US lowered its de minimis threshold from $800 to $200 for shipments from China, effective March 2026. This means more shipments now attract formal entry and duty calculation, not just informal clearance.
  • Environmental Tariffs on Steel and Aluminum: The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) now applies to steel, aluminum, and hydrogen imports. You must report embedded emissions and pay a duty surcharge of €30–€60 per ton of CO2 equivalent, calculated separately from standard tariffs.
  • Digital Services Levy Import Impact: The UK and India now require importers of digital goods (software, e-books, streaming devices) to include a 2% digital services levy in the customs value, effectively raising the duty base.

These updates mean that a simple product like a steel bracket from China now requires three separate calculations: standard duty (25% under Section 301), CBAM surcharge (€45/ton), and potential anti-dumping duties (18.4% for certain Chinese steel products). Ignoring any one of these can lead to customs holds or fines.

Step-by-Step: Calculate Your Import Duty in 2026

Here is the precise process you should follow for every shipment:

  1. Identify the correct 10-digit HS code. Use the latest 2026 national tariff schedule. For example, a wooden table from Brazil to the US uses HS 9403.30.80.00. The base rate is free under the US-Brazil FTA, but you must certify origin.
  2. Calculate customs value. Add the FOB price ($5,000), ocean freight ($1,200), and insurance ($300) to get a CIF value of $6,500. Do not subtract discounts or commissions unless they are bona fide and documented.
  3. Apply the duty rate. If the MFN rate is 3.9% (as for wooden furniture in the US), the duty is $6,500 × 0.039 = $253.50. Then add any trade remedies (e.g., Section 301 duties of 25% on Chinese-origin goods, which would add $1,625).
  4. Factor in VAT or GST. In the EU, VAT (typically 20%) is applied on the CIF value plus duty. So: ($6,500 + $253.50) × 0.20 = $1,350.70 VAT.
  5. Total landed cost: $6,500 (CIF) + $253.50 (duty) + $1,625 (Section 301) + $1,350.70 (VAT) = $9,729.20. Without the Section 301 duty, it would be $8,104.20 — a 20% difference.

Always double-check for preferential rates under FTAs. For example, under the USMCA, a wooden table from Mexico may qualify for a 0% duty if it meets the regional value content (RVC) of 60%.

Common Pitfalls in Duty Calculation (and How to Avoid Them)

Even experienced importers make mistakes. Here are the most frequent errors in 2026:

  • Using the wrong valuation method. Some importers mistakenly use FOB instead of CIF. This understates the duty base, leading to penalties. In 2026, customs audits in the US found that 23% of valuation errors involved freight omissions.
  • Ignoring anti-dumping and countervailing duties. These are product-specific and change frequently. For instance, in April 2026, the US Commerce Department increased anti-dumping duties on Chinese aluminum extrusions from 23.4% to 31.8%. Check the Federal Register monthly.
  • Overlooking post-importation adjustments. If you receive a price reduction after import, you must file a duty refund claim within one year. In 2026, the US CBP streamlined this process via the Automated Commercial Environment (ACE), but only 12% of eligible importers took advantage.
  • Not calculating duties for samples and low-value shipments. With the new de minimis rules, even shipments under $200 from certain countries require formal entry. Use a tariff calculator that updates thresholds in real time.
