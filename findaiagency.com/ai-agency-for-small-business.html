How to Choose an AI Agency for Small Business

Most companies between 5 and 50 employees do not need an AI strategy. They need two or three manual workflows to stop eating staff time, and someone accountable for keeping them running. This guide covers what these engagements actually include, what they cost, the questions that expose a reseller, and what a first 90 days should deliver.

What an AI agency actually does for a 5-50 person business

At this company size, work clusters into six buckets. If an agency's proposal does not map to one of them, ask what you are buying.

  • Discovery and automation audit (2-4 weeks). Someone sits with your team and watches how quotes get built, invoices get matched, and inbound email gets triaged. The output is a ranked backlog with an estimated hours-saved figure and a build cost per item. It is a document, not software.
  • Workflow automation. Intake forms qualified automatically, purchase-order PDFs extracted into your accounting system, CRM records enriched and de-duplicated, reminders sent, support tickets tagged and drafted. The stack is usually n8n, Make, or Zapier for orchestration, a document model for extraction, and Postgres or Airtable for state.
  • Custom internal assistants. A retrieval-augmented chat over your SOPs, contracts, or past project files, with citation links back to the source so staff verify rather than trust.
  • Data plumbing. Connecting Jobber or ServiceTitan to QuickBooks, HubSpot to a warehouse, Shopify to fulfilment. Unglamorous, and the reason most pilots die in month two.
  • Managed operations. A retainer to monitor runs, repair broken API calls, update prompts when a model changes, and ship one new workflow per quarter.
  • Fractional AI leadership. Ten to twenty hours a month from someone who scopes work and manages other vendors rather than building.

What it is not: headcount replacement, a chatbot bolted onto your homepage, or an 80-page strategy deck.

Five things move the quote: the number of systems touched (every new API has its own authentication, rate limits, and messy data); whether the automation runs straight through or needs an approval queue; fixed fee versus time and materials, where fixed fee protects you and usually carries a 15-25% premium; who pays for model usage, since token and API costs are typically passed through; and maintenance, which you should budget at 15-25% of build cost per year. Contracts offering unlimited changes have already priced that in.

Hourly rates run roughly $125-$250 for senior AI or automation engineers and $75-$150 for implementation work. Rates under about $60 an hour usually mean offshore subcontracting with a project manager layered on top — acceptable if it is disclosed up front.

The questions that separate a real agency from a reseller

Resellers sell access to someone else's platform and white-label the delivery. That is fine for commodity work and a poor fit when the workflow touches billing or customer data. These questions surface the difference in ten minutes.

  • “Walk me through the last three projects you shipped, and what broke.” Ask which systems were involved and request a reference call with a client of similar size. Vague answers, or an NDA invoked for everything, is a tell.
  • “Who writes the code, and are they on your payroll?” Then ask which of those people will actually be in your Slack channel.
  • “How do you measure whether the model is right?” Real builders talk about eval sets, accuracy thresholds, and what happens when confidence is low. Resellers talk about the model's reputation.
  • “Which APIs does this touch, and how do you handle rate limits, retries, and failed webhooks?” Generic answers mean the integration has never been built.
  • “Who owns the workflows, prompts, code, and credentials when we stop working together?” You should receive the repository, environment configuration, documentation, and API keys in your own accounts.
  • “What happens after launch, and what is the response time?” Ask for the support window and the hourly rate for changes outside scope.
  • “Will you run a paid two-week discovery with a fixed deliverable?” Agencies that scope before quoting will say yes. Resellers steer you toward a platform contract instead.

Red flags: when to walk away

  • Guaranteed headcount reductions or savings figures quoted before anyone has looked at your workflows.
  • No named technical lead — every call is a salesperson and architecture questions get deferred.
  • Refusal to provide a client reference or to run a paid pilot.
  • Demos made entirely of dashboards, with no production system to inspect.
  • Admin credentials to every system requested on day one, with no data processing agreement, sub-processor list, or security review.
  • Fees tied to a percentage of your savings, which cannot be audited and reward overstatement.
  • A closed platform where workflows cannot be exported, so leaving means rebuilding from scratch.
  • A 12-month auto-renewing contract signed before a pilot has proven anything.
  • No mention of error handling, human review, or what happens when the output is wrong.

What the first 90 days should deliver

The first 90 days are a proving ground, not a transformation. A well-run engagement looks like this.

  • Days 1-14: access and baseline. Read access to the relevant systems, two or three staff shadowed while doing the target work, and current numbers recorded — hours per task, error rate, cycle time. Deliverable: process map, ranked backlog, one agreed success metric per workflow.
  • Days 15-45: build one workflow end to end. In production, behind a human review step, running on real volume rather than a demo environment with dummy data. Deliverable: a working automation with logging and a defined exception path.
  • Days 46-75: measure and tune. Compare against the baseline, fix the failure modes, and only start workflow two if workflow one hit its metric. Deliverable: ROI figures, a runbook, and two trained internal owners.
  • Days 76-90: handover and decide. Access transferred, documentation complete, credentials sitting in your vault. Then choose a retainer, another project, or nothing at all.

Structure payment the same way: a 20-30% deposit, milestone payments, and 10-20% held until acceptance testing passes. Insist on a 30-day post-launch warranty covering defects. An agency that wants the full fee up front is the red flag from the previous section wearing a contract.

The short version: pay for discovery first, insist the first workflow runs in production on real volume, hold back part of the fee until it does, and make sure you own the code, prompts, and credentials before you sign anything.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to hire an AI agency for a small business?

Most 5-50 person companies spend $3,000-$12,000 on a discovery audit, $8,000-$25,000 on a first production workflow, and $1,500-$8,000 per month if they keep the agency on retainer. A three-to-five workflow program typically lands between $25,000 and $75,000. Treat anything far outside those bands as a signal to ask why.

How long does an AI automation project take?

A single workflow usually goes live in 4 to 8 weeks: about two weeks of discovery, two to four weeks of build, then a week or two of tuning against real volume. Multi-workflow programs run two to five months. If an agency promises a production system in under two weeks, ask what is actually being deployed and to whom.

Do we need clean data before contacting an agency?

No, but you need to know how messy it is. A good agency audits duplicates, missing fields, and inconsistent formats during discovery, then either includes cleanup in the scope or tells you to fix it first. Agencies that assume your CRM is already clean are the ones whose automations break in month two.

What is the difference between an AI agency and a freelance AI developer?

A freelancer is usually one builder: cheaper per hour, faster to start, and a single point of failure. An agency brings a delivery lead, one or more engineers, and a QA process, which matters when the workflow touches billing, customer data, or payroll. For one contained workflow a freelancer is often the better buy; for anything customer-facing or multi-system, use an agency.

Can a 10-person company justify hiring an AI agency?

Yes, if one recurring task costs more than roughly 20 staff hours a month. At that size, a single well-chosen automation such as quote generation, invoice matching, or email triage often repays the build cost within four to nine months. Below that threshold, start with off-the-shelf tools before paying for custom work.
