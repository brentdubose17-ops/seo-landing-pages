Is Semaglutide Legal? A Plain-Language Guide

Semaglutide is legal in the United States as a prescription drug — and essentially nothing else. The confusion comes from three separate questions being asked at once: whether the molecule is lawful, whether a particular pharmacy's version of it is lawful, and whether the person prescribing it is allowed to do so where the patient lives.

The short answer: legal by prescription, not by default

Semaglutide sits in the ordinary prescription-drug category, not in the controlled-substance category. That single fact answers a lot of the questions people actually have.

FDA has approved three branded semaglutide products, each with its own approved uses on the label: Ozempic (injectable, approved in 2017), Rybelsus (oral tablets, approved in 2019), and Wegovy (injectable, approved in 2021). All three are prescription-only. That means a licensed practitioner with prescribing authority can lawfully prescribe them, and a patient can lawfully possess them with a valid prescription.

Semaglutide is not listed on any of the Controlled Substances Act schedules. There is no DEA schedule number for it, no refill restriction tied to a schedule, and no federal prescription monitoring requirement of the kind that applies to opioids and certain stimulants. "Not scheduled" is not the same as "available without a prescription," though, and this is where a large share of online misinformation fails. The prescription requirement comes from the drug's approved labeling and from the federal framework that governs prescription drugs generally, not from the controlled-substance schedules.

Federal law also does not bar a clinician from prescribing an approved drug for an unapproved, off-label use. Off-label prescribing is a standard part of medical practice and is regulated by state medical boards rather than by FDA. That is a meaningful distinction for weight-management and metabolic clinics, because a large share of real-world semaglutide prescribing falls outside the exact approved indication.

Branded vs. compounded semaglutide: two different legal tracks

Branded and compounded semaglutide are governed by separate rules, enforced by separate mechanisms, and they are not interchangeable in a legal sense even when the active ingredient is the same molecule.

Pathway What it is General legal posture
FDA-approved branded product Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus, manufactured by the innovator and distributed through the normal supply chain Lawful when prescribed by an authorized practitioner and dispensed on a valid prescription
503A state-licensed compounding pharmacy Patient-specific compounded preparations made under state pharmacy board licensure Permitted only with a valid patient-specific prescription and compliant ingredient sourcing; FDA's position after the shortage was declared resolved is that compounding copies of the approved product is not permitted
503B outsourcing facility Larger-scale compounding under FDA registration and cGMP-like standards Cannot compound a drug that is essentially a copy of a commercially available product unless it appears on FDA's shortage list; semaglutide was removed from that list in 2025
"Research use only" online product Vials sold with disclaimers such as "not for human consumption" Not a lawful medicine. A label disclaimer does not convert an unapproved drug into a legal one when it is sold for human use

The compounding question has moved the most. FDA declared a shortage of semaglutide injection in 2022, which opened a window in which compounders could prepare the drug. FDA declared that shortage resolved in February 2025 and set wind-down enforcement dates in the spring of 2025. Compounding trade groups challenged the delisting in federal court, and those dates shifted more than once as the litigation progressed. By 2026 the practical position is that large-volume compounding of semaglutide as a cheaper substitute for the branded product is outside the standard legal pathways, while genuinely patient-specific compounding remains a narrow category.

FDA has also flagged several GLP-1 active ingredients as presenting demonstrable difficulties for compounding, which adds a further layer of restriction for outsourcing facilities. That designation is separate from shortage status and does not disappear when a shortage ends.

What telehealth prescribing rules actually permit

Semaglutide is not a controlled substance, so the Ryan Haight Act — the federal law that generally requires an in-person examination before a controlled substance is prescribed via telemedicine — does not apply to it. That removes the single biggest federal obstacle people expect to find.

What remains is state law. Non-controlled prescription drugs dispensed through telehealth are governed primarily by the state where the patient is physically located at the time of the encounter. Four things generally have to line up:

  • Licensure. The prescriber must hold a license in the state where the patient is located. A clinician licensed in one state is not automatically authorized to prescribe for patients sitting in another.
  • Modality. Some states accept asynchronous or questionnaire-based intake for non-controlled drugs; others require a synchronous audio-video encounter or a documented history and physical before prescribing.
  • Standard of care. The prescriber must meet the same standard as an in-person practitioner, including appropriate screening, documented informed consent, and follow-up.
  • Advertising and platform conduct. Marketing that promises a prescription outcome, or that presents a clinical decision as guaranteed, draws scrutiny from both state boards and the FTC.

A useful mental model: telehealth did not create a new legal category of semaglutide. It changed the channel through which a normal prescription can be issued. If the underlying prescription would not be lawful in person in that state, running it through a video call does not make it lawful.

How state law changes the answer

Federal law sets the floor; states set the ceiling. Two clinics operating under identical federal rules can face different legal answers because of where their patients sit.

State-level variables that commonly change the outcome include the state's telemedicine statute and whether it recognizes an established patient relationship formed remotely; the state's medical and pharmacy board rules on prescribing for weight loss specifically; whether the state requires an in-person examination for certain categories of prescriptions; state-specific informed-consent requirements; and state pharmacy rules on accepting out-of-state prescriptions for compounded preparations. A handful of states have layered additional requirements onto weight-management prescribing, and a handful have taken enforcement action against specific telehealth operators rather than against the drug itself.

State boards of pharmacy also police compounding directly. A 503A pharmacy must satisfy the board in the state where it is licensed, and a prescriber sending prescriptions into another state can trigger scrutiny in both jurisdictions. For multi-state operators, the compliance question is rarely "is semaglutide legal" — it is "is this prescription, from this prescriber, to this patient, filled by this pharmacy, lawful in these two states at once."

What is clearly not legal

Some areas are genuinely settled. Each of the following is outside the lawful pathways for human use:

  • Selling semaglutide for human use without a prescription. Prescription-only status is not a formality.
  • Importing semaglutide from abroad for personal use. FDA's personal importation policy is a statement of enforcement discretion — a decision about when the agency will pursue a case — not a legal authorization to bring an unapproved drug into the country. The Prescription Drug Marketing Act separately restricts reimportation.
  • Marketing a "research chemical" version for human consumption. Labeling a vial "not for human consumption" or "for research use only" does not legalize a drug when the actual distribution channel is people using it as medicine.
  • Advertising a compounded product as equivalent to a branded one. Claims that a compounded preparation is "the same as" or "bioequivalent to" an approved product raise both FDA misbranding issues and FTC deceptive-advertising issues.
  • Prescribing without state licensure. Practicing across state lines without authorization is a state-law violation regardless of the drug involved.
  • Manufacturing without registration. Producing drug products at scale without the appropriate FDA registration or pharmacy licensure is not a gray area.
  • Distributing counterfeit or mislabeled product. This is straightforwardly illegal and has been the subject of repeated FDA and Customs enforcement actions involving GLP-1 products.

A working compliance checklist for clinics and researchers

For operators who need to reduce risk rather than debate it, the following checks map to the areas where enforcement actually happens.

  • Confirm the prescriber holds an active license in every state where patients are located, and that the platform tracks patient location at the time of each encounter.
  • Document the modality used for each encounter against that state's telemedicine requirements, including any synchronous-visit rule.
  • Verify whether the supplying pharmacy is a licensed 503A pharmacy or an FDA-registered 503B facility, and keep that distinction in your records.
  • Confirm the pharmacy's own position on compounding semaglutide following the February 2025 shortage resolution, and get it in writing.
  • Check whether the active ingredient source is an FDA-registered facility, and retain the documentation.
  • Review all marketing copy for implied equivalence to branded products and for guarantees of a prescription outcome.
  • Keep the patient-specific prescription for every compounded preparation; batch or bulk distribution for individual patients does not fit the 503A model.
  • Track the litigation calendar — enforcement dates in the semaglutide compounding dispute have moved more than once, and any compliance policy keyed to a fixed date will go stale.
  • For research use, keep acquisition and use records clearly separated from any clinical or patient-facing activity.

Frequently asked questions

Is semaglutide legal in the United States?

Yes, but only as a prescription drug. Branded semaglutide products such as Ozempic, Wegovy, and Rybelsus are FDA-approved and legal when a licensed clinician prescribes them to a patient. Buying semaglutide without a prescription, importing it from abroad, or selling it as a research chemical for human use is not legal.

Is compounded semaglutide legal?

It depends on shortage status and the pharmacy's pathway. While semaglutide was on FDA's shortage list, 503A and 503B compounders could prepare it under specific conditions. After FDA declared the shortage resolved in February 2025, the agency's position is that compounding a copy of an approved semaglutide product is no longer permitted under the standard pathways, subject to litigation and shifting enforcement dates.

Can a telehealth provider legally prescribe semaglutide?

Often yes, because semaglutide is not a federally controlled substance, so the Ryan Haight Act's in-person requirements do not apply. The clinician generally must be licensed in the state where the patient is located and must follow that state's telemedicine and prescribing rules, which vary widely.

Is it legal to buy semaglutide online without a prescription?

No. A prescription is required for human use, and FDA's personal importation policy is a statement of enforcement discretion rather than a lawful route to obtain the drug. Products sold online as research chemicals or not for human consumption are not legal to use as medicine.

Does state law change whether semaglutide is legal?

Yes. Licensing, telemedicine, informed consent, and prescribing standards are set at the state level, and some states impose stricter requirements than federal law, including in-person examination rules for certain prescriptions.
